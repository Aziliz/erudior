## After the doctor's office

In lesson 20 Tom left Dr. Lehmann's practice with *ein Rezept* for cough syrup and the instruction *Nehmen Sie ihn dreimal täglich nach dem Essen.* Then what? He goes to the *Apotheke*, and the medicine comes in a box with a folded sheet of paper inside: *die Packungsbeilage*, in everyday speech *der Beipackzettel*. It's one of the most important texts you'll ever read in German, and one of the hardest, because it's written in a dense, formal style.

This honors lesson explains how medicines are sold in Germany, how a package insert is built, and the two grammar patterns that fill it: the **passive** (*Die Tabletten werden … eingenommen*) and long **compound nouns** (*die Packungsbeilage, das Schmerzmittel*). Everything here is about understanding; you don't need to write a package insert.

One rule comes first: this lesson teaches language, not medicine. The medicine in it, *Musterin*, is made up. For a real medicine, follow your own package insert and ask the doctor or the pharmacist (*der Apotheker, die Apothekerin*).

## Three kinds of medicine

German law sorts medicines (*die Arzneimittel*, the official word; *das Medikament* from A1 is the everyday one) into three groups:

| Group | Where you get it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *verschreibungspflichtig* (prescription only) | at the pharmacy, with a prescription | antibiotics, most heart medicines |
| *apothekenpflichtig* (pharmacy only) | at the pharmacy, no prescription needed | many painkillers, cough syrups |
| *freiverkäuflich* (on general sale) | also in supermarkets and drugstores | some herbal teas and other mild remedies |

Medicines from the last two groups are *rezeptfrei*: you don't need a prescription. Notice that in Germany even a simple painkiller (*das Schmerzmittel*) is often sold only in a pharmacy, not next to the shampoo; the pharmacist can advise you.

The doctor *verschreibt* a medicine (*verschreiben* = to prescribe; *hat verschrieben*). Since 2024, prescriptions for people with statutory health insurance (*Krankenkasse*, lesson 20) are electronic: the *E-Rezept*. You redeem it at any pharmacy with your *Versichertenkarte*, with an app, or with a printed code. For prescription medicines, adults with statutory insurance usually pay a share (*die Zuzahlung*) of 10 percent of the price, at least 5 and at most 10 euros per pack (never more than the medicine costs); children don't pay it.

At night and on Sundays, one pharmacy in each area stays open by rota: *die Notdienst-Apotheke*. Every pharmacy door shows which one is on duty.

### At the pharmacy

> Apothekerin: Guten Tag, was kann ich für Sie tun?
>
> Tom: Ich habe hier ein Rezept für einen Hustensaft. Und haben Sie auch etwas gegen Halsschmerzen?
>
> Apothekerin: Ja, diese Lutschtabletten sind rezeptfrei. Nehmen Sie alle drei Stunden eine, höchstens sechs am Tag. Nehmen Sie noch andere Medikamente?
>
> Tom: Nein, nur den Hustensaft.
>
> Apothekerin: Gut. Die Zuzahlung für den Saft beträgt fünf Euro, die Lutschtabletten kosten 7,95 Euro. Die genaue Dosierung steht in der Packungsbeilage.
>
> Tom: Vielen Dank.

In English:

> Pharmacist: Hello, what can I do for you?
>
> Tom: I have a prescription here for cough syrup. And do you also have something for a sore throat?
>
> Pharmacist: Yes, these lozenges don't need a prescription. Take one every three hours, at most six a day. Are you taking any other medicines?
>
> Tom: No, only the cough syrup.
>
> Pharmacist: Good. The prescription charge for the syrup is five euros, the lozenges cost 7.95 euros. The exact dosage is in the package insert.
>
> Tom: Thank you very much.

*die Lutschtablette* = lozenge (*lutschen* = to suck); *höchstens* = at most; *die Dosierung* = dosage. *Nehmen Sie noch andere Medikamente?* is a question every pharmacist asks, because medicines can affect each other.

## How a package insert is built

Every package insert in the European Union follows the same template, so once you know it in German you can find your way in any of them:

![The first page of a package insert for the made-up medicine Musterin 200 mg: a title, a box telling you to read the whole leaflet before taking the medicine and to keep it, the six numbered section headings, and the start of section 3 with the dosage](images/de2-m5-insert.svg)

| Section heading | What you find there |
|---|---|
| 1. *Was ist … und wofür wird es angewendet?* | what the medicine is and what it's for |
| 2. *Was sollten Sie vor der Einnahme … beachten?* | when not to take it, warnings, pregnancy, other medicines |
| 3. *Wie ist … einzunehmen?* | how much, how often, how long |
| 4. *Welche Nebenwirkungen sind möglich?* | side effects |
| 5. *Wie ist … aufzubewahren?* | how to store it, expiry date |
| 6. *Inhalt der Packung und weitere Informationen* | the ingredients, the maker |

Key words: *der Wirkstoff* (active ingredient), *die Anwendung* (use), *anwenden* (to use, apply: *angewendet*), *die Einnahme* (taking it), *einnehmen* (to take a medicine; separable: *Nehmen Sie die Tablette ein*), *wirken* (to work, have an effect: *Das Mittel wirkt schnell*), *die Schwangerschaft* (pregnancy; *schwanger* is from lesson 20). Medicines come as *Tabletten*, *Kapseln* (*die Kapsel*), *Tropfen* (*der Tropfen*, drops), *Saft* (syrup) or *Salbe* (*die Salbe*, ointment). *schlucken* means to swallow.

Headings 3 and 5 use *ist … einzunehmen*, *ist … aufzubewahren*: "how is it to be taken, how is it to be stored". *sein* + *zu* + infinitive expresses what must or can be done, and you'll meet it often in official letters (next honors lesson). Here simply read *Wie ist Musterin einzunehmen?* as "How should I take Musterin?"

### Side effects and what "rare" means

Section 4 lists side effects (*die Nebenwirkungen*) by how often they occur, and the words have exact meanings:

| Word | Meaning in a package insert |
|---|---|
| *sehr häufig* | more than 1 in 10 people treated |
| *häufig* | 1 to 10 in 100 |
| *gelegentlich* | 1 to 10 in 1,000 |
| *selten* | 1 to 10 in 10,000 |
| *sehr selten* | fewer than 1 in 10,000 |

So *häufig* (frequent) here means "up to one person in ten", which is less than many readers think, and *selten* (rare) means at most one in a thousand. Writing numbers, German uses a point where English uses a comma: *10.000* is ten thousand.

## The passive: who does it doesn't matter

Section 3 of the Musterin leaflet says:

*Die Tabletten werden unzerkaut mit einem Glas Wasser nach dem Essen eingenommen.* — The tablets are taken whole (unchewed) with a glass of water after meals.

The sentence doesn't say who takes the tablets; that's the point. This is the passive: **werden** (in its normal present forms) + the past participle at the end.

| Active (who does it is named) | Passive (the action is what counts) |
|---|---|
| *Man nimmt die Tabletten nach dem Essen ein.* | *Die Tabletten werden nach dem Essen eingenommen.* |
| *Der Arzt verschreibt das Mittel.* | *Das Mittel wird (vom Arzt) verschrieben.* |
| *Man bewahrt die Salbe im Kühlschrank auf.* | *Die Salbe wird im Kühlschrank aufbewahrt.* |

How to recognize it:

- a form of *werden* in position 2 (*wird, werden*);
- a past participle at the end (*eingenommen, verschrieben, aufbewahrt*);
- the thing being acted on is the subject.

You already know *werden* in two other jobs: "to become" with an adjective or noun (*Er wird Arzt.*, lesson 8) and the future with an infinitive (*Ich werde anrufen.*, lesson 14). With a past participle it's the passive. At A2 you only need to understand it; German B1 teaches you to build it.

The passive is everywhere in instructions, rules and news: *Hier wird nicht geraucht.* (No smoking here.) *Die Praxis wird um 18 Uhr geschlossen.* (The practice closes at 6 p.m.)

### Example 1: reading the dosage

The Musterin leaflet says: *Erwachsene: 1 Tablette bis zu dreimal täglich. Nehmen Sie nicht mehr als 3 Tabletten pro Tag ein.* What's the most a man may take in two days?

1. *bis zu dreimal täglich* = up to three times a day, one tablet each time.
2. *nicht mehr als 3 Tabletten pro Tag* confirms the daily maximum: 3.
3. Two days: 2 × 3 = 6 tablets at most.

Then check the other conditions in the same section: *unzerkaut* (don't chew), *mit einem Glas Wasser*, *nach dem Essen*.

### Example 2: turning passive into active

Make these leaflet sentences into everyday active sentences with *man* or *Sie*:

1. *Die Kapseln werden morgens eingenommen.* → subject *die Kapseln*, participle *eingenommen* from *einnehmen*. Active: *Man nimmt die Kapseln morgens ein.* or *Nehmen Sie die Kapseln morgens ein.*
2. *Die Salbe wird zweimal täglich dünn aufgetragen.* (*auftragen* = to apply, spread) → *Man trägt die Salbe zweimal täglich dünn auf.*
3. *Das Arzneimittel wird bei Kindern unter 12 Jahren nicht angewendet.* → *Man wendet das Arzneimittel bei Kindern unter 12 Jahren nicht an.* (*bei* + dative here means "in the case of".)

## Compound nouns: read them from the back

A compound noun joins two or more nouns into one word, and the package insert is full of them. Two rules unlock nearly all of them:

- The **last** part says what the thing is, and gives the gender: *die Packung* + *die Beilage* = *die Packungsbeilage*, a kind of *Beilage* (enclosure). *der Schmerz* + *das Mittel* = *das Schmerzmittel*, a kind of *Mittel* (remedy).
- Read the parts from the back to the front: *der Hustensaft* = *Saft* for *Husten* (cough syrup); *die Lutschtablette* = a tablet for sucking.

Often a linking *-s-* or *-n-* sits between the parts: *die Packung-s-beilage, die Schwangerschaft-s-woche, der Schmerz-en-s-geld* (compensation for pain). You can't always predict it, but you can always see it.

### Example 3: a long word step by step

*die Arbeitsunfähigkeitsbescheinigung* is the doctor's note that says you can't work (lesson 20: *krankschreiben*). Take it apart from the back:

1. *die Bescheinigung* — certificate (lesson 19). So the word is feminine and is a certificate.
2. *Unfähigkeit* — inability (*un-* + *fähig*, able, + *-keit*).
3. *Arbeit-s-* — work, with a linking *s*.

So: a certificate of inability to work. People shorten it to *die AU* or *die Krankschreibung*, and since 2023 doctors send it electronically to the *Krankenkasse*; employers then call it up there. In Germany, if you're ill for more than three calendar days, you need one by the next working day, and your employer may ask for it from the first day.

## Common mistakes

- Reading *werden* + participle as the future. *Die Tabletten werden eingenommen* is "are taken", not "will take". Future has an infinitive: *Ich werde die Tabletten nehmen.*
- *Ich nehme die Tablette.* is fine in speech, but in instructions the verb is separable *einnehmen*: *Nehmen Sie die Tablette nach dem Essen ein.* Don't forget the *ein* at the end.
- Thinking *häufig* means "most people". In a package insert it means up to one in ten.
- Getting the gender of a compound from the first part: *der Schmerz* but *das Schmerzmittel*.
- Asking at the supermarket for *Kopfschmerztabletten*: in Germany most painkillers are only at the *Apotheke*.
- Treating an online translation of a package insert as enough. If anything is unclear, ask the pharmacist: *Können Sie mir sagen, wie ich das einnehmen muss?*

## Key points

- Medicines are *verschreibungspflichtig*, *apothekenpflichtig* or *freiverkäuflich*; the last two are *rezeptfrei*. Prescriptions are now usually *E-Rezepte*; adults pay a *Zuzahlung* of 5 to 10 euros.
- Every package insert has the same six sections: what it's for, warnings, dosage, side effects, storage, contents.
- *häufig* = up to 1 in 10, *gelegentlich* = up to 1 in 100, *selten* = up to 1 in 1,000.
- The passive is *werden* + past participle: *Die Tabletten werden nach dem Essen eingenommen.* The action matters, not who does it. Recognize it; you'll build it in B1.
- Compound nouns: the last part gives the meaning and the gender; read from the back. *die Packungsbeilage, das Schmerzmittel, der Hustensaft.*
