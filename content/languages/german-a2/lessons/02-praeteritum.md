## What this lesson is for

In lesson 1 you told your life story in the perfect. But some verbs sound clumsy in the perfect, and Germans avoid it with them. You already know two: nobody says *Ich bin müde gewesen*; they say *Ich war müde*. At A1 you learned *war* and *hatte*, the **simple past** of *sein* and *haben*.

This lesson adds the rest of the simple past you need at A2. You'll learn to *use* it for the modal verbs, because *konnte, musste, durfte* are what Germans actually say when they talk about what they could, had to or were allowed to do as children. You'll learn to *recognise* the simple past of other common verbs, because you'll meet them in every story, article and fairy tale you read. And you'll find out when Germans choose which past tense.

The theme is childhood and school days: what was allowed, what was forbidden, who was strict and what you remember.

## Two past tenses, one meaning

German has two everyday past tenses, the perfect (*ich habe gespielt*) and the simple past, also called the **Präteritum** (*ich spielte*). The important thing for an English speaker: they mean the same. *Ich habe gespielt* and *ich spielte* both translate as "I played", "I have played" or "I was playing". The difference is style, not time.

| | perfect | simple past |
|---|---|---|
| where | conversation, messages, e-mails to friends | books, newspapers, stories, reports, fairy tales |
| which verbs | almost all verbs | *sein, haben, werden* and the modals, in speech too |
| example | *Gestern habe ich Oma besucht.* | *Der König hatte drei Töchter.* |

So in speech you mix the two: the perfect for most verbs, the simple past for *sein, haben* and the modals. *Gestern war ich bei Oma. Wir haben Kuchen gegessen, und ich musste ihr den Computer erklären.* That one sentence uses both, and it's completely normal.

In northern Germany people use the simple past a little more in speech than in the south, Austria and Switzerland, where the perfect is almost everything. You won't go wrong with the rule in the table.

## sein, haben and werden

You know *war* and *hatte*. *werden* (to become) has the simple past *wurde*. All three use the same endings, which are also the endings of every simple past form: nothing for *ich* and *er/sie/es*, *-st* for *du*, *-t* for *ihr* and the plural ending *-en* (or *-n*).

| | sein | haben | werden |
|---|---|---|---|
| ich | war | hatte | wurde |
| du | warst | hattest | wurdest |
| er / sie / es | war | hatte | wurde |
| wir | waren | hatten | wurden |
| ihr | wart | hattet | wurdet |
| sie / Sie | waren | hatten | wurden |

- *Mit zwölf wurde ich sehr frech.* — At twelve I became very cheeky.
- *Meine Lehrerin war streng, aber fair.* — My teacher was strict but fair.
- *Wir hatten keinen Fernseher.* — We didn't have a TV.

In lesson 1 you said *Ich bin 1990 geboren.* With *wurde* you can say *Ich wurde 1990 geboren*, which sounds more formal, like a CV. For people who have died, Germans normally use *wurde*: *Mozart wurde 1756 in Salzburg geboren.*

## The modal verbs in the simple past

Modal verbs are rarely used in the perfect. To say what you could, wanted, had to or were allowed to do, use their simple past. The rule is easy: take the infinitive, drop the umlaut if there is one, and add *-te*.

| infinitive | drop umlaut, add -te | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| können | konnte | could, was able to |
| müssen | musste | had to |
| dürfen | durfte | was allowed to |
| wollen | wollte | wanted (to) |
| sollen | sollte | was supposed to |
| mögen | mochte | liked |

*mögen* is the only odd one: the *g* becomes *ch*, *mochte*. And *möchten* has no past of its own: for "wanted to" use *wollte*. *Ich möchte ein Eis* becomes *Ich wollte ein Eis*.

The endings are the same as for *war* and *wurde*:

| | können | müssen | dürfen | wollen | sollen | mögen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ich | konnte | musste | durfte | wollte | sollte | mochte |
| du | konntest | musstest | durftest | wolltest | solltest | mochtest |
| er / sie / es | konnte | musste | durfte | wollte | sollte | mochte |
| wir | konnten | mussten | durften | wollten | sollten | mochten |
| ihr | konntet | musstet | durftet | wolltet | solltet | mochtet |
| sie / Sie | konnten | mussten | durften | wollten | sollten | mochten |

The word order is the same as in the present: the modal in position 2, the infinitive at the end.

- *Mit sechs konnte ich schon lesen.* — At six I could already read.
- *Wir mussten jeden Tag um sieben aufstehen.* — We had to get up at seven every day.
- *Ich durfte abends nicht fernsehen.* — I wasn't allowed to watch TV in the evenings.
- *Meine Schwester wollte immer Ärztin werden.* — My sister always wanted to become a doctor.
- *Du solltest doch um sechs zu Hause sein!* — You were supposed to be home at six!
- *Als Kind mochte ich keinen Spinat.* — As a child I didn't like spinach.

*Als Kind* means "as a child", a useful chunk; the word *als* gets its own section in lesson 4.

Watch *sollte*: it can also mean "should" in the present (*Du solltest mehr schlafen* — You should sleep more). The context tells you which; lesson 15 covers the "should" meaning properly.

## Recognising the simple past of other verbs

In speech you'll use the perfect for most verbs. But you'll *read* the simple past all the time, so you need to recognise it. There are three patterns, and they match the three participle types from lesson 1.

### Weak verbs: -te

Weak verbs add *-te* to the stem, just like the modals: *sagen → sagte*, *machen → machte*, *spielen → spielte*, *wohnen → wohnte*. Stems ending in *-t* or *-d* add *-ete*: *arbeiten → arbeitete*, *warten → wartete*. If you see *-te* in the middle of a verb, think "past".

### Strong verbs: a new vowel, no ending

Strong verbs change their vowel, and the *ich* and *er/sie/es* forms have no ending at all: *er ging* (he went), *sie kam* (she came). Here are the most common ones in stories:

| simple past | infinitive | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ging | gehen | went |
| kam | kommen | came |
| sah | sehen | saw |
| gab | geben | gave; (es gab) there was, there were |
| fand | finden | found |
| stand | stehen | stood |
| saß | sitzen | sat |
| lag | liegen | lay, was lying |
| las | lesen | read |
| schrieb | schreiben | wrote |
| fuhr | fahren | drove, went |
| lief | laufen | ran |
| blieb | bleiben | stayed |
| nahm | nehmen | took |
| sprach | sprechen | spoke |
| aß | essen | ate |

*es gab* is especially common: *Damals gab es kein Internet.* — Back then there was no internet.

### Mixed verbs: new vowel and -te

The mixed verbs from lesson 1 combine both: a new vowel and *-te*. *denken → dachte*, *bringen → brachte*, *kennen → kannte*, *wissen → wusste*, *nennen → nannte*, *rennen → rannte*.

- *Ich dachte, du kommst heute.* — I thought you were coming today.
- *Das wusste ich nicht.* — I didn't know that.

A dictionary lists the simple past next to the participle: *finden, fand, gefunden*.

## Fairy tales

German fairy tales, *die Märchen*, are told in the simple past and almost always start with *Es war einmal …* (Once upon a time there was …). They're a good way to practise recognising the forms:

*Es war einmal ein Mädchen, das wohnte mit seiner Mutter am Rand von einem großen Wald. Eines Tages sagte die Mutter: „Bring der Großmutter diesen Kuchen. Aber du darfst nicht vom Weg gehen!“ Das Mädchen nahm den Korb und ging los.* — Once upon a time there was a girl who lived with her mother at the edge of a big forest. One day the mother said: "Take this cake to Grandmother. But you mustn't leave the path!" The girl took the basket and set off.

You don't need to produce this style yet; you only need to understand it.

## Remembering

To say you remember something, use *sich erinnern an* + accusative. It's a reflexive verb, which you'll learn properly in lesson 5. For now, use these chunks:

- *Ich erinnere mich an meine erste Lehrerin.* — I remember my first teacher.
- *Erinnerst du dich an unseren Spielplatz?* — Do you remember our playground?
- *Ich kann mich nicht erinnern.* — I can't remember.

The noun is *die Erinnerung* (memory): *Ich habe schöne Erinnerungen an meine Kindheit.*

## School in Germany

Children usually go to *der Kindergarten* from age three, and to *die Grundschule* (primary school) at six. A school year is divided into *Klassen*: *Ich war in der dritten Klasse* (I was in third grade). *Die Klasse* also means the group of children. A school subject is *das Fach*, and at the end of each half-year children get a report, *das Zeugnis*, with *Noten* (grades) from 1 (sehr gut) to 6 (ungenügend). In Germany a 1 is the best grade, not the worst! Between lessons there's *die Pause*, and children play outside on *der Schulhof* (the schoolyard).

## Allowed and forbidden

Childhood is full of rules. *erlauben* (to allow) is the verb behind *erlaubt*, which you know from A1, and *das Verbot* (ban, rule against something) is the noun behind *verboten*. Parents want their children to be *brav* (good, well-behaved), but children are often *frech* (cheeky).

- *Meine Eltern haben mir fast alles erlaubt.* — My parents allowed me almost everything.
- *Bei uns gab es ein Verbot: kein Fernsehen vor den Hausaufgaben.* — We had one rule: no TV before homework.
- *Unser Nachbar hat oft geschimpft, denn unser Ball lag immer in seinem Garten.* — Our neighbour often told us off, because our ball was always lying in his garden.
- *Pass auf deinen Bruder auf!* — Keep an eye on your brother! (*aufpassen auf* + accusative = to look after, to watch out for)

## Text: Opa erzählt

Lena's grandfather, born in 1952, tells her about his school days.

> Lena: Opa, wie war die Schule früher?
>
> Opa: Oh, ganz anders als heute. Wir waren fünfunddreißig Kinder in einer Klasse. Unser Lehrer, Herr Krämer, war sehr streng. Wir durften im Unterricht kein Wort sagen.
>
> Lena: Und wenn doch?
>
> Opa: Dann schimpfte er laut, und man musste in der Pause in der Klasse bleiben. Einmal war ich frech und habe eine Maus mitgebracht. Die hat sich unter seinem Tisch versteckt. Er hat sie nie gefunden!
>
> Lena: Was war dein Lieblingsfach?
>
> Opa: Sport. In Mathe hatte ich immer schlechte Noten. Mein Zeugnis wollte ich meinem Vater nie zeigen.
>
> Lena: Und nach der Schule?
>
> Opa: Wir hatten kein Spielzeug wie ihr heute, nur einen alten Ball und eine Puppe für meine Schwester. Wir konnten den ganzen Nachmittag draußen spielen und auf Bäume klettern. Unsere Nachbarin, Frau Lutz, passte auf uns auf. Wir sollten um sechs zu Hause sein, aber das hat selten geklappt.
>
> Lena: Hast du schöne Erinnerungen an diese Zeit?
>
> Opa: Ja, sehr schöne. Wir hatten nicht viel, aber wir waren frei.

In English:

> Lena: Grandpa, what was school like in the old days?
>
> Grandpa: Oh, very different from today. We were thirty-five children in one class. Our teacher, Mr Krämer, was very strict. We weren't allowed to say a word in lessons.
>
> Lena: And if you did?
>
> Grandpa: Then he scolded loudly, and you had to stay in the classroom during break. Once I was cheeky and brought a mouse along. It hid under his desk. He never found it!
>
> Lena: What was your favourite subject?
>
> Grandpa: PE. In maths I always had bad grades. I never wanted to show my report to my father.
>
> Lena: And after school?
>
> Grandpa: We didn't have toys like you do today, just an old ball and a doll for my sister. We could play outside all afternoon and climb trees. Our neighbour, Mrs Lutz, kept an eye on us. We were supposed to be home at six, but that rarely worked out.
>
> Lena: Do you have happy memories of that time?
>
> Grandpa: Yes, very happy ones. We didn't have much, but we were free.

Grandpa speaks naturally: *war, hatte, durften, musste, konnte, wollte, sollten* in the simple past, and *habe mitgebracht, hat gefunden, hat geklappt* in the perfect. *schimpfte* and *passte auf* are simple past too; he's telling an old story, so they slip in. (*Und wenn doch?* = And if you did?; *der Unterricht* = lessons, teaching; *der Lieblings-* = favourite; *der Baum, die Bäume* = tree; *selten* = rarely; *klappen* = to work out.)

## Pronunciation

- *durfte, musste, konnte*: short vowels, no umlaut. Don't say *dürfte* or *müsste*; those are different forms with a different meaning (lesson 15).
- *wurde*: a short *u*, like English "put". Don't confuse it with *würde* (would).
- *das Zeugnis*: ZEUG-nis, *eu* as in "boy", the *g* sounds like *k*.
- *das Märchen*: long *ä*, MÄR-chen.
- *die Grundschule*: GRUND-schu-le, the *d* sounds like *t*.

## Common mistakes

- *Ich habe nicht gekonnt schwimmen.* — Use the simple past of the modal: *Ich konnte nicht schwimmen.*
- *Ich möchtete …* — *möchten* has no simple past; use *wollte*: *Ich wollte ein Fahrrad.*
- *Er konntet …* / *Ich musstet …* — The ich and er forms have no ending after *-te*: *ich konnte, er musste*.
- *Ich durfe nicht …* — Don't forget the *t*: *durfte*.
- *Ich mögte …* — The past of *mögen* is *mochte*.
- *Ich bin müde gewesen.* isn't wrong, but it sounds stiff. With *sein* and *haben*, people say *Ich war müde*, *Ich hatte Zeit*.
- Reading *sie ging* as present: a strong verb with a new vowel and no ending is past. *Sie geht* is present, *sie ging* is past.

## Key points

- Perfect and simple past mean the same; the perfect is for speaking, the simple past for writing and stories.
- In speech too, use the simple past for *sein* (*war*), *haben* (*hatte*), *werden* (*wurde*) and the modals.
- Modal past: no umlaut + *-te*: *konnte, musste, durfte, wollte, sollte, mochte*. *möchten* → *wollte*.
- Endings: ich –, du -st, er –, wir -en, ihr -t, sie -en. The ich and er forms are the same.
- Recognise strong pasts by the new vowel (*ging, kam, sah, gab, fand*) and weak pasts by *-te* (*sagte, machte*).
- *Es war einmal …* starts a fairy tale; *es gab* = there was / there were.
