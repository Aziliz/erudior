## What this lesson is for

In lesson 16 you learned the weather words (*sonnig, bewölkt, das Gewitter, der Nebel*) and read a forecast for the north, the south and the Alps. In lesson 14 you met the future with *werden* for predictions: *Morgen wird es regnen.* Germans talk about the weather a lot, at the bakery, with neighbours, at the start of almost every phone call, so it's worth going further.

This honors lesson gives you more weather verbs and how they work, three ways to talk about tomorrow's weather (and what each one says about how sure you are), the temperature scale Germans use and how to convert it, and the weather sayings and customs you'll meet in Germany, Austria and Switzerland. It ends with a longer radio forecast in German.

## The weather verbs: es does the work

Weather verbs have no real subject. Nobody "does" the raining, so German uses *es*, like English "it". A verb used with this empty *es* as its only possible subject is called an **impersonal verb**. You know *es regnet* and *es schneit* from A1. Here are more:

| Verb | Meaning | Noun |
|---|---|---|
| *es regnet* | it's raining | *der Regen* |
| *es nieselt* | it's drizzling | *der Nieselregen* (drizzle) |
| *es schneit* | it's snowing | *der Schnee* |
| *es hagelt* | it's hailing | *der Hagel* (hail) |
| *es donnert* | it's thundering | *der Donner* (thunder) |
| *es blitzt* | there's lightning | *der Blitz* (lightning, a flash) |
| *es friert* | it's freezing (below 0 °C) | *der Frost* (frost) |
| *es taut* | it's thawing | — |

Two rules make these verbs easy:

1. ***es* never disappears.** Unlike some other uses of *es*, the weather *es* stays when another word takes position 1: *Morgen regnet es.* *Gestern hat es gehagelt.* *Ich glaube, dass es heute Nacht friert.*
2. **They form the perfect with *haben*:** *Es hat die ganze Nacht geregnet.* *Hat es gestern geblitzt?*

Careful with *frieren*. With *es* it describes the weather: *Heute Nacht friert es.* With a person, it means to feel cold: *Ich friere* — I'm freezing. Another way to say that is *Mir ist kalt* (the person in the dative).

Besides the verbs, there are two other patterns: *es gibt* + accusative for what the weather brings (*Morgen gibt es Gewitter. Im Norden gibt es viel Wind.*) and *es ist* + adjective (*Es ist neblig. Es ist schwül* — it's humid and close).

### Adjectives from weather nouns

Most weather adjectives are made by adding *-ig* to the noun: *die Sonne → sonnig, der Wind → windig, der Nebel → neblig* (the *e* drops), *die Wolke → wolkig* (cloudy), *der Frost → frostig* (frosty). One important exception: *der Regen → regnerisch* (rainy), with *-isch* (likewise *der Sturm → stürmisch*, stormy). You'll hear *bewölkt* and *wolkig* for much the same thing; in forecasts, *wolkig* usually means partly cloudy, and *bedeckt* means completely overcast.

## Talking about tomorrow: three ways

German has three common ways to say what the weather will do, and they differ in how sure the speaker is.

| Form | Example | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| present + time word | *Morgen regnet es.* | a plain statement, like a fact |
| *werden* + infinitive | *Morgen wird es regnen.* | a prediction: I think so, I expect so (lesson 14) |
| *sollen* + infinitive | *Morgen soll es regnen.* | a report: the forecast says so, I'm passing it on |

The third one is new. *sollen* normally means "should, be supposed to". When you pass on what you've heard or read, it means "they say", "apparently": *Morgen soll es 30 Grad warm werden.* — It's supposed to get to 30 degrees tomorrow. You saw it in lesson 16: *Morgen soll es in München Gewitter geben.* Use it whenever your source is a forecast, a weather app or another person.

### Example 1: Choosing the form

Your weather app shows rain for Saturday. Your friend asks, *Wie wird das Wetter am Samstag?*

- You're reporting the app: *Am Samstag soll es regnen.*
- You add your own expectation: *Ich glaube, es wird den ganzen Tag regnen.*
- You make a plan, treating it as fact: *Am Samstag regnet es, also gehen wir ins Museum.*

All three are correct; the first is the most natural answer to the question, because you're passing on the forecast.

## Celsius: how warm is 30 Grad?

German-speaking countries, like most of the world, measure temperature in degrees Celsius: *Es sind 20 Grad* means 20 °C. On a German thermometer (*das Thermometer*), water freezes at 0 and boils at 100.

![A thermometer with a Celsius scale on the left and a Fahrenheit scale on the right. Marked points: 100 °C = 212 °F, Wasser kocht (water boils); 37 °C = 98.6 °F, Körpertemperatur (body temperature); 20 °C = 68 °F, Zimmertemperatur (room temperature); 0 °C = 32 °F, Wasser gefriert (water freezes); minus 10 °C = 14 °F.](images/de2-m4-thermometer.svg)

If you think in Fahrenheit, use these formulas:

- Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9, divide by 5, add 32 (F = C × 9/5 + 32).
- Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract 32, multiply by 5, divide by 9 (C = (F − 32) × 5/9).
- A quick estimate from Celsius: double it and add 30. It's close in normal weather (20 °C → about 70 °F; exactly 68 °F) but gets worse at the extremes.

### Example 2: Converting both ways

1. The forecast says *25 Grad*. 25 × 9/5 = 45; 45 + 32 = **77 °F**. A warm summer day.
2. A friend in the US writes that it's 50 °F. 50 − 32 = 18; 18 × 5/9 = **10 °C**: *Bei uns sind es nur 10 Grad.*
3. *Minus fünf Grad*: −5 × 9/5 = −9; −9 + 32 = **23 °F**. Below freezing in both scales.

How to say temperatures:

- *Es sind 30 Grad (im Schatten).* — It's 30 degrees (in the shade). (*der Schatten* = shade)
- *Es sind minus fünf Grad* or *fünf Grad unter null.* — It's minus five.
- *null Grad* — zero degrees; *Plusgrade* and *Minusgrade* — temperatures above and below zero.
- With decimals, German uses a comma: *37,5 Grad Fieber*, said *siebenunddreißig Komma fünf*.

## Weather in German-speaking countries

A few things you'll hear about that have no exact English equivalent:

- ***Aprilwetter***: the quickly changing weather of April, sun, rain and hail in one afternoon. A common saying: *April, April, der macht, was er will.* — April does what it wants.
- ***Die Eisheiligen***: in folk tradition, frost is still possible until the "ice saints" days, 11 to 15 May, so many gardeners wait until after them to plant tomatoes. It's a rule of thumb, not a forecast.
- ***Der Föhn***: a warm, dry wind that comes down from the Alps; on the north side it brings clear views and sudden warmth to Munich or Innsbruck (when the wind blows the other way, the south side gets it). Many people say the *Föhn* gives them headaches; whether it really does is not clearly proven. (*der Föhn* is also the everyday word for a hairdryer.)
- ***Hitzefrei***: on very hot days, schools in Germany may send pupils home early. The rules differ from state to state, and many schools no longer have a fixed temperature for it, but every German child knows the word.

And the most-quoted weather saying of all, popular with hikers and parents: *Es gibt kein schlechtes Wetter, nur falsche Kleidung.* — There's no bad weather, only the wrong clothes.

### Weather idioms

| Idiom | Meaning | Register |
|---|---|---|
| *Es regnet in Strömen.* | It's pouring (in streams). | neutral |
| *Es gießt (wie aus Eimern).* | It's bucketing down. (*der Eimer* = bucket) | informal |
| *Was für ein Schmuddelwetter!* | What miserable, grey, wet weather! | informal |
| *bei Wind und Wetter* | in all weathers | neutral |
| *Ich friere wie ein Schneider.* | I'm freezing (literally: like a tailor). | informal |

### Example 3: Reading a forecast line

*Am Nachmittag wolkig, örtlich Gewitter mit Hagel, Höchstwerte 24 bis 28 Grad.* Take it apart:

- *Am Nachmittag wolkig*: in the afternoon, cloudy (forecasts often leave out the verb).
- *örtlich Gewitter mit Hagel*: thunderstorms with hail in places (*örtlich* = locally, in places).
- *Höchstwerte 24 bis 28 Grad*: highs of 24 to 28 degrees, which is about 75 to 82 °F. (*der Höchstwert* = highest value; the lowest is *der Tiefstwert*.)

A friend would retell it like this: *Heute Nachmittag soll es Gewitter geben, vielleicht sogar mit Hagel.*

## A text: Das Wetter im Radio

> Und nun das Wetter für Samstag. Im Norden bleibt es wolkig und regnerisch, an der Nordsee nieselt es fast den ganzen Tag. Die Temperaturen liegen dort bei 14 Grad. Im Süden wird es freundlicher: In München scheint am Vormittag die Sonne, am Nachmittag gibt es vom Westen her einzelne Gewitter, örtlich kann es auch hageln. Höchstwerte 26 Grad. In Österreich und der Schweiz bringt der Föhn trockene, warme Luft: In Innsbruck soll es bis zu 28 Grad warm werden. In der Nacht kühlt es überall ab, in den Alpen auf drei bis fünf Grad. Und am Sonntag? Da wird es im ganzen Land sonnig, aber deutlich kühler.

> Mia: Hast du den Wetterbericht gehört? In Innsbruck soll es morgen 28 Grad haben!
>
> Luca: Und hier an der Nordsee nieselt es. Typisch. Sauwetter!
>
> Mia: Es gibt kein schlechtes Wetter, nur falsche Kleidung.
>
> Luca: Ha, ha. Ich friere trotzdem.

In English:

> And now the weather for Saturday. In the north it stays cloudy and rainy, and on the North Sea coast it drizzles almost all day. Temperatures there are around 14 degrees. In the south it gets brighter: in Munich the sun shines in the morning; in the afternoon there are isolated thunderstorms coming in from the west, with hail in places. Highs 26 degrees. In Austria and Switzerland the Föhn brings dry, warm air: in Innsbruck it's expected to reach 28 degrees. At night it cools down everywhere, in the Alps to three to five degrees. And on Sunday? It'll be sunny across the whole country, but noticeably cooler.

> Mia: Did you hear the weather report? It's supposed to be 28 degrees in Innsbruck tomorrow!
>
> Luca: And here on the North Sea it's drizzling. Typical. Lousy weather!
>
> Mia: There's no bad weather, only the wrong clothes.
>
> Luca: Ha ha. I'm freezing all the same.

(*der Wetterbericht* = weather report, *freundlich* here = bright, pleasant, *einzeln* = isolated, single, *die Luft* = air, *abkühlen* = to cool down, *deutlich* = clearly, noticeably, *das Sauwetter* = lousy weather, informal. *Es hat 28 Grad* is a common southern German and Austrian way to say *Es sind 28 Grad*.) Notice the forms: *bleibt, nieselt, scheint* (plain present for the forecast), *soll … warm werden* and *soll … haben* (reporting), *wird es … sonnig* (prediction).

## Pronunciation

- *Föhn*: long *ö*, the *h* only lengthens it: FÖN.
- *nieseln*: long *ie*, *s* between vowels sounds like English *z*: NEE-zeln.
- *regnerisch*: REG-ne-risch, stress on the first syllable; the *g* is a clear *g*.
- *Thermometer*: ter-mo-MAY-ter; *th* is just *t* in German.
- *Eisheiligen*: EIS-hei-li-gen, both *ei* like English "eye".

## Common mistakes

- *Morgen regnet.* — The weather *es* never drops out: *Morgen regnet es.*
- *Es ist regnerig.* — The adjective is *regnerisch*.
- *Ich bin kalt.* — For feeling cold, say *Mir ist kalt* or *Ich friere*. *Ich bin kalt* describes a cold person.
- *Es sind 25 Grade.* — After a number, *Grad* has no ending: *25 Grad* (lesson 16).
- *Morgen wird es regnen* when you're only repeating the forecast. It's fine, but *Morgen soll es regnen* makes clear that it's what you heard.
- Converting with 5/9 and 9/5 the wrong way round. Celsius to Fahrenheit makes the number bigger (× 9/5, then + 32).

## Key points

- Weather verbs are **impersonal verbs** with *es*: *es regnet, nieselt, schneit, hagelt, donnert, blitzt, friert, taut*. The *es* always stays, and the perfect uses *haben*.
- *es gibt* + accusative (*Es gibt Gewitter*) and *es ist* + adjective (*Es ist schwül*).
- Adjectives from nouns with *-ig* (*sonnig, wolkig, neblig, frostig*); exception *regnerisch*.
- Tomorrow's weather: present (fact), *wird … regnen* (prediction), *soll … regnen* (the forecast says so).
- F = C × 9/5 + 32; C = (F − 32) × 5/9; *minus fünf Grad / fünf Grad unter null*.
- Culture: *Aprilwetter, Eisheiligen* (11–15 May), *Föhn, Hitzefrei*; *Es gibt kein schlechtes Wetter, nur falsche Kleidung.*
