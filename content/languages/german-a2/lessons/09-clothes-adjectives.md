## What this lesson is for

In A1 your adjectives always came after *sein*: *Die Jacke ist neu. Der Rock ist zu kurz.* That's safe, but real descriptions put the adjective right in front of the noun: "a new jacket", "my old shoes", "a short skirt". German can do that too, but then the adjective needs an ending. This module is about those endings, and it's the part of A2 grammar that learners worry about most.

The good news: the endings follow a small, logical system, and you already know its building blocks, because they are the same letters you see on *der, die, das, dem* and *den*. This lesson covers adjectives after *ein*, *kein* and the possessives (*mein, dein, sein, unser …*). Lesson 10 adds *der, dieser* and *welcher*, lesson 11 covers adjectives with no article at all, and lesson 12 compares things (*bigger, the biggest*).

The theme is clothes, because nothing makes you describe things as much as shopping for something to wear.

## Clothes

| der (masculine) | die (feminine) | das (neuter) |
|---|---|---|
| der Rock — skirt | die Kleidung — clothes, clothing | das Kleid — dress |
| der Pullover — jumper, sweater | die Hose — trousers | das Hemd — shirt |
| der Mantel — coat | die Jeans — jeans | das T-Shirt — T-shirt |
| der Anzug — suit | die Bluse — blouse | |
| der Schuh — shoe | die Jacke — jacket | |
| der Stiefel — boot | die Socke — sock | |
| der Hut — hat (with a brim) | die Mütze — woolly hat, cap | |
| der Schal — scarf | die Größe — size | |
| der Handschuh — glove | die Mode — fashion | |
| der Gürtel — belt | | |

A few things to notice:

- *die Hose* is singular in German: one pair of trousers is *eine Hose*. Two pairs are *zwei Hosen*. *die Jeans* can be singular or plural: *Die Jeans ist neu* and *Die Jeans sind neu* are both fine.
- *die Kleidung* is a collective word, like English "clothing": *Ich kaufe neue Kleidung* (I'm buying new clothes).
- A *Hut* has a brim; a *Mütze* is a soft woolly hat or a cap. In winter you wear a *Mütze*.
- *der Handschuh* is literally a "hand shoe".

The verb for wearing clothes is *tragen*, which also means "to carry". It changes its vowel like *fahren*: *ich trage, du trägst, er trägt*. To try something on in a shop, use the separable verb *anprobieren*: *Kann ich die Jacke anprobieren?* (Can I try the jacket on?) Three useful adjectives: *eng* (tight), *bequem* (comfortable) and *modisch* (fashionable). *weit*, which you know as "far", also means "loose, wide" for clothes: *Die Hose ist zu weit.* Patterns are *gestreift* (striped), *kariert* (checked) and *bunt* (colourful).

From lesson 7 you know that *passen* and *stehen* take the dative: *Die Hose passt mir* (the trousers fit me), *Das Kleid steht dir* (the dress suits you).

## Before the noun, an adjective needs an ending

Compare these two sentences:

- *Der Pullover ist warm.* — The jumper is warm.
- *Ich habe einen warmen Pullover.* — I have a warm jumper.

In the first, *warm* comes after *sein* and has no ending. This is a **predicative adjective**, and it never changes, exactly as in A1. In the second, *warm* stands between the article and the noun. This is an **attributive adjective**, and in German it always takes an **adjective ending**: *warmen*.

The ending depends on three things you already think about for articles: the gender of the noun, the case (nominative, accusative, dative), and whether it's singular or plural. It also depends on which article comes before it. That sounds like a lot, but there are only two possible endings in most places: *-e* and *-en*. The other three (*-er, -es, -em*) appear only where they are needed as signals.

## The signal idea

German likes every noun phrase to show its gender and case somewhere, once. Usually the article does this job: *der* shouts "masculine, nominative", *dem* shouts "masculine or neuter, dative". When the article already gives the signal, the adjective can relax and take a plain *-e* or *-en*.

But *ein* is a weak signal. *ein* can be masculine nominative (*ein Rock*), neuter nominative (*ein Kleid*) or neuter accusative (*ein Kleid*). Looking at *ein* alone, you can't tell. So in exactly those three places, the adjective takes over the job and shows the ending that *der* or *das* would have had:

- *der Rock* → *ein kurzer Rock* (the *-er* of *der*)
- *das Kleid* → *ein rotes Kleid* (the *-es* of *das*)

This is the **signal ending** rule: if the article doesn't show gender and case, the adjective does. Remember it, and the table below stops being something to memorise and becomes something you can work out.

## The endings after ein, kein and mein

*ein, kein* and the possessives *mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr* all take the same endings, so grammars call them **ein-words**. Here are their adjective endings in the nominative and accusative:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nominative | ein neuer Rock | eine neue Jacke | ein neues Kleid | meine neuen Schuhe |
| accusative | einen neuen Rock | eine neue Jacke | ein neues Kleid | meine neuen Schuhe |

Read the table with the signal idea:

- Masculine nominative: *ein* gives no signal, so the adjective takes *-er* (from *der*).
- Masculine accusative: *einen* already shows the accusative, so the adjective takes *-en*.
- Feminine: *eine* is clear, and the adjective takes *-e* in both cases.
- Neuter: *ein* gives no signal in either case, so the adjective takes *-es* (from *das*) in both.
- Plural: *meine, keine, unsere* are clear, so the adjective takes *-en*. (There is no plural of *ein*; lesson 11 deals with adjectives that have no article.)

Examples:

- *Das ist ein schöner Mantel.* — That's a lovely coat.
- *Ich suche einen warmen Mantel.* — I'm looking for a warm coat.
- *Sie trägt eine gestreifte Bluse.* — She's wearing a striped blouse.
- *Er hat ein kariertes Hemd an.* — He's got a checked shirt on.
- *Das sind keine bequemen Schuhe.* — Those aren't comfortable shoes.
- *Wo sind meine alten Stiefel?* — Where are my old boots?

## The dative: always -en

The dative is the easiest case of all. After *einem, einer, keinem, meinen* and the rest, every adjective ending is *-en*:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| dative | einem neuen Rock | einer neuen Jacke | einem neuen Kleid | meinen neuen Schuhen |

The dative article (*einem, einer*) is a strong, clear signal, so the adjective just adds *-en*. Remember that plural nouns add *-n* in the dative too: *mit meinen neuen Schuhen*.

- *Ich gehe mit meiner besten Freundin einkaufen.* — I'm going shopping with my best friend.
- *Was trägst du zu einem schwarzen Anzug?* — What do you wear with a black suit?
- *Er spricht mit einem netten Verkäufer.* — He's talking to a nice sales assistant.

## Putting it together

All the ein-word endings in one table:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nominative | -er | -e | -es | -en |
| accusative | -en | -e | -es | -en |
| dative | -en | -en | -en | -en |

A memory hook: draw a line around the five boxes in the top left (masculine nominative, feminine nominative and accusative, neuter nominative and accusative). Those are the only places that aren't *-en*. Everything else is *-en*.

To choose an ending, ask three questions: What gender is the noun (or is it plural)? What case is it in? Does the article already show that? Then:

1. *Ich kaufe (ein) (blau) Pullover.* — masculine, accusative (object of *kaufen*), and *einen* shows it → *einen blauen Pullover*.
2. *(Ein) (gelb) Mütze liegt auf dem Tisch.* — feminine, nominative → *Eine gelbe Mütze*.
3. *Sie kommt mit (ein) (rot) Kleid.* — neuter, dative (after *mit*) → *mit einem roten Kleid*.

## Small spelling changes

- Adjectives ending in *-el* drop the *e* before an ending: *dunkel* → *eine dunkle Hose*.
- Adjectives ending in *-er* after *au* or *eu* drop that *e* too: *teuer* → *ein teures Hemd*.
- Two or more adjectives take the same ending: *ein langer, warmer Mantel*.
- A few colours borrowed from other languages, like *lila* (purple) and *rosa* (pink), take no ending at all in standard German: *ein rosa Hemd*.

## Dialogue: In the shop

> Verkäuferin: Guten Tag! Kann ich Ihnen helfen?
>
> Lena: Ja, gern. Ich suche einen warmen Mantel für den Winter. Mein alter Mantel ist leider kaputt.
>
> Verkäuferin: Welche Größe haben Sie?
>
> Lena: Achtunddreißig, glaube ich. Haben Sie auch einen langen Mantel in Dunkelblau?
>
> Verkäuferin: Ja, hier. Das ist ein sehr modischer Mantel, und er ist aus Wolle. Möchten Sie ihn anprobieren?
>
> Lena: Gern … Hm, er ist ein bisschen eng. Haben Sie ihn eine Nummer größer?
>
> Verkäuferin: Moment … Ja, hier ist er in Größe 40. Der ist bequemer.
>
> Lena: Ja, der passt mir gut. Und haben Sie vielleicht auch einen karierten Schal dazu?
>
> Verkäuferin: Natürlich. Zu einem dunkelblauen Mantel passt ein grauer oder ein roter Schal sehr gut.
>
> Lena: Dann nehme ich den roten. Meine Mutter sagt immer, ich trage zu wenig Farbe!

In English:

> Sales assistant: Hello! Can I help you?
>
> Lena: Yes, please. I'm looking for a warm coat for the winter. Unfortunately my old coat is broken.
>
> Sales assistant: What size are you?
>
> Lena: Thirty-eight, I think. Do you also have a long coat in dark blue?
>
> Sales assistant: Yes, here. This is a very fashionable coat, and it's made of wool. Would you like to try it on?
>
> Lena: I'd love to … Hm, it's a bit tight. Do you have it one size bigger?
>
> Sales assistant: One moment … Yes, here it is in size 40. This one's more comfortable.
>
> Lena: Yes, this one fits me well. And do you maybe have a checked scarf to go with it?
>
> Sales assistant: Of course. A grey or a red scarf goes very well with a dark blue coat.
>
> Lena: Then I'll take the red one. My mother always says I wear too little colour!

Look at the endings: *einen warmen Mantel* and *einen langen Mantel* (masculine accusative, *-en*); *mein alter Mantel* and *ein sehr modischer Mantel* (masculine nominative, *-er*); *zu einem dunkelblauen Mantel* (dative, *-en*); *ein grauer oder ein roter Schal* (subject of *passt*, so nominative, *-er*). *aus Wolle* means "made of wool"; lesson 10 has more materials. *bequemer* and *größer* are comparatives, which lesson 12 explains. *Dann nehme ich den roten* uses the article *den* and leaves out the noun; lesson 10 shows how that works.

## Pronunciation

- *Hemd*: the *d* at the end sounds like *t*: "hemt".
- *Bluse*: the *s* before a vowel is a soft "z": BLOO-ze.
- *Mütze*: short *ü*, then a sharp *tz*: MÜT-se.
- *Jeans* and *T-Shirt* are said roughly as in English, but *T-Shirt* is often "tee-shirt" with a German *t*.
- *anprobieren*: stress on the separable prefix and on *-ie-*: AN-pro-bee-ren.
- Adjective endings are never stressed: *ein KURzer Rock*, *einen WARmen Mantel*. Don't swallow them, though: the ending is the whole point.

## Common mistakes

- *Das ist ein neu Kleid.* — Before a noun, the adjective needs an ending: *ein neues Kleid*.
- *Der Pullover ist warmer.* — After *sein*, no ending: *Der Pullover ist warm.* (*wärmer* means "warmer", which is a comparison.)
- *Ich trage ein schwarzer Pullover.* — The object is accusative: *einen schwarzen Pullover*.
- *Sie hat eine neues Bluse.* — *Bluse* is feminine, so *-e*: *eine neue Bluse*. Check the gender first.
- *mit einem roter Schal* — after any dative article the ending is *-en*: *mit einem roten Schal*.
- *eine dunkele Jacke* — drop the *e* of *-el*: *eine dunkle Jacke*.
- *Ich trage eine Hosen.* — *Hose* is singular in German: *eine Hose*.

## Key points

- An adjective after *sein* never has an ending; an adjective before a noun always does.
- After *ein, kein* and possessives: masculine nominative *-er*, neuter nominative and accusative *-es*, feminine nominative and accusative *-e*, everything else *-en*.
- Every adjective in the dative and every plural after *keine/meine* ends in *-en*.
- The signal rule: when *ein* doesn't show the gender, the adjective shows it with the ending of *der* or *das*.
- *dunkel → dunkle*, *teuer → teure*; *lila* and *rosa* never change.
- *tragen* (du trägst, er trägt) means to wear and to carry; *anprobieren* is separable.
