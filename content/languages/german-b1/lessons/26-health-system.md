## What this lesson is for

This last module is about living on your own in a German-speaking country: dealing with doctors and offices, neighbours and landlords, travelling, writing letters, and finally the B1 exam. We start with the health system and social services, the part of daily life where you most often have to pass on what someone told you: *Die Ärztin hat gesagt, dass ich drei Tage zu Hause bleiben soll.* *Die Krankenkasse hat gefragt, ob ich eine Überweisung habe.*

So the grammar of this lesson is **reported speech**: saying what someone else said, asked or told you to do. In everyday German you already have nearly everything you need for it (*dass*, *ob*, question words and the Konjunktiv II from module 5). The new part is a set of verb forms you will mainly meet in the news, the **Konjunktiv I** (*er sei, sie habe, er komme*). At B1 you only need to recognize them; using them actively is a B2 skill.

## How the health system works

Health insurance is compulsory in Germany. Most employees have statutory insurance with a *Krankenkasse* (health insurance fund); people above a certain income, the self-employed and civil servants can choose private insurance instead. Contributions come straight out of your wages, and your employer pays about half. Health insurance is one of five branches of the *Sozialversicherung* (social insurance):

| German | English |
|---|---|
| *die Krankenversicherung* | health insurance |
| *die Pflegeversicherung* | long-term care insurance |
| *die Rentenversicherung* | pension insurance |
| *die Arbeitslosenversicherung* | unemployment insurance |
| *die Unfallversicherung* | accident insurance (at work) |

Notice how German builds these: *Kranken-* + *Versicherung*, *Arbeitslosen-* + *Versicherung*. The last part decides the gender (*die Versicherung*, so all of them are *die*), and the first part says what kind.

When you're ill, you usually go to your *Hausarzt* or *Hausärztin* (family doctor, GP) first, ideally during the *Sprechstunde* (surgery hours). For many problems the family doctor gives you an *Überweisung* to a *Facharzt* or *Fachärztin* (specialist). You know *die Überweisung* as a bank transfer from A2; here it means a referral, and the logic is the same: something is "sent over" to someone else. If you're too ill to work, the doctor will *krankschreiben* you (sign you off sick). In Germany your employer usually needs this sick note, the *Krankmeldung*, from the fourth day of illness, though some employers want it from the first day.

In an emergency you go to the *Notaufnahme* (A&E, emergency room) of a hospital, or call 112. For anything that can wait until the next day, many people sit in the *Wartezimmer* (waiting room) of their family doctor instead.

Some other words you will need:

- *Die Ärztin stellt eine Diagnose und schlägt eine Behandlung vor.* — The doctor makes a diagnosis and suggests a treatment.
- *Welche Nebenwirkungen hat das Medikament?* — What side effects does the medicine have?
- *Er hat eine chronische Krankheit und braucht eine Therapie.* — He has a chronic illness and needs therapy.
- *Die Krankenkasse zahlt einen Zuschuss für die Brille.* — The health insurance fund pays a contribution towards the glasses.

*Vorsorge* is prevention: things you do so that you don't get ill. *Vorsorgeuntersuchungen* (check-ups) and *Impfungen* (vaccinations) are typical *Leistungen* (benefits, services) of the *Krankenkasse*: *Ich lasse mich jeden Herbst gegen Grippe impfen.* — I get vaccinated against flu every autumn. The verb *vorbeugen* takes the dative: *einer Krankheit vorbeugen* — to prevent an illness.

When people get old or can't look after themselves, a *Pflegekraft* (care worker) may come to their home, or they move into a *Pflegeheim* (care home). The *Pflegeversicherung* pays part of the cost. If you need help with any of these questions, there is usually a *Beratungsstelle* (advice centre), for example at the town hall or at a charity.

Switzerland and Austria work differently in the details: in Switzerland, everyone must take out basic health insurance but buys it from a private insurer, and in Austria most employees are insured through their job with the *Österreichische Gesundheitskasse*. The words are mostly the same.

## Reported speech in everyday German

When you report what somebody said, you change the point of view, just as in English: *I* becomes *he* or *she*, *you* becomes *I*.

> Dr. Weber: „Sie müssen drei Tage im Bett bleiben."
>
> You, later: *Dr. Weber hat gesagt, dass ich drei Tage im Bett bleiben muss.*

### Statements: dass, or main-clause order

Use *dass* + verb at the end, as you learned in A2. In speech, German also often drops *dass* and keeps main-clause order after the comma:

- *Sie sagt, dass sie morgen kommt.* — She says (that) she's coming tomorrow.
- *Sie sagt, sie kommt morgen.* — the same, more relaxed.

Both are correct. The second one is only possible without *dass*: ~~Sie sagt, dass sie kommt morgen~~ is wrong.

### Yes/no questions: ob

A reported yes/no question starts with *ob* (whether, if):

- „Haben Sie eine Überweisung?" → *Die Sprechstundenhilfe hat gefragt, ob ich eine Überweisung habe.* — The receptionist asked whether I had a referral.

### W-questions: the question word

A reported W-question keeps its question word, and the verb goes to the end:

- „Wann ist die Sprechstunde?" → *Er möchte wissen, wann die Sprechstunde ist.*
- „Wie lange dauert die Behandlung?" → *Sie hat gefragt, wie lange die Behandlung dauert.*

### Requests and instructions: sollen

When you pass on an instruction, German uses *sollen*:

- „Nehmen Sie die Tabletten nach dem Essen!" → *Der Arzt hat gesagt, ich soll die Tabletten nach dem Essen nehmen.* — The doctor said I should take the tablets after meals.
- „Ruf mich bitte an!" → *Mama sagt, du sollst sie anrufen.* — Mum says you're to call her.

### Tense: no need to shift

English often shifts the tense back ("She said she *was* ill"). German doesn't have to: if she said „Ich bin krank", you can report *Sie hat gesagt, dass sie krank ist*. If the original was in the past, keep it in the past: „Ich war beim Arzt" → *Er hat erzählt, dass er beim Arzt war.*

### Konjunktiv II for distance

In spoken German many people use the Konjunktiv II in reported speech, especially when they doubt what they're reporting or want to sound careful:

- *Er hat gesagt, er hätte keine Zeit.* — He said he didn't have time (but who knows).
- *Sie meinte, das wäre kein Problem.* — She said it wasn't a problem.

This is normal and correct in speech and informal writing. In news texts you'll see a different form, the Konjunktiv I.

## Konjunktiv I: recognizing the news form

Newspapers, radio news and official reports need to make clear that they are only passing on what someone said, without claiming it's true. For this they use the Konjunktiv I. You almost only meet it in the third person singular, where it is built from the infinitive stem + *-e*:

| infinitive | indicative | Konjunktiv I |
|---|---|---|
| *sein* | *er ist* | *er sei* |
| *haben* | *er hat* | *er habe* |
| *werden* | *er wird* | *er werde* |
| *kommen* | *er kommt* | *er komme* |
| *geben* | *es gibt* | *es gebe* |
| *können* | *er kann* | *er könne* |
| *müssen* | *er muss* | *er müsse* |
| *wollen* | *er will* | *er wolle* |
| *wissen* | *er weiß* | *er wisse* |

The past is *habe* or *sei* + participle: *Er habe nichts gewusst. Sie sei nach Hause gegangen.* *sei* also has plural forms you'll see: *Die Kosten seien gestiegen.*

How to spot it: a third-person verb ending in *-e* (*er komme, sie habe*) or the forms *sei/seien*. Then read it as "he/she says that …":

- *Der Minister erklärte, das Gesundheitssystem sei stabil.* — The minister said the health system was stable (according to him).
- *Laut der Krankenkasse gebe es genug Impfstoff.* — According to the health insurance fund, there is enough vaccine.

Where the Konjunktiv I looks the same as the normal present (*sie haben*, *wir kommen*), writers switch to the Konjunktiv II: *Die Ärzte sagen, sie hätten zu wenig Zeit für ihre Patienten.* That's why you see both in the same article.

Two words often travel with reported information: *laut* (according to, with the dative or genitive: *laut dem Bericht*, *laut des Berichts*) and *angeblich* (supposedly, allegedly): *Die Praxis ist angeblich bis Mai voll.* — The practice is supposedly full until May.

## Reading: a news report

> *Lange Wartezeiten beim Facharzt*
>
> Patientinnen und Patienten in Deutschland müssen immer länger auf einen Termin beim Facharzt warten. Das geht aus einer neuen Umfrage einer großen Krankenkasse hervor. Laut der Studie wartet man auf einen Termin bei einer Hautärztin im Durchschnitt fast sechs Wochen. Besonders schwierig sei die Lage auf dem Land, sagte eine Sprecherin der Krankenkasse. Dort gebe es zu wenige Praxen, und viele ältere Ärzte fänden keine Nachfolger.
>
> Die Ärzteverbände widersprechen nur zum Teil. Ein Vertreter erklärte, das Problem sei bekannt. Viele Menschen gingen aber direkt zum Facharzt, obwohl der Hausarzt sie auch behandeln könnte. Er empfahl, zuerst in die Sprechstunde der Hausärztin zu gehen. Mit einer Überweisung bekomme man oft schneller einen Termin.
>
> Auch bei der Vorsorge sieht die Krankenkasse Probleme. Viele Versicherte wüssten nicht, welche Untersuchungen und Impfungen sie kostenlos bekommen können. Die Kasse will deshalb mehr informieren: Ab Herbst sollen ihre Mitglieder einmal im Jahr eine Erinnerung per App bekommen. Wer Fragen zu seinen Leistungen hat, kann sich auch an die Beratungsstellen in den Städten wenden.

Translation:

> *Long waits for specialists*
>
> Patients in Germany are having to wait longer and longer for an appointment with a specialist. This is the result of a new survey by a large health insurance fund. According to the study, people wait almost six weeks on average for an appointment with a dermatologist. The situation is particularly difficult in the countryside, said a spokeswoman for the insurance fund. There are too few practices there, she said, and many older doctors can't find anyone to take over.
>
> The doctors' associations only partly disagree. A representative said the problem was known. But many people went straight to a specialist, he said, although their family doctor could also treat them. He recommended going to the family doctor's surgery first. With a referral, he said, you often get an appointment faster.
>
> The insurance fund also sees problems with preventive care. Many insured people don't know, it says, which check-ups and vaccinations they can get free of charge. The fund therefore wants to inform people more: from the autumn, its members will get a reminder once a year by app. Anyone with questions about their benefits can also contact the advice centres in the towns.

Notes: find the Konjunktiv I forms (*sei, gebe, bekomme*) and the Konjunktiv II forms used instead where Konjunktiv I would look like the indicative (*fänden, gingen, wüssten*). The English translation keeps adding "she said", "he said", "it says", because English has no verb form that does this job. *aus etwas hervorgehen* means "to emerge from, be shown by"; *der Nachfolger* is the successor; *die Hautärztin* is a dermatologist (*die Haut* = skin). *sich an jemanden wenden* means to turn to, contact someone.

## Pronunciation

- *Patient*: pa-TSIENT, with *ti* pronounced *tsi* as in *Information*.
- *Therapie*: the-ra-PIE, stress on the last syllable, like *Diagnose* (di-a-GNO-se, stress on *-gno-*).
- *chronisch*: KRO-nisch; *ch* before *r* in Greek words is a *k*.
- *Impfung*: the *pf* is one sound; don't drop the *p*.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to change the pronouns: „Ich bin müde", sagt Tom → ~~Tom sagt, dass ich müde bin.~~ → *Tom sagt, dass er müde ist.*
- Using *wenn* or *dass* for a yes/no question: ~~Sie fragt, wenn ich Zeit habe.~~ → *Sie fragt, ob ich Zeit habe.*
- Main-clause order after *dass*: ~~Er sagt, dass er hat Fieber.~~ → *Er sagt, dass er Fieber hat* or *Er sagt, er hat Fieber.*
- Using *müssen* for a reported request: „Kommen Sie morgen!" → *Sie hat gesagt, ich soll morgen kommen* (not *muss*, which sounds like an obligation from outside).
- Reading *er sei* as a typo or a command. It's the Konjunktiv I of *sein*: he is (they say).
- *der Patient* is a weak noun: *den Patienten, dem Patienten* (lesson 5).
- *vorbeugen* with the accusative: ~~eine Krankheit vorbeugen~~ → *einer Krankheit vorbeugen*.

## Key points

- Report statements with *dass* (verb at the end) or with a comma and normal order: *Sie sagt, sie kommt.*
- Yes/no questions → *ob*; W-questions → the question word; instructions → *sollen*.
- Change the pronouns to fit the new speaker; the tense can stay the same.
- In speech, Konjunktiv II (*er hätte, sie wäre*) adds distance.
- In the news, the Konjunktiv I (*er sei, sie habe, es gebe, er könne*) means "according to what was said". Recognize it; you don't need to produce it.
- The *Sozialversicherung* has five parts; your *Hausarzt* gives you an *Überweisung* to a *Facharzt*; a doctor can *krankschreiben* you.
