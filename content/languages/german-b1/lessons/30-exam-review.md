## What this lesson is for

This is the last lesson of German B1. As at the end of A1 and A2, it does three things: it explains what B1 means, describes the best-known exam at this level, the Goethe-Zertifikat B1, with strategies for each part, and reviews the grammar of the whole course with pointers back to the lessons. The questions at the end of the lesson cover the whole course. You don't need to take the exam, but B1 is the level many people need for work, study or residence, so it's worth knowing how it works. Check the current requirements with the office or employer that asks for it.

## What B1 means

B1 is the third of the six levels of the **CEFR** (Common European Framework of Reference for Languages), and the first of the two "independent user" levels. The framework describes each level with **can-do statements**. In plain words, someone at B1:

- understands the main points when people speak clear standard language about familiar things: work, school, free time, the news on topics they know;
- can deal with most situations that come up while travelling where the language is spoken;
- can write simple, connected texts on topics they know or care about;
- can describe experiences, events, hopes and plans, and give short reasons and explanations for opinions.

The step from A2 is real. At A2 you got by in routine situations; at B1 you can tell a story in the past with *nachdem* and the Präteritum (lessons 1–3), argue with *obwohl, damit, je … desto* (lessons 6–10), describe things precisely with relative clauses and the genitive (lessons 11–15), follow the news with the passive (lessons 16–20), talk about what could have been with the Konjunktiv II (lessons 21–23), and handle offices, doctors, landlords and formal letters (this module). What's still hard is fast speech, dialects, and abstract topics you don't know, and that is the job of B2.

## The Goethe-Zertifikat B1

The adult exam is **modular**: there are four *Module* (modules), and you can take all four on one day or only the ones you need.

| module | time | what you do |
|---|---|---|
| Lesen (reading) | 65 minutes | 5 parts: a blog or other personal text; press articles; matching people's situations to ads; readers' opinions on a topic; rules or instructions |
| Hören (listening) | about 40 minutes | 4 parts: short announcements and messages; a monologue such as a guided tour; an informal conversation; a discussion on the radio |
| Schreiben (writing) | 60 minutes | 3 tasks: an informal e-mail (about 80 words); a forum post giving your opinion (about 80 words); a semi-formal e-mail (about 40 words) |
| Sprechen (speaking) | about 15 minutes, plus 15 minutes to prepare | in pairs: plan something together; present a topic; react to your partner's presentation and answer questions on your own |

Each module is scored out of 100 points and passed with 60. The grades are the same as at A2:

| points | grade |
|---|---|
| 90–100 | sehr gut (very good) |
| 80–89 | gut (good) |
| 70–79 | befriedigend (satisfactory) |
| 60–69 | ausreichend (sufficient, a pass) |

Because the modules are separate, you can repeat only the one you failed. Results from the same year can usually be combined into one certificate if you took them at the same exam centre or in the same country; the details depend on the centre, so ask before you register. Dates, fees and rules can change too.

The Goethe-Institut publishes free official practice material:

Other B1 exams, such as telc Deutsch B1 and the ÖSD Zertifikat B1, and the *Deutsch-Test für Zuwanderer* used in German integration courses, test the same level with different tasks.

## Strategies for each module

### Lesen

- Read the *Aufgabenstellung* (task instructions) and the questions first. Every *Anweisung* tells you exactly what to do: *Lesen Sie die Aussagen 1 bis 6. Sind sie richtig oder falsch?*
- In true/false tasks, look for the sentence in the text that matches the *Aussage* (statement), then compare carefully. Small words decide: *nur, noch nicht, kaum, nicht mehr, alle, manche*.
- In matching tasks, you *zuordnen* (match) situations to ads. Underline the key need of each person, and remember that one situation may have no matching ad. Check whether an ad really *zutrifft* (applies) to all of the need, not only one word.
- In the readers' opinions part, decide for each person whether they are for or against something. Watch for *zwar … aber*, *einerseits … andererseits* and *obwohl*: the main opinion often comes after them.
- Use your time: 65 minutes for five parts. Don't spend 20 minutes on one difficult question.

### Hören

- Read the questions before each recording and *markieren* (mark) or *unterstreichen* (underline) the key words.
- In announcements, the last information counts: *Der Zug fährt heute nicht von Gleis 3, sondern von Gleis 5.*
- In conversations and discussions, keep track of who says what: each *Sprecher* or *Sprecherin* has a position. Note a word for each person as you listen.
- Listen for the main point, not every word. If you miss something, keep going; the next question is more important.

### Schreiben

- Lesson 29 is your preparation. For each task, plan for two minutes: a short *Gliederung* (outline) with the content points in order.
- Cover every point. The *Inhalt* (content) counts as much as language.
- Choose the right register: friend → informal; forum → neutral; teacher or landlord → semi-formal.
- Show what you can do: one or two subordinate clauses, a connector or two, a polite Konjunktiv II. Write sentences you are sure of; a simple, correct *Formulierung* is better than a complicated wrong one.
- Save five minutes to check *Rechtschreibung* (spelling), capitals, verb position and commas.

### Sprechen

- In the 15 minutes of preparation, write keywords, not full sentences. Nobody wants to hear you read.
- In the planning task, the examiners want a real conversation: suggest, react, disagree politely, agree. Use your partner's ideas: *Gute Idee, und dann könnten wir …*
- For the presentation, follow the five-part structure from lesson 28: introduction, own experience, home country, pros and cons with your opinion, conclusion. *Gliedern* (structure) your talk out loud: *Zuerst …, dann …, zum Schluss …*
- When your partner has finished, react: say something positive or ask a question. Then answer the questions you're asked.
- If you can't find a word, describe it: *Das ist ein Ding, mit dem man …* Being able to *sich ausdrücken* (express yourself) around a gap is a B1 skill.

## How the writing and speaking are marked

Examiners don't count mistakes. They look at a few broad questions:

- Did you do the task? (all points covered, the right register, the right length)
- Is the text or talk well connected? (connectors, references back, a clear order)
- How wide is your *Wortschatz* (vocabulary) and your range of *Ausdrücke* (expressions)?
- How correct is the *Grammatik*, and do mistakes get in the way of understanding?
- In speaking: pronunciation, and whether you really interact with your partner.

So a text with a few small errors that is clear, complete and *angemessen* (appropriate) for the reader gets a good *Punktzahl* (score). A text that's perfect but misses a point or uses the wrong register loses points.

## Dialogue: planning together

Two learners, Lukas and Amira, practise the first speaking task. Their class wants to give a farewell party for their teacher.

> Amira: Lukas, wir sollen ja die Abschiedsfeier für Frau Brandt organisieren. Wann wollen wir die Feier machen?
>
> Lukas: Ich würde sagen, am letzten Kurstag, also am Freitag. Dann sind noch alle da.
>
> Amira: Das finde ich gut. Aber nicht im Kursraum, oder? Der ist so klein.
>
> Lukas: Stimmt. Wie wäre es mit dem Café gegenüber? Wir könnten dort einen Tisch reservieren.
>
> Amira: Hm, ich bin nicht sicher. Das ist ziemlich teuer, und wir sind zwanzig Leute. Was hältst du davon, wenn jeder etwas zu essen mitbringt und wir im Park feiern?
>
> Lukas: Gute Idee, aber was machen wir, wenn es regnet?
>
> Amira: Dann fragen wir die Schule, ob wir den großen Raum im Erdgeschoss benutzen dürfen. Das kann ich übernehmen.
>
> Lukas: Super. Und ein Geschenk brauchen wir auch. Ich schlage vor, dass wir ein Buch über unsere Stadt kaufen und alle etwas hineinschreiben.
>
> Amira: Das ist schön persönlich. Ich sammle das Geld, und du kaufst das Buch?
>
> Lukas: Einverstanden. Also: Freitag, Park oder Erdgeschoss, Essen von allen, und ein Buch mit Widmungen.

Translation:

> Amira: Lukas, we're supposed to organize the farewell party for Ms Brandt. When shall we have it?
>
> Lukas: I'd say on the last day of the course, so on Friday. Everyone will still be there then.
>
> Amira: I think that's good. But not in the classroom, right? It's so small.
>
> Lukas: True. How about the café opposite? We could book a table there.
>
> Amira: Hm, I'm not sure. It's quite expensive, and there are twenty of us. What do you think about everyone bringing something to eat and having the party in the park?
>
> Lukas: Good idea, but what do we do if it rains?
>
> Amira: Then we'll ask the school whether we can use the big room on the ground floor. I can take care of that.
>
> Lukas: Great. And we need a present too. I suggest we buy a book about our town and everyone writes something in it.
>
> Amira: That's nice and personal. I'll collect the money and you buy the book?
>
> Lukas: Agreed. So: Friday, the park or the ground floor, food from everyone, and a book with dedications.

What makes this a good B1 conversation: both speakers make suggestions (*Wie wäre es mit …? Was hältst du davon, wenn …? Ich schlage vor, dass …*), react to each other, disagree politely with a reason (*Hm, ich bin nicht sicher. Das ist ziemlich teuer*), solve a problem (*was machen wir, wenn es regnet?*), share the jobs, and sum up at the end. *die Widmung* is a dedication; *übernehmen* is to take on, take care of.

If you remember only three things from B1, make them these: the verb's position tells the reader what kind of clause they're in (second, end, or first); the Konjunktiv II is how German is polite, imagines, and regrets; and connectors turn sentences into a text.

## What comes next: B2

At B2 you'll understand the main ideas of complex texts, including technical discussions in your own field, and talk with native speakers without strain on either side. The grammar continues with the Konjunktiv I used actively, more participle constructions (*die von der Stadt gebaute Brücke*), noun-heavy written style, and more connectors. The biggest change, though, is vocabulary, and the best way to build it is to read and listen to real German every day, mainly things you enjoy. The reading list for this course is a good start, and regular review keeps what you have learned.

## Exam and study language

These words come up in instructions, in feedback and in your own planning:

- *Insgesamt hat die Prüfung vier Module.* — Altogether the exam has four modules.
- *Der Text war inhaltlich gut, aber die Rechtschreibung war schwach.* — The text was good in terms of content, but the spelling was weak.
- *Formulieren Sie Ihre Meinung in zwei oder drei Sätzen.* — Put your opinion into words in two or three sentences.
- *Im Hörtext geht es hauptsächlich um das Wohnen.* — The listening text is mainly about housing.
- *Wie bereitest du dich auf die Prüfung vor?* — *Ich lerne jeden Tag neue Wörter, und am Wochenende mache ich eine Wiederholung.* — How are you preparing for the exam? — I learn new words every day, and at the weekend I do a review.
- *Die Vorbereitung dauert ungefähr drei Monate.* — The preparation takes about three months.
- *Die Prüferin hat mir viele Fragen gestellt.* *Der Prüfer war sehr freundlich.* — The examiner asked me lots of questions. The examiner was very friendly.
- *Meine Strategie: zuerst die Fragen lesen, dann den Text.* — My strategy: read the questions first, then the text.
- *Mein Niveau ist jetzt B1.* — My level is now B1.

## Pronunciation

- *Modul*: mo-DUL, stress on the second syllable (not like English "module").
- *Niveau*: ni-WOH, from French; the *-eau* is a long *o*.
- *Grammatik*: gram-MA-tik, stress on the second syllable.
- *Formulierung*: for-mu-LIE-rung, stress on *-lie-* like all *-ieren* verbs and their nouns.

## Common mistakes

- In Lesen, choosing an ad because one word matches. It has to fit the whole situation.
- In Schreiben, writing 150 words for an 80-word task. Longer isn't better; it only gives you more room for mistakes and less time to check.
- In Sprechen, giving a monologue in the planning task. React to your partner.
- Learning set phrases for the presentation but not practising them aloud. Say your introduction and conclusion until they feel automatic.
- *die Prüfung bestehen* (pass) vs *die Prüfung machen / ablegen* (take): ~~Ich habe die Prüfung gemacht~~ doesn't tell anyone whether you passed.

## Key points

- B1 = an independent user on familiar topics: you can follow the main points, travel, write connected texts, and describe experiences and plans with reasons.
- The Goethe-Zertifikat B1 has four modules (Lesen 65 min, Hören about 40 min, Schreiben 60 min, Sprechen about 15 min plus 15 min preparation), each out of 100, passed with 60.
- Modules can be taken and repeated separately.
- Read the task first, cover every point, choose the right register, react to your partner.
- Practise with the free official model tests.
