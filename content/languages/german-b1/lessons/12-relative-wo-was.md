## What this lesson is for

In the last lesson every relative clause described a noun with a gender: *der Kollege, mit dem …*; *die Kritik, die …*. But you often want to describe things that don't work that way: a place (*the town where I grew up*), something without a real noun (*everything I know*, *nothing that helps*), or a whole situation (*he was late again, which annoyed me*). For these German uses three question words as relative words: *wo* (and *wohin, woher*), *was* and *wer*.

The theme is cities and culture: old towns and castles, museums and exhibitions, festivals and the stage. When you show someone around or write about a trip, you need exactly these sentences: *die Burg, wo …*, *das Beste, was …*, *alles, was man sehen muss*.

## wo, wohin, woher for places

When the antecedent is a place, you can use *wo* instead of a preposition + relative pronoun. Both are correct:

- *Das ist das Café, in dem wir uns kennengelernt haben.*
- *Das ist das Café, wo wir uns kennengelernt haben.* — That's the café where we met.

*wo* is a **relative adverb**: it doesn't change for gender or case, which makes it easy. It is very common in speech and fine in writing. Use *wohin* and *woher* when the verb shows movement, exactly as in questions:

- *Die Stadt, wo ich studiert habe, hat einen schönen Dom.* — The town where I studied has a beautiful cathedral. (*wo*: place, no movement)
- *Das Schloss, wohin der Ausflug geht, liegt am See.* — The palace where the trip is going is by the lake. (*wohin*: direction)
- *Das Dorf, woher meine Großeltern kommen, hat nur 300 Einwohner.* — The village my grandparents come from has only 300 inhabitants. (*woher*: origin)

### With the names of towns and countries: only wo

With a city or country name that has no article, *wo* is the natural choice; a preposition + pronoun sounds stiff:

- *Ich war letztes Jahr in Salzburg, wo ich viele Konzerte besucht habe.* — I was in Salzburg last year, where I went to lots of concerts.
- *Im Sommer fahren wir nach Sizilien, wohin ich schon immer reisen wollte.* — In summer we're going to Sicily, where I've always wanted to go.

Note the comma: a relative clause after a name just adds information, but it still needs commas.

### wo for time: spoken only

In speech you will hear *wo* after time words too: *in dem Moment, wo das Licht ausging*; *an dem Tag, wo wir uns kennengelernt haben*. It is everyday German, but in writing use a preposition + relative pronoun or *als*: *an dem Tag, an dem wir uns kennengelernt haben*.

## was after alles, etwas, nichts

After words that don't name a real thing, the relative pronoun is *was*:

| antecedent | example |
|---|---|
| *alles* | *Das ist alles, was ich über die Stadt weiß.* — That's all I know about the town. |
| *etwas* | *Gibt es etwas, was du unbedingt sehen willst?* — Is there anything you really want to see? |
| *nichts* | *Es gab nichts, was mir nicht gefallen hat.* — There was nothing I didn't like. |
| *vieles, einiges, manches* | *Es gibt vieles, was man hier erleben kann.* — There's a lot you can experience here. |
| *das* (that) | *Das, was du gesagt hast, stimmt nicht.* — What you said isn't true. |

*was* doesn't change for case: *alles, was passiert ist* (subject); *alles, was ich gesehen habe* (object). With *etwas* you will also see *das* (*etwas, das …*); both are correct.

When the verb needs a preposition, use a *wo(r)-* word: *wo* + preposition, with an *r* before a vowel:

- *Das ist alles, worüber wir gesprochen haben.* — That's everything we talked about.
- *Es gibt nichts, wofür ich mich weniger interessiere.* — There's nothing I'm less interested in.
- *Das Konzert war etwas, worauf ich mich lange gefreut hatte.* — The concert was something I'd looked forward to for a long time.

### After superlatives and neuter adjectival nouns

After a neuter adjective used as a noun, and especially after a superlative, German also uses *was*:

- *Das Beste, was ich in Wien gegessen habe, war ein Apfelstrudel.* — The best thing I ate in Vienna was an apple strudel.
- *Das Einzige, was mir nicht gefallen hat, war das Wetter.* — The only thing I didn't like was the weather.
- *Das ist das Schönste, was ich je gesehen habe.* — That's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen. (*je* = ever)

Compare a real noun: *das schönste Gemälde, das ich je gesehen habe*. With a noun → *das*; without a noun → *was*.

### was for a whole clause

*was* can also refer back to everything that was said in the main clause. English uses "which" here:

- *Das Museum war am Montag geschlossen, was wir nicht wussten.* — The museum was closed on Monday, which we didn't know.
- *Die Führung dauerte drei Stunden, was für die Kinder zu lang war.* — The tour lasted three hours, which was too long for the children.
- *Am Ende hat das Publikum zehn Minuten geklatscht, worüber sich die Schauspieler sehr gefreut haben.* — In the end the audience clapped for ten minutes, which made the actors very happy.

Test: if you can't point to one noun that the clause describes, but to the whole situation, it's *was* (or a *wo(r)-* word).

## wer … (der): whoever *(receptive)*

*wer* and *was* can start a relative clause that has no antecedent at all. *wer* means "whoever, anyone who", and the main clause often picks it up again with *der*. This is an **indefinite relative pronoun**, and you know it from proverbs:

- *Wer zuerst kommt, mahlt zuerst.* — First come, first served. (Literally: whoever comes first grinds first, from the old mills.)
- *Wer A sagt, muss auch B sagen.* — In for a penny, in for a pound.
- *Wer die Altstadt kennenlernen will, (der) sollte eine Führung machen.* — Anyone who wants to get to know the old town should take a guided tour.
- *Was du heute kannst besorgen, das verschiebe nicht auf morgen.* — Don't put off till tomorrow what you can do today. (Old word order, for the rhyme; *besorgen* = to get done.)

If the two clauses need different cases, the second pronoun shows its own case: *Wer Kunst liebt, dem gefällt die Ausstellung bestimmt.* — Anyone who loves art will surely like the exhibition. You only need to understand this pattern; it is mostly found in proverbs, signs and formal advice.

## Culture and sights: the words

| German | English |
|---|---|
| *die Altstadt, der Stadtteil, die Fußgängerzone* | old town, district, pedestrian zone |
| *das Rathaus, der Marktplatz, der Brunnen* | town hall, market square, fountain |
| *der Dom, das Schloss, die Burg, der Turm* | cathedral, palace, castle, tower |
| *das Denkmal, das Gebäude, die Architektur* | monument, building, architecture |
| *die Ausstellung, die Galerie, das Gemälde, die Skulptur, das Kunstwerk* | exhibition, gallery, painting, sculpture, work of art |
| *die Oper, das Orchester, die Bühne, das Publikum* | opera, orchestra, stage, audience |
| *die Veranstaltung, das Festival, die Führung, der Eintritt* | event, festival, guided tour, admission |

Some notes:

- *das Schloss* is a palace or stately home (Schloss Neuschwanstein); *die Burg* is a castle built for defence, usually on a hill. *das Schloss* also means a lock.
- *der Dom* is a large church, usually a cathedral: *der Kölner Dom*.
- *der Eintritt* is admission or the entrance fee: *Der Eintritt ist frei.* — Admission is free. *Die Eintrittskarte* is the ticket.
- *die Führung* is a guided tour: *Um 11 Uhr gibt es eine Führung durch das Schloss.*
- *ausstellen* (to exhibit) and *veranstalten* (to organize an event): *Die Galerie stellt Fotos aus. Die Stadt veranstaltet jedes Jahr ein Festival.*
- *das Viertel*, which you know as "quarter" (*Viertel nach drei*), also means a quarter of a city: *das Studentenviertel*. *der Stadtteil* is the official word for a district.

## Text: Ein Wochenende in Kronstein

Mara writes a travel blog. Kronstein is an invented town, but there are many like it.

> Kronstein ist eine Stadt, von der viele noch nie gehört haben. Das ist schade, denn kulturell gibt es vieles, was man hier entdecken kann. Wir haben am Freitagabend im Hotel „Zum Turm“ eingecheckt, das direkt am Marktplatz liegt, wo jeden Samstag ein Bauernmarkt stattfindet.
>
> Am Samstag haben wir eine Führung durch die Altstadt gemacht. Unsere Stadtführerin hat uns alles gezeigt, was man in Kronstein gesehen haben muss: das Rathaus aus dem 16. Jahrhundert, den Dom, dessen Turm man schon von Weitem sieht, und den alten Brunnen, wo sich die Jugendlichen am Abend treffen. Das Einzige, was uns nicht so gut gefallen hat, war die Fußgängerzone: zu viele Ketten, zu wenig eigene Geschäfte.
>
> Am Nachmittag sind wir zur Burg hinaufgestiegen, von wo man einen fantastischen Blick auf das ganze Tal hat. In der Burg gibt es gerade eine Ausstellung mit Gemälden und Skulpturen junger Künstler, was wir nicht erwartet hatten. Der Eintritt war frei, und die Ausstellung ist wirklich sehenswert.
>
> Abends waren wir im Stadttheater, wo das Orchester ein Sommerkonzert gab. Das Publikum saß zum Teil draußen vor der Bühne. Das war das Schönste, was wir an diesem Wochenende erlebt haben. Wer Kultur ohne Touristenmassen sucht, der ist in Kronstein genau richtig!

In English:

> Kronstein is a town many people have never heard of. That's a pity, because culturally there's a lot you can discover here. On Friday evening we checked in to the hotel "Zum Turm", which is right on the market square, where a farmers' market takes place every Saturday.
>
> On Saturday we took a guided tour of the old town. Our guide showed us everything you have to have seen in Kronstein: the 16th-century town hall, the cathedral, whose tower you can see from far away, and the old fountain, where young people meet in the evening. The only thing we didn't like so much was the pedestrian zone: too many chain stores, too few independent shops.
>
> In the afternoon we climbed up to the castle, from where you have a fantastic view of the whole valley. There's currently an exhibition in the castle with paintings and sculptures by young artists, which we hadn't expected. Admission was free, and the exhibition is really worth seeing.
>
> In the evening we were at the town theatre, where the orchestra was giving a summer concert. Some of the audience sat outside in front of the stage. That was the most beautiful thing we experienced that weekend. If you're looking for culture without crowds of tourists, Kronstein is just right for you!

Notes: *stattfinden* = to take place; *die Kette* (a chain, here a chain store, a new sense of a word you know from jewellery); *von Weitem* = from afar; *hinaufsteigen* = to climb up; *von wo* (from where) is the usual way to say "from which" for a viewpoint; *junger Künstler* = of young artists (genitive, lesson 13); *zum Teil* = partly, some of. Find each relative word and decide why it is *wo, von wo, was, dessen* or *der/die/das*.

## Pronunciation

- *Skulptur*: skulp-TUR, long *u* at the end.
- *Publikum*: PU-bli-kum, stress on the first syllable.
- *Orchester*: or-KES-ter in Germany (in Austria and Switzerland or-CHES-ter with a soft *ch*).
- *Galerie*: ga-le-RIE, with a long *ie* at the end.
- *Architektur*: ar-chi-tek-TUR; the *ch* is the soft *ch* of *ich*.

## Common mistakes

- *das* instead of *was* after *alles, etwas, nichts* and superlatives: ~~alles, das ich weiß~~ → *alles, was ich weiß*; ~~das Beste, das ich gegessen habe~~ → *das Beste, was …*.
- *was* after a real noun: ~~das Buch, was ich lese~~ is heard in speech but wrong in writing → *das Buch, das ich lese*.
- *wo* for direction: ~~die Stadt, wo wir fahren~~ → *die Stadt, wohin wir fahren* (or *in die wir fahren*).
- *was* + preposition: ~~alles, über was wir gesprochen haben~~ → *alles, worüber wir gesprochen haben*.
- Using *das* for a whole clause: ~~Er kam zu spät, das mich geärgert hat.~~ → *…, was mich geärgert hat.*
- English "which" ≠ German *welche*: the relative pronoun is *der/die/das* or *was*, not *welcher*.

## Key points

- *wo, wohin, woher* can replace preposition + relative pronoun for places; with town and country names they are the natural choice.
- *wo* for time (*der Tag, wo …*) is spoken German; in writing use *an dem* or *als*.
- *was* after *alles, etwas, nichts, vieles, das*, after neuter superlatives (*das Beste, was*) and for a whole clause.
- With a preposition, *was* becomes a *wo(r)-* word: *alles, worüber …*; *etwas, worauf …*.
- *wer … (der)* = whoever; mostly in proverbs and formal advice. Recognize it.
