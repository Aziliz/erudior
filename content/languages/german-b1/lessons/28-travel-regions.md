## What this lesson is for

German is spoken in Germany, Austria, most of Switzerland, Liechtenstein, parts of Belgium and Italy, and Luxembourg. Each of these places has its own regions, dialects and *Spezialitäten*, and tourism is a big part of their economies. This lesson gives you the words to talk about them.

It also teaches two practical skills. The first is grammar: the little words *hin* and *her*, which tell you whether a movement goes away from the speaker or towards them (*Komm herein! Geh hinaus!*). The second is speaking: how to give a short, well-structured presentation on a topic, with an introduction, your own experience, the situation in your home country, pros and cons, and a conclusion. This is exactly what the second part of the Goethe-Zertifikat B1 speaking test asks for (lesson 30), and it's also a skill you'll use at work and in courses.

## The German-speaking regions

| country | parts | example |
|---|---|---|
| Germany | 16 *Bundesländer* (federal states) | *Bayern, Sachsen, Nordrhein-Westfalen* |
| Austria | 9 *Bundesländer* | *Tirol, Steiermark, Wien* |
| Switzerland | 26 *Kantone* (cantons) | *Zürich, Bern, Graubünden* |

*Das Bundesland* is a compound of *der Bund* (federation) and *das Land*. Switzerland is divided into *Kantone*; German is the main language in most of them, but French, Italian and Romansh are spoken in others.

The *Alpen* (Alps, plural only) cover most of Austria and much of Switzerland and reach into the far south of Germany. Germany's highest mountain, the Zugspitze, is just under 3,000 metres; the highest in Austria, the Großglockner, is almost 3,800. In a *Gebirge* (mountain range) you'll find *Gipfel* (summits), *Hütten* (mountain huts) where hikers eat and sleep, and *Seilbahnen* (cable cars) that take you up without the climb.

Every region has a *Dialekt* or *Mundart* (both mean dialect; *Mundart* sounds more traditional). In German-speaking Switzerland, almost everyone speaks Swiss German in daily life and writes standard German. In northern Germany, older people in the countryside may still speak *Plattdeutsch*. You don't need to speak a dialect, but it helps to know that people will switch to standard German for you if you ask: *Könnten Sie bitte Hochdeutsch sprechen?*

Some sentences with the words of this lesson:

- *Jedes Bundesland hat seine eigenen Ferien.* — Every federal state has its own school holidays.
- *Käsespätzle sind eine typische Spezialität aus dem Allgäu.* — Cheese noodles are a typical speciality of the Allgäu.
- *Der Tourismus ist für die Region sehr wichtig.* — Tourism is very important for the region.
- *Die Saison beginnt im Dezember und endet im April.* — The season starts in December and ends in April.
- *In der Hauptsaison sind die Hotels voll und teuer; in der Nebensaison ist es ruhiger.* — In the high season the hotels are full and expensive; in the low season it's quieter.
- *Wien ist ein beliebtes Reiseziel für Touristen und Touristinnen aus aller Welt.* — Vienna is a popular destination for tourists from all over the world.
- *Im Restaurant gibt es nur regionale Produkte.* — The restaurant only has regional produce.

## hin and her: away and towards

German marks the direction of a movement with two small words:

- *hin* = away from the speaker, "there"
- *her* = towards the speaker, "here"

You know them already from *wohin?* (where to?) and *woher?* (where from?). In speech the question word is often split: *Wo gehst du hin?* — Where are you going? *Wo kommst du her?* — Where do you come from?

These words combine with prepositions to make **directional adverbs**, which are often used as separable prefixes:

| away from the speaker | towards the speaker | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *hinauf* | *herauf* | up |
| *hinunter* | *herunter* | down |
| *hinein* | *herein* | in |
| *hinaus* | *heraus* | out |
| *hinüber* | *herüber* | over, across |

The choice depends on where the speaker is:

- You're at the bottom of the stairs, your friend is at the top. You call up: *Ich komme gleich hinauf!* — I'm coming up in a second! Your friend calls down: *Komm herauf!* — Come up (here)!
- Someone knocks on your door: *Herein!* — Come in! You're inside, so the movement is towards you.
- A sign on an old train window: *Bitte nicht hinauslehnen.* — Please don't lean out.
- *Kannst du die Kiste bitte herunterbringen? Ich bin unten im Keller.* — Can you bring the box down, please? I'm down in the cellar.
- *Die Kinder laufen in den Garten hinaus.* — The children run out into the garden (away from me, inside the house).
- *Wir sind mit der Seilbahn hinaufgefahren und zu Fuß heruntergekommen.* — We went up by cable car and came down on foot (said by someone now at the bottom).

### Spoken short forms

In everyday speech, Germans don't think about *hin* and *her* very much. They say *rauf, runter, rein, raus, rüber*, which come from the *her-* forms but are used for both directions:

- *Ich geh mal kurz raus.* — I'm just popping out.
- *Kommst du rein?* — Are you coming in?
- *Wir fahren morgen auf den Berg rauf.* — We're going up the mountain tomorrow.

In writing and in the exam, use the full forms when the direction is clear; in conversation the short forms are completely natural.

### Fixed expressions

Some uses have become idioms: *Wo ist mein Handy hin?* (Where has my phone got to?), *Wo hast du das her?* (Where did you get that from?), *hin und her* (back and forth), and *hin und zurück* (there and back, return ticket). And one you'll need for presentations: *Ich möchte darauf hinweisen, dass …* — I'd like to point out that …

## Giving a short presentation

In a short presentation (*eine Präsentation* or *ein kurzer Vortrag*) you speak for about three minutes about a question, such as *Sollte man im Urlaub lieber in die Berge oder ans Meer fahren?* A clear structure makes it easier for you and for your listeners. In a course or at work you'd have slides (*Folien*); in the exam you get five "slides" printed on paper, one for each part below. Each part has its typical phrases.

### 1. Introduction (die Einleitung)

- *In meiner Präsentation geht es um das Thema …* — My presentation is about …
- *Ich möchte heute über … sprechen.* — Today I'd like to talk about …
- *Zuerst erzähle ich von meinen eigenen Erfahrungen, danach …* — First I'll talk about my own experiences, then …

### 2. Your own experience

- *Ich habe selbst erlebt, dass …* — I have experienced myself that …
- *Als ich in … war, habe ich …* — When I was in …, I …
- *Ich persönlich …* — Personally, I …

### 3. The situation in your home country

- *In meinem Heimatland ist das ganz anders / ähnlich.* — In my home country it's quite different / similar.
- *Bei uns in … ist es üblich, dass …* — Where I come from, it's usual that …
- *Viele Leute in … finden, dass …* — Many people in … think that …

### 4. Pros and cons, and your opinion

- *Ein Vorteil ist, dass … Ein Nachteil ist, dass …* — One advantage is that … One disadvantage is that …
- *Einerseits …, andererseits …* — On the one hand …, on the other hand …
- *Erstens …, zweitens …, außerdem …* — Firstly …, secondly …, also …
- *Meiner Meinung nach … / Ich bin der Meinung, dass …* — In my opinion …

### 5. Conclusion (der Schluss)

- *Abschließend möchte ich sagen, dass …* — To conclude, I'd like to say that …
- *Zusammenfassend kann man sagen: …* — In summary, you can say: …
- *Vielen Dank fürs Zuhören. Haben Sie noch Fragen?* — Thank you for listening. Do you have any questions?

*einerseits … andererseits* work like *deshalb* or *trotzdem*: when they start the clause, they fill position 1 and the verb comes right after: *Einerseits ist die Hauptsaison teuer, andererseits ist das Wetter dann am besten.*

## A model presentation

Ana is from Portugal and lives in Munich. Her course asked her to *präsentieren* a short talk on the question: *Urlaub in der Hauptsaison oder in der Nebensaison?*

> Guten Tag! In meiner Präsentation geht es um die Frage, ob man lieber in der Hauptsaison oder in der Nebensaison Urlaub machen sollte. Zuerst erzähle ich von meinen Erfahrungen, dann spreche ich über die Situation in Portugal, und am Ende nenne ich Vor- und Nachteile.
>
> Letzten Sommer bin ich im August in die Alpen gefahren. Das war ein Fehler: Auf jeder Hütte waren Hunderte von Touristen, und an der Seilbahn haben wir eine Stunde gewartet, bis wir hinauffahren konnten. Im Oktober war ich dann noch einmal in derselben Region. Es war ruhig, die Wirtin auf der Hütte hatte Zeit für uns, und der Blick vom Gipfel war fantastisch.
>
> In meinem Heimatland ist der Tourismus sehr wichtig, vor allem an der Küste im Süden. Im Sommer kommen so viele Urlauber, dass die Einheimischen manchmal kaum noch einen Parkplatz finden. Deshalb wirbt die Region inzwischen auch für den Winter.
>
> Einerseits hat die Nebensaison viele Vorteile: Es ist billiger, ruhiger, und man lernt die Leute besser kennen. Andererseits ist das Wetter nicht so sicher, und manche Restaurants und Museen sind geschlossen. Für Familien ist es außerdem schwierig, weil die Kinder nur in den Ferien freihaben.
>
> Abschließend möchte ich sagen: Wer flexibel ist, sollte in der Nebensaison reisen. Das ist besser für die Reisenden und für die Menschen, die dort wohnen. Vielen Dank fürs Zuhören!

Translation:

> Hello! My presentation is about whether it's better to go on holiday in the high season or the low season. First I'll tell you about my experiences, then I'll talk about the situation in Portugal, and at the end I'll give some pros and cons.
>
> Last summer I went to the Alps in August. That was a mistake: there were hundreds of tourists at every mountain hut, and at the cable car we waited for an hour before we could go up. Then in October I went to the same region again. It was quiet, the landlady at the hut had time for us, and the view from the summit was fantastic.
>
> In my home country, tourism is very important, especially on the coast in the south. In summer so many holidaymakers come that the locals sometimes can hardly find a parking space. That's why the region now advertises the winter too.
>
> On the one hand, the low season has many advantages: it's cheaper and quieter, and you get to know the people better. On the other hand, the weather is less reliable, and some restaurants and museums are closed. It's also difficult for families, because the children only have time off in the school holidays.
>
> To conclude, I'd like to say: if you're flexible, you should travel in the low season. That's better for the travellers and for the people who live there. Thank you for listening!

Notes: *dieselbe Region* (the same region) is written as one word. *Hunderte von Touristen* is "hundreds of tourists". *die Einheimischen* are the locals (an adjectival noun, lesson 5). *werben für* is to advertise, promote. Notice how each paragraph matches one of the five parts.

## Pronunciation

- *Tourismus*: tu-RIS-mus; *Tourist*: tu-RIST, stress on the last syllable.
- *Saison*: sä-ZONG, or with a French nasal ending, in Germany and Switzerland; in Austria (and parts of south-east Germany) it's se-SOHN, with a sharp *s* and a plain *n* (plural there: *Saisonen*).
- *Spezialität*: shpe-tsi-a-li-TÄT, stress on the last syllable, like all nouns in *-ität*.
- *hinein, heraus*: stress on the second part: hi-NEIN, he-RAUS.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up *hin* and *her*: you're in the room and want someone to come in: ~~Komm hinein!~~ → *Komm herein!*
- Using *Bundesland* for Switzerland: the Swiss have *Kantone*.
- Forgetting the verb after *einerseits*: ~~Einerseits es ist teuer.~~ → *Einerseits ist es teuer.*
- Reading a written text aloud in a presentation. Use short notes and speak freely; that's what listeners and examiners expect.
- Leaving out the conclusion. Even one sentence (*Abschließend möchte ich sagen, …*) makes the talk sound finished.
- *die Alpen* is plural only: ~~die Alpe~~ (that's a mountain pasture in some regions).

## Key points

- Germany and Austria have *Bundesländer*, Switzerland has *Kantone*.
- *hin* = away from the speaker, *her* = towards the speaker: *hinauf/herauf, hinein/herein, hinaus/heraus*. In speech: *rauf, rein, raus, runter*.
- A short presentation has five parts: *Einleitung*, own experience, situation in your home country, *Vor- und Nachteile* with your opinion, *Schluss*.
- *einerseits … andererseits*, *erstens … zweitens*, *abschließend*: verb right after them.
