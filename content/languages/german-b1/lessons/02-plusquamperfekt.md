## What this lesson is for

In the last lesson's story, the manuscript *war verschwunden* and the baker *hatte* it *gestohlen*. Those forms take you one step further back: they describe something that happened *before* another past event. This is the **past perfect** (*Plusquamperfekt*), English "had done".

The past perfect matters most with *nachdem* (after), which almost always needs it, and it makes your stories clearer: the reader knows what happened first without extra words. This lesson also covers *bevor* (before), the rules for which tense goes with which, and the time adverbs *vorher, zuvor* and *anschließend*. The theme is history: wars, the Berlin Wall, the *Wende* of 1989 and the people who lived through it.

## The form: hatte or war + participle

The past perfect is built exactly like the perfect, but the helper verb is in the simple past: *hatte* instead of *hat*, *war* instead of *ist*. Anything that takes *haben* in the perfect takes *hatte* here; anything with *sein* takes *war*.

| | with haben | with sein |
|---|---|---|
| ich | hatte … gesehen | war … geflohen |
| du | hattest … gesehen | warst … geflohen |
| er / sie / es | hatte … gesehen | war … geflohen |
| wir | hatten … gesehen | waren … geflohen |
| ihr | hattet … gesehen | wart … geflohen |
| sie / Sie | hatten … gesehen | waren … geflohen |

- perfect: *Die Familie ist 1961 nach Westberlin geflohen.*
- past perfect: *Die Familie war 1961 nach Westberlin geflohen.* — The family had fled to West Berlin in 1961.
- perfect: *Der Krieg hat die Stadt zerstört.*
- past perfect: *Der Krieg hatte die Stadt zerstört.* — The war had destroyed the city.

Word order is the same as in the perfect: the helper in position 2, the participle at the end (the sentence bracket); in a subordinate clause the helper goes to the very end: *…, weil der Krieg die Stadt zerstört hatte.*

There is nothing new to learn about participles. If you know *ist geflohen*, you know *war geflohen*.

## When to use it

Use the past perfect for an event that was already finished *before* the time you are talking about in the past. Think of two points on a timeline: your story is at point B (simple past or perfect), and you look back to point A (past perfect).

- *Als wir am Bahnhof ankamen, war der Zug schon abgefahren.* — When we got to the station, the train had already left. (The train left first.)
- *Sie konnte nicht mitkommen. Sie hatte sich das Bein gebrochen.* — She couldn't come. She had broken her leg.
- *Die Stadt sah 1950 ganz anders aus. Bomben hatten fast alle alten Häuser zerstört.* — The city looked completely different in 1950. Bombs had destroyed almost all the old houses.

Do not use it just because something is "long ago". A whole story set in 1945 is told in the simple past. You only switch to the past perfect when you step back from your story's time to something earlier. This is the **sequence of tenses** in a narrative:

| timeline | tense | example |
|---|---|---|
| now | present | *Heute ist Berlin wieder eine Stadt.* |
| the story's time | simple past (writing) / perfect (speech) | *1989 fiel die Mauer.* |
| before the story's time | past perfect | *Sie hatte die Stadt 28 Jahre lang geteilt.* |

In speech, Germans are relaxed about this; you will hear the perfect where a book would use the past perfect. In writing, and especially with *nachdem*, keep the rule.

## nachdem: after (+ past perfect)

*nachdem* is a **subordinating conjunction** like *weil* and *dass*: the verb goes to the end. Its clause describes the earlier action, so its tense is one step further back than the main clause:

| *nachdem* clause | main clause |
|---|---|
| past perfect | simple past or perfect |
| perfect | present (or future) |

- *Nachdem die Mauer gefallen war, reisten viele Ostdeutsche zum ersten Mal in den Westen.* — After the Wall had fallen, many East Germans travelled to the West for the first time.
- *Nachdem er das Abitur gemacht hatte, begann er eine Ausbildung.* — After he had done his school-leaving exam, he started an apprenticeship.
- *Nachdem ich gegessen habe, mache ich einen Spaziergang.* — After I've eaten, I go for a walk. (a habit in the present)

When the *nachdem* clause comes first, it fills position 1, so the main verb follows immediately: *Nachdem …, reisten viele …* (verb, comma, verb).

Two warnings:

- *nachdem* is a conjunction (it introduces a clause with a verb). *nach* is a preposition (it is followed by a noun): *nach dem Krieg* (after the war) but *nachdem der Krieg zu Ende war*.
- *danach* and *anschließend* are adverbs meaning "after that, afterwards". They do not join two clauses; they stand in a main clause and push the verb into position 2: *Er machte das Abitur. Danach begann er eine Ausbildung.*

## bevor: before

*bevor* is the opposite: its clause describes the *later* action. Because "later" needs no special tense, *bevor* simply takes the same tense as the main clause.

- *Bevor die Mauer gebaut wurde, konnte man frei von Ost- nach Westberlin gehen.* — Before the Wall was built, you could walk freely from East to West Berlin.
- *Mein Großvater floh, bevor der Krieg zu Ende war.* — My grandfather fled before the war was over.
- *Bevor du gehst, mach bitte das Licht aus.* — Before you go, please turn off the light.

Again: *bevor* is the conjunction; *vor* is the preposition (*vor dem Krieg*, before the war); *vorher* and *zuvor* are adverbs (beforehand, previously).

## Time adverbs: vorher, zuvor, anschließend

These adverbs place an action on the timeline without a second clause. They go in position 1 or in the middle of the sentence.

| adverb | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| *vorher* | before, beforehand | *Wir besuchten das Museum. Vorher hatten wir einen Film über die Mauer gesehen.* |
| *zuvor* | before, previously (more formal) | *Zwei Jahre zuvor hatte sie ihr Studium beendet.* |
| *anschließend* | afterwards, then | *Erst gab es eine Rede, anschließend spielte eine Band.* |
| *danach* | after that | *Danach wurde es still.* |
| *damals* | back then, at that time | *Damals gab es zwei deutsche Staaten.* |

*zuvor* often follows a time expression: *ein Jahr zuvor* (a year earlier), *kurz zuvor* (shortly before), *wie nie zuvor* (as never before). The adjective *damalig* (of that time) goes before a noun: *die damalige Regierung* — the government of the time.

## Talking about history

Some words you need to describe the past of a country. Notice how often the past perfect appears when a history text explains the background:

- *Das 20. Jahrhundert war eine Epoche der Kriege und Revolutionen.* — The 20th century was an era of wars and revolutions.
- *Nach dem Zweiten Weltkrieg bauten die Menschen die zerstörten Städte wieder auf.* — After the Second World War people rebuilt the destroyed cities.
- *Viele Soldaten und noch mehr Zivilisten hatten den Krieg nicht überlebt.* — Many soldiers and even more civilians had not survived the war.
- *Die Teilung Deutschlands dauerte vier Jahrzehnte.* — The division of Germany lasted four decades.
- *Zeitzeugen berichten, wie die Diktatur ihren Alltag kontrolliert hatte.* — Contemporary witnesses report how the dictatorship had controlled their everyday life.
- *Nach der Flucht lebten viele Flüchtlinge jahrelang in Lagern.* — After the escape many refugees lived in camps for years.
- *Am 3. Oktober feiert Deutschland den Tag der Deutschen Einheit, den Jahrestag der Wiedervereinigung.* — On 3 October Germany celebrates the Day of German Unity, the anniversary of reunification.
- *Jede Generation erinnert sich anders an diese Zeit.* — Every generation remembers this time differently.

*aufbauen* is separable (*sie bauten auf*); *zerstören* and *überleben* are inseparable, so their participles have no *ge-*: *zerstört, überlebt*. *fliehen* is strong and takes *sein*: *floh, ist geflohen*. *die Folge* means "consequence" and also "episode" of a TV series; *zur Folge haben* = to result in.

## Text: Ein Zeitzeuge erzählt

A school class interviewed a man who grew up in East Berlin. Here is part of the article they wrote for the school website.

> Herr Brandt wurde 1958 in Ostberlin geboren. Drei Jahre später baute die Regierung der DDR die Mauer. Seine Tante war kurz zuvor mit ihren Kindern in den Westen geflohen, und die Familie sah sie fast drei Jahrzehnte lang nicht mehr. „Für mich war die Mauer einfach normal“, erzählte er uns. „Ich kannte die Zeit davor ja nicht.“
>
> Nachdem er die Schule beendet hatte, wurde er Elektriker. Er hatte eine Wohnung, eine Arbeit und Freunde, aber keine Freiheit. Reisen in den Westen waren verboten, und wer die Regierung kritisierte, bekam Probleme. 1989 änderte sich alles. Im Sommer waren schon Tausende über Ungarn geflohen, im Herbst gingen in Leipzig und Berlin immer mehr Menschen auf die Straße. Am Abend des 9. November hörte Herr Brandt im Radio, dass die Grenze offen war. „Zuerst dachte ich, das ist ein Witz“, sagte er. Anschließend lief er mit seinen Nachbarn zum Grenzübergang. Um Mitternacht stand er zum ersten Mal in Westberlin, und seine Tante, die er seit 28 Jahren nicht gesehen hatte, wartete schon auf ihn.
>
> Die Wende hatte für ihn aber auch Folgen, die er vorher nicht erwartet hatte: 1991 verlor er seine Stelle. „Die Freiheit war das Wichtigste“, sagt er heute. „Aber leicht war die Zeit danach nicht.“

Translation:

> Mr Brandt was born in East Berlin in 1958. Three years later the GDR government built the Wall. His aunt had fled to the West with her children shortly before, and the family didn't see her again for almost three decades. "For me the Wall was just normal," he told us. "After all, I didn't know the time before it."
>
> After he had finished school, he became an electrician. He had a flat, a job and friends, but no freedom. Travel to the West was forbidden, and anyone who criticised the government got into trouble. In 1989 everything changed. In the summer thousands had already fled via Hungary, and in the autumn more and more people took to the streets in Leipzig and Berlin. On the evening of 9 November Mr Brandt heard on the radio that the border was open. "At first I thought it was a joke," he said. Then he ran with his neighbours to the border crossing. At midnight he stood in West Berlin for the first time, and his aunt, whom he had not seen for 28 years, was already waiting for him.
>
> But for him the Wende also had consequences that he had not expected beforehand: in 1991 he lost his job. "Freedom was the most important thing," he says today. "But the time afterwards wasn't easy."

Notes: the main story is in the simple past (*baute, sah, wurde, änderte sich, hörte, lief, stand, verlor*). Each past perfect steps back to something earlier: the aunt *had* fled before the Wall, he *had* finished school before becoming an electrician, thousands *had* fled before the autumn protests. The class quotes him in the present tense where he speaks today. *die DDR* (Deutsche Demokratische Republik) = the GDR, East Germany; *der Grenzübergang* = border crossing; *der Witz* = joke; *auf die Straße gehen* = to protest, to take to the streets; *die Wende* = the turning point, the name Germans give to the changes of 1989–90 that led to reunification in 1990.

## Pronunciation

- *das Jahrhundert*: JAHR-hun-dert; *das Jahrzehnt*: jahr-ZEHNT, stress on the second part.
- *die Epoche*: e-PO-che, with *ch* as in *ach*.
- *die Revolution*: re-vo-lu-TSION; *-tion* is always [tsjoːn].
- *fliehen, floh, geflohen*: the *h* is silent; *floh* has a long *o*.
- *der Flüchtling*: FLÜCHT-ling, a short *ü*.

## Common mistakes

- Using *haben* with a *sein* verb: ~~Er hatte geflohen~~ → *Er war geflohen.* The choice of helper is the same as in the perfect.
- Using the past perfect for everything old: ~~1945 hatte der Krieg geendet~~ in the middle of a story about 1945 → *1945 endete der Krieg.* Use it only to look back from the story's time.
- The same tense in *nachdem* and the main clause: ~~Nachdem er aß, ging er~~ → *Nachdem er gegessen hatte, ging er.*
- Mixing up *nachdem* and *nach*: ~~Nachdem dem Krieg~~ → *Nach dem Krieg* (noun) or *Nachdem der Krieg vorbei war* (clause).
- Verb position after *danach* and *anschließend*: they are adverbs, not conjunctions, so the verb comes straight after them: ~~Danach er ging nach Hause~~ → *Danach ging er nach Hause.*
- Translating "before" with *vor* + clause: ~~Vor ich gehe …~~ → *Bevor ich gehe …*

## Key points

- Past perfect = *hatte* / *war* + participle: *ich hatte gesehen, sie war geflohen*. Same helper as in the perfect.
- It describes an action before another past action; the story itself stays in the simple past.
- *nachdem* + past perfect, main clause in the simple past or perfect. *nachdem* + perfect, main clause in the present.
- *bevor* takes the same tense as the main clause.
- Conjunctions *nachdem, bevor* (verb at the end) vs prepositions *nach, vor* (+ noun) vs adverbs *danach, anschließend, vorher, zuvor* (verb in position 2).
