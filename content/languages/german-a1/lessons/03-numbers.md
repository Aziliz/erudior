## What this lesson is for

You can now say who you are and where you're from. The next things people ask are how old you
are, your phone number and your address, and the answers are all numbers. This lesson teaches
the numbers from 0 to 100, the one big surprise in how German says them, and how to give your
age, phone number, postcode and e-mail address. You'll also meet a second key verb, *haben*
(to have).

## 0 to 12

These have to be learned one by one, as in English.

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 *null* | 1 *eins* | 2 *zwei* | 3 *drei* |
| 4 *vier* | 5 *fünf* | 6 *sechs* | 7 *sieben* |
| 8 *acht* | 9 *neun* | 10 *zehn* | 11 *elf* |
| 12 *zwölf* | | | |

Watch the sounds from Lesson 1: *zwei* and *zehn* start with "ts", *vier* starts with "f",
*neun* rhymes with "coin", and *sechs* sounds like "zex". *elf* looks
like the English creature, but it's eleven.

On the phone and in announcements, people often say *zwo* instead of *zwei*, so it can't be
confused with *drei*. You'll hear *zwo* at train stations and in phone numbers; both are correct.

## 13 to 19

Like English "-teen", German adds *-zehn* (ten) to the unit:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 13 *dreizehn* | 14 *vierzehn* | 15 *fünfzehn* |
| 16 *sechzehn* | 17 *siebzehn* | 18 *achtzehn* |
| 19 *neunzehn* | | |

Two spelling traps: *sechzehn* drops the *s* of *sechs*, and *siebzehn* drops the *-en* of
*sieben*.

## The tens

The tens end in *-zig*, which is pronounced like "-tsich" with the soft *ch* of *ich*. (In
southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland you'll hear "-tsik".)

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 20 *zwanzig* | 30 *dreißig* | 40 *vierzig* |
| 50 *fünfzig* | 60 *sechzig* | 70 *siebzig* |
| 80 *achtzig* | 90 *neunzig* | 100 *hundert* |

*dreißig* is the odd one: it ends in *-ßig*, not *-zig*. *sechzig* and *siebzig* lose letters,
just like 16 and 17. *hundert* can also be *einhundert*.

## 21 to 99: the units come first

This is the big difference from English. German says the unit first, then *und* (and), then
the ten, all as one word:

| Number | German | Literally |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | *einundzwanzig* | one-and-twenty |
| 34 | *vierunddreißig* | four-and-thirty |
| 47 | *siebenundvierzig* | seven-and-forty |
| 58 | *achtundfünfzig* | eight-and-fifty |
| 66 | *sechsundsechzig* | six-and-sixty |
| 99 | *neunundneunzig* | nine-and-ninety |

Memory hook: think of the old English nursery rhyme "four-and-twenty blackbirds". German
always counts this way. When you hear a number, hold the first part in your head until the
ten arrives: *dreiundsiebzig* — you hear 3, then 70, and you write 73.

![The number 73 with two crossing arrows: the 3 is read first as drei, then comes und, then the 7 is read as siebzig, giving dreiundsiebzig as one word.](images/de1-m1-number-73.svg)

Practise by reading numbers off
car number plates or prices until you stop reversing them.

*eins* becomes *ein* inside a longer number: *einundzwanzig*, *einunddreißig*. On its own, or
at the end, it's *eins*: *hunderteins* (101). And *sechs* keeps its *s* in *sechsundzwanzig*,
*sechsundsechzig*; only 16 and 60 drop it.

## How old are you?

*alt* means "old". To ask someone's age:

*Wie alt bist du?* — How old are you? (informal)
*Wie alt sind Sie?* — How old are you? (formal)
*Ich bin 29 Jahre alt.* — I'm 29 years old.
*Ich bin 29.* — I'm 29.

*das Jahr* is "the year", and its plural is *die Jahre*: *ein Jahr*, *zwei Jahre*. German
uses *sein* (to be) for age, just like English, so *Ich habe 29 Jahre* is wrong. The opposite
of *alt* is *jung* (young): *Das Kind ist sehr jung.* (The child is very young.)

## The verb haben (to have)

*haben* is the second most important German verb. Like *sein*, it's slightly irregular:

| | haben |
|---|---|
| ich | *habe* |
| du | *hast* |
| Sie | *haben* |
| er / sie | *hat* |

The *du* and *er/sie* forms drop the *b*: *du hast*, *er hat*. You'll see *haben* in full
in Lesson 6.

*Hast du eine Telefonnummer?* — Do you have a phone number?
*Ich habe eine Handynummer.* — I have a mobile number.
*Haben Sie eine E-Mail-Adresse?* — Do you have an e-mail address?

(*eine* means "a" here; more on that in Lesson 5.)

## Phone numbers

*die Telefonnummer* is a phone number in general; *die Handynummer* is a mobile number (*das
Handy* is the German word for a mobile phone). *die Nummer* is a number used as a label, like
a phone, room or house number; *die Zahl* is a number as a quantity or figure: *Sieben ist eine
Zahl.*

Germans usually say phone numbers digit by digit, or in pairs: 0176 48 25 93 can be *null eins
sieben sechs, achtundvierzig, fünfundzwanzig, dreiundneunzig*. Asking someone to repeat is
normal: *Noch einmal, bitte. Langsam, bitte.*

*Wie ist deine Telefonnummer?* — What's your phone number? (informal)
*Wie ist Ihre Handynummer?* — What's your mobile number? (formal)
*Meine Nummer ist 0151 23 40 17.* — My number is 0151 23 40 17.

*deine*, *Ihre* and *meine* mean "your" and "my"; Lesson 6 explains them.

## Addresses and e-mail

A German address puts the street first and the house number after it, then the postcode
before the town:

> Lisa Krämer
>
> Lindenstraße 14
>
> 04107 Leipzig

*die Adresse* is the address, *die Hausnummer* the house number, *die Postleitzahl* the postcode
(often shortened to *PLZ*). German postcodes have five digits; Austrian and Swiss ones have four.

On an envelope, the parts of the address sit like this (*die Straße* is the street; you'll
learn the word properly in Lesson 17):

![An envelope with a stamp and a small sender line at the top. The address reads Lisa Krämer, Lindenstraße 14, 04107 Leipzig. Labels point to Lisa (Vorname), Krämer (Familienname), Lindenstraße (Straße), 14 (Hausnummer), 04107 (Postleitzahl) and Leipzig (Stadt).](images/de1-m1-envelope.svg)

*Wie ist Ihre Adresse? — Lindenstraße 14, 04107 Leipzig.*
*Die Hausnummer ist vierzehn.* — The house number is fourteen.
*Die Postleitzahl ist null-vier-eins-null-sieben.* — The postcode is 04107.

For an e-mail address, @ is said like English "at", the dot is *Punkt*, and the hyphen is
*Bindestrich*. *die E-Mail* is both "the e-mail" (the message) and,
in everyday speech, short for *die E-Mail-Adresse*.

*Meine E-Mail ist lisa.kraemer@post.de.* — "lisa Punkt kraemer at post Punkt de"

Notice the address uses *ae* for *ä*, because many e-mail addresses can't contain umlauts.

## Dialogue: signing up at a gym

> Mitarbeiter: Guten Tag! Wie ist Ihr Name, bitte?
>
> Frau Okafor: Okafor, Grace Okafor.
>
> Mitarbeiter: Und wie alt sind Sie, Frau Okafor?
>
> Frau Okafor: Ich bin 34 Jahre alt.
>
> Mitarbeiter: Wie ist Ihre Adresse?
>
> Frau Okafor: Bergstraße 72, 50667 Köln.
>
> Mitarbeiter: Zweiundsiebzig oder siebenundzwanzig?
>
> Frau Okafor: Zweiundsiebzig. Sieben, zwei.
>
> Mitarbeiter: Danke. Haben Sie eine Handynummer?
>
> Frau Okafor: Ja, die Nummer ist 0172 58 39 61.

Hello! What's your name, please? — Okafor, Grace Okafor. — And how old are you, Ms Okafor? —
I'm 34 years old. — What's your address? — Bergstraße 72, 50667 Cologne. — Seventy-two or
twenty-seven? — Seventy-two. Seven, two. — Thank you. Do you have a mobile number? — Yes, the
number is 0172 58 39 61.

(*der Mitarbeiter* is the employee; *oder* means "or".) The question "72 or 27?" is exactly the
confusion German numbers cause, even among Germans: saying the digits separately settles it.

## Pronunciation

- *zwanzig*, *dreißig*, *vierzig*: stress on the first syllable; *-ig* like *ich*.
- *dreißig*: *ei* as in "eye", then a sharp *s*: "DRY-sich".
- *sieben*: "ZEE-ben" (*s* before a vowel is z; *ie* is ee).
- *fünf*: round your lips for *ü*; don't say "funf".
- *zwölf*: *ö* with rounded lips, *z* = ts: "tsvölf".
- *Postleitzahl*: *st* in the middle of a word is a plain "st"; stress on *POST*.
- *achtzig*: the *ch* is the throaty one, as in *Nacht*.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing two-digit numbers: writing 52 for *fünfundzwanzig* (25). Units first, then tens.
- Writing *dreizig* or *dreissig*. It's *dreißig* (or *dreissig* only in Switzerland).
- Writing *sechszehn*, *siebenzehn*, *sechszig*, *siebenzig*. Correct: *sechzehn*, *siebzehn*,
  *sechzig*, *siebzig*.
- *eins und zwanzig* as separate words. Numbers under a million are one word: *einundzwanzig*.
- *Ich habe 30 Jahre.* German uses *sein* for age: *Ich bin 30 Jahre alt.*
- *du habst*, *er habt*. The forms are *du hast*, *er hat*.

## Key points

- Learn 0–12 by heart; 13–19 add *-zehn*; tens end in *-zig*, except *dreißig*.
- *sechzehn, sechzig, siebzehn, siebzig* drop letters.
- 21–99: unit + *und* + ten, written as one word: *vierundfünfzig* = 54.
- *zwo* is an alternative to *zwei*, common on the phone.
- Age: *Wie alt bist du? Ich bin … Jahre alt.*
- *haben*: *ich habe, du hast, Sie haben, er/sie hat*.
- Addresses: street + number, then postcode + town.
