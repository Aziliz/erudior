## What this lesson is for

In the last lesson you talked about what you like doing. Now you learn to say what you can
do, what you want to do and what you would like to do: *Ich kann schwimmen*, *Ich will
Gitarre lernen*, *Ich möchte eine Party feiern*. These sentences need a new kind of verb and a
new word order, and both are central to German. Once you have them, you can also make plans
and invitations.

## What a modal verb does

A **modal verb** adds an attitude to another verb: ability (can), wish (want, would like),
obligation (must) or permission (may). In English: "I *can* swim", "I *want to* travel". In
German it works the same way, with one big difference in word order:

- *Ich kann schwimmen.* — I can swim.
- *Ich will reisen.* — I want to travel.
- *Ich möchte tanzen.* — I'd like to dance.

The modal verb is conjugated and sits in position 2. The second verb stays in the infinitive
(the form ending in -en, as in the dictionary) and goes to the very end of the sentence. There
is no "zu" (no "to") in front of it.

This lesson covers *können*, *wollen*, *mögen* and *möchten*. Lesson 15 adds *müssen*,
*dürfen* and *sollen*.

## The forms

Modal verbs are irregular in the singular. Three things are true of all of them:

1. The ich and er/sie/es forms are the same and have no ending: *ich kann, er kann*.
2. Most of them change their vowel in the singular: *können → ich kann*.
3. The plural is regular, built from the infinitive: *wir können, ihr könnt, sie können*.

| | können (can) | wollen (want) | mögen (like) | möchten (would like) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ich | kann | will | mag | möchte |
| du | kannst | willst | magst | möchtest |
| er/sie/es | kann | will | mag | möchte |
| wir | können | wollen | mögen | möchten |
| ihr | könnt | wollt | mögt | möchtet |
| sie/Sie | können | wollen | mögen | möchten |

You know *möchte* from ordering food in lesson 9 (*Ich möchte einen Kaffee*). It is really a
polite form of *mögen*, but at A1 treat it as its own verb. Note that its er form is *er möchte*,
with no -t.

Memory hook: *ich* and *er* are twins with modal verbs. If you remember *ich kann*, you
already know *er kann*.

## The sentence bracket

German puts the two parts of the verb at two fixed points and fills the middle with
everything else. This is called the **sentence bracket**: the modal verb opens it in
position 2, the infinitive closes it at the end.

| Position 1 | Position 2 (modal) | middle | end (infinitive) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ich | kann | gut | kochen. |
| Mein Bruder | will | am Samstag eine Party | feiern. |
| Am Sonntag | möchten | wir | wandern. |
| Was | kannst | du | spielen? |

In English the two verbs stay together ("I can cook well"). In German they separate: *Ich
kann gut kochen*, never "Ich kann kochen gut".

Yes/no questions start with the modal; the infinitive is still at the end:

- *Kannst du Ski fahren?* — Can you ski?
- *Willst du am Wochenende Freunde treffen?* — Do you want to meet friends at the weekend?
- *Möchten Sie etwas trinken?* — Would you like something to drink?

*Nicht* usually goes just before the infinitive: *Ich kann heute nicht kommen.* — I can't come
today.

You already know a bracket from lesson 12: *Ich stehe um sieben auf.* With a modal verb, a
separable verb comes back together as one word at the end: *Ich will um sieben aufstehen.*
*Kommst du mit?* becomes *Willst du mitkommen?* And *einladen* (to invite): *Ich möchte
meine Freunde einladen.*

## können: ability and possibility

*Können* says that you are able to do something, or that something is possible:

- *Ich kann ein bisschen Gitarre spielen.* — I can play the guitar a bit.
- *Kannst du gut kochen?* — Can you cook well? / Are you a good cook?
- *Meine Schwester kann Klavier spielen, aber sie kann nicht singen.* — My sister can play the
  piano, but she can't sing.
- *Kannst du am Freitag?* — Can you (make it) on Friday?
- *Wir können zusammen Auto fahren.* — We can drive together.

For languages, German often leaves out the second verb: *Ich kann Deutsch* — I can speak
German. *Kannst du Englisch?* — Do you speak English?

To say how well, use *gut, sehr gut, ein bisschen, nicht so gut, nicht*, and put it before the
infinitive (or before the noun that goes with it): *Ich kann sehr gut schwimmen.*

## wollen and möchten: wishes and plans

Both express a wish, but they feel different:

- *möchte* is polite and soft: "would like". Use it with strangers, in shops, and whenever you
  ask for something.
- *will* is direct and firm: "want". Fine for plans and for talking about yourself with
  friends, but *Ich will einen Kaffee* to a waiter sounds pushy, like a child saying "I want…".

Examples:

- *Ich will im Sommer nach Spanien reisen.* — I want to travel to Spain in the summer.
- *Was willst du heute machen?* — What do you want to do today?
- *Ich möchte bitte zwei Brötchen.* — I'd like two rolls, please.

False friend warning: *Ich will* does not mean "I will". It means "I want". The English future
"I will" is a different verb that you meet after A1.

## mögen: liking things and people

*Mögen* mostly takes a noun, not an infinitive: *Ich mag Pizza.* — I like pizza. *Magst du
Jazz?* — Do you like jazz? *Ich mag meine Kollegen.* — I like my colleagues. For liking
activities, keep using *gern* from lesson 13: *Ich tanze gern* (I like dancing).

## Useful little words for plans

| German | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *natürlich* | of course | *Natürlich kann ich helfen.* — Of course I can help. |
| *vielleicht* | maybe | *Vielleicht komme ich später.* — Maybe I'll come later. |
| *leider* | unfortunately | *Ich kann leider nicht.* — Unfortunately I can't. |
| *schon* | already | *Kannst du schon Auto fahren?* — Can you drive already? |
| *noch* | still | *Ich lerne noch.* — I'm still learning. |
| *noch nicht* | not yet | *Ich kann noch nicht Ski fahren.* — I can't ski yet. |
| *gleich* | right away, in a moment | *Ich komme gleich!* — I'm coming right away! |
| *wirklich* | really | *Das ist wirklich toll.* — That's really great. |
| *sicher* | sure, certainly | *Sie kommt sicher.* — She's sure to come. |

Two useful nouns: *die Lust* (desire, being in the mood) and *der Spaß* (fun).

- *Hast du Lust auf eine Party?* — Do you feel like a party? (*auf* is part of the chunk.)
- *Ich habe keine Lust.* — I don't feel like it.
- *Tanzen macht Spaß.* — Dancing is fun.

## Dialogue: Eine Idee

Lea calls her friend Max.

> Lea: Du, Max, ich habe eine Idee. Ich will am Samstag eine Party machen.
>
> Max: Super! Was möchtest du feiern?
>
> Lea: Das Wochenende! Ich möchte alle Freunde aus dem Kurs einladen. Kannst du kommen?
>
> Max: Natürlich! Kann ich helfen? Ich kann ein bisschen kochen.
>
> Lea: Ja, gern. Und du spielst Gitarre, oder? Kannst du etwas spielen?
>
> Max: Hm, ich lerne noch. Ich kann noch nicht wirklich gut spielen.
>
> Lea: Egal! Wir wollen ja nur singen und tanzen.
>
> Max: Okay, dann bringe ich die Gitarre mit. Kommt Jana auch?
>
> Lea: Vielleicht. Ich frage sie gleich.
>
> Max: Toll, ich habe wirklich Lust auf eine Party!

Translation:

> Lea: Hey, Max, I've got an idea. I want to have a party on Saturday.
>
> Max: Great! What would you like to celebrate?
>
> Lea: The weekend! I'd like to invite all my friends from the course. Can you come?
>
> Max: Of course! Can I help? I can cook a bit.
>
> Lea: Yes, please. And you play the guitar, don't you? Can you play something?
>
> Max: Hm, I'm still learning. I can't really play well yet.
>
> Lea: Doesn't matter! We only want to sing and dance anyway.
>
> Max: Okay, then I'll bring the guitar. Is Jana coming too?
>
> Lea: Maybe. I'll ask her right away.
>
> Max: Great, I'm really in the mood for a party!

New in passing: *egal* (it doesn't matter), *oder?* at the end of a sentence ("…, don't
you?"), *mitbringen* (to bring along, separable like *mitkommen*).

## Pronunciation

- *können, möchten, mögen*: ö is made by saying "e" with rounded lips. Don't flatten it to
  "o": *können* with ö (we can) and *kann* with a (I can) sound clearly different.
- *will* has a short i, like English "will"; the w is a German w, which sounds like English v:
  "vill".
- *Klavier*: the v sounds like English v here (it's a foreign word), and the stress is at the
  end: kla-VIER. *Gitarre* is stressed on the second syllable: gi-TAR-re. *Instrument*: in-stru-MENT.
- *Lust*: the u is short, as in English "put", and the st is said "st", not "sht".

## Common mistakes

- Keeping the two verbs together, as in English: "Ich kann spielen Gitarre". Correct: *Ich
  kann Gitarre spielen.*
- Adding "zu": "Ich will zu reisen". Modal verbs never take *zu*: *Ich will reisen.*
- Adding endings to ich and er: "ich kanne", "er kannt", "er möchtet". Correct: *ich kann, er
  kann, er möchte.*
- Conjugating both verbs: "Ich kann schwimme". Only the modal changes; the other verb stays in
  the infinitive.
- Reading *Ich will* as "I will". It means "I want".
- Using *will* when ordering. Say *Ich möchte …* to be polite.
- Using *mögen* for activities: "Ich mag schwimmen" is understood but sounds odd at this level;
  say *Ich schwimme gern*.

## Key points

- Modal verbs: *können* (can), *wollen* (want), *mögen* (like), *möchten* (would like).
- ich and er/sie/es forms are identical with no ending: *ich kann, er kann; ich will, er will;
  ich möchte, er möchte.*
- Sentence bracket: modal in position 2, infinitive at the end, everything else in between.
- Questions: *Kannst du …?* with the infinitive at the end. *Nicht* goes before the infinitive.
- Separable verbs join up again at the end: *Willst du mitkommen?*
- *möchte* is polite, *will* is direct; *will* never means "will".
- *Hast du Lust?* and *Das macht Spaß* are everyday phrases for plans.
