## What this lesson is for

In the last lesson you learned that every noun has a gender and an article: *der Stift, die
Tasche, das Handy*. Now you'll use that knowledge to talk about the people in your life — and
to say whose they are. *My* brother, *your* sister, *our* dog: words like these are called
**possessive articles**, and they take the place of *der/die/das* or *ein/eine*.

You'll also learn all forms of *haben* (to have), so you can say *Ich habe zwei Brüder* or
*Wir haben eine Katze*.

## Family words

| Masculine | Feminine | Neuter / plural |
|---|---|---|
| der Vater (father) | die Mutter (mother) | die Eltern (parents — plural only) |
| der Sohn (son) | die Tochter (daughter) | das Kind, die Kinder (child, children) |
| der Bruder (brother) | die Schwester (sister) | die Geschwister (brothers and sisters, siblings) |
| der Großvater / der Opa (grandfather / grandpa) | die Großmutter / die Oma (grandmother / grandma) | das Baby (baby) |
| der Mann (man; husband) | die Frau (woman; wife) | die Familie (family) |
| der Freund (friend; boyfriend) | die Freundin (friend; girlfriend) | die Person (person) |

Some notes:

- *die Frau* means "the woman" and also "the wife". Without an article, *Frau Klein* is "Mrs
  (or Ms) Klein", as you learned in lesson 2. In the same way *der Mann* is "the man" and *mein
  Mann* is "my husband".
- *die Eltern* and *die Geschwister* are used only in the plural. German has a handy single
  word for "brothers and sisters": *Hast du Geschwister?* — Do you have any siblings?
- *Opa* and *Oma* are the everyday words. *Großvater* and *Großmutter* sound a bit more formal.
- Many family plurals take an umlaut: *die Mütter, die Väter, die Brüder, die Töchter, die
  Söhne*. *die Schwester* becomes *die Schwestern*.

Pets count as family too: *der Hund* (dog), *die Katze* (cat), and the general word *das Tier*
(animal).

## Possessive articles: whose is it?

Here are the possessive articles, one for each person you met in lesson 4:

| Pronoun | Possessive | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ich | mein | my |
| du | dein | your (one person, informal) |
| er | sein | his |
| sie | ihr | her |
| es | sein | its |
| wir | unser | our |
| ihr | euer | your (several people, informal) |
| sie | ihr | their |
| Sie | Ihr | your (formal, one or more people) |

Note two things. First, *ihr* has several meanings: "her", "their", and with a capital *I*,
the formal "your" (*Ihr*). Context makes it clear. Second, the pronoun *ihr* (you, plural)
and the possessive *ihr* (her/their) look identical but are different words.

## The endings: same as ein and eine

A possessive article takes exactly the same endings as *ein*: no ending for masculine and
neuter, *-e* for feminine. Unlike *ein*, it also has a plural form, also with *-e*.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a | ein Bruder | eine Schwester | ein Kind | — |
| my | mein Bruder | meine Schwester | mein Kind | meine Eltern |
| your | dein Bruder | deine Schwester | dein Kind | deine Eltern |
| his | sein Bruder | seine Schwester | sein Kind | seine Eltern |
| her | ihr Bruder | ihre Schwester | ihr Kind | ihre Eltern |
| our | unser Bruder | unsere Schwester | unser Kind | unsere Eltern |
| your (pl.) | euer Bruder | eure Schwester | euer Kind | eure Eltern |
| their | ihr Bruder | ihre Schwester | ihr Kind | ihre Eltern |
| your (formal) | Ihr Bruder | Ihre Schwester | Ihr Kind | Ihre Eltern |

*euer* drops its second *e* when an ending is added: *eure*, not ~~euere~~.

These are the forms for the subject of the sentence, the person or thing doing the action or
being described. Grammar calls this form the **nominative**: it's the first of German's four
**cases**, the forms that show a noun's job in the sentence. You'll meet the next case in
lesson 9.

### Two questions, two decisions

English speakers often get stuck on *sein* versus *ihr*. Use two steps:

1. Who owns it? That chooses the word: a man or boy → *sein*; a woman or girl → *ihr*;
   several people → *ihr*.
2. What is owned? Its gender and number choose the ending: masculine or neuter → no ending;
   feminine or plural → *-e*.

- *Paul und seine Mutter* — Paul and his mother (Paul owns → *sein*; *Mutter* is feminine → *-e*)
- *Anna und ihr Vater* — Anna and her father (Anna owns → *ihr*; *Vater* is masculine → no ending)
- *Anna und ihre Mutter* — Anna and her mother
- *Die Kinder und ihr Hund* — The children and their dog

Step 1 is the same as in English. Step 2 is the new part: the ending agrees with the thing
owned, not the owner.

## Wer ist das?

To ask about a person, use *wer* (who); for a thing, *was* (what):

- *Wer ist das?* — *Das ist mein Bruder Jonas.* — Who's that? — That's my brother Jonas.
- *Wer sind die Leute auf dem Foto?* — *Das sind meine Eltern.* — Who are the people in the
  photo? — Those are my parents. (*die Leute* = people; *auf dem Foto* = in the photo.)
- *Ist das deine Freundin?* — *Nein, das ist meine Schwester.* — Is that your girlfriend? —
  No, that's my sister.

As in the last lesson, *das* in *Das ist / Das sind* never changes.

## haben in full

You know *ich habe, du hast, Sie haben*. Here is the whole verb. Watch the *er/sie/es* form:
it's *hat*, not ~~habt~~.

| | haben |
|---|---|
| ich | habe |
| du | hast |
| er / sie / es | hat |
| wir | haben |
| ihr | habt |
| sie / Sie | haben |

- *Wir haben zwei Kinder, einen Sohn und eine Tochter.* — We have two children, a son and a
  daughter.
- *Hat sie Geschwister?* — Does she have siblings?
- *Habt ihr ein Tier?* — Do you (all) have a pet?
- *Mein Vater hat eine Katze.* — My father has a cat.

One small preview: after *haben*, a masculine noun takes *einen* instead of *ein*: *Ich habe
einen Bruder* (I have a brother), *Er hat einen Hund*. The same happens to the possessives:
*Ich habe meinen Schlüssel*. Feminine, neuter and plural don't change. Lesson 9 explains why;
for now, treat *einen* as the "after *haben*" form of *ein* for masculine nouns.

## Married, single, divorced

To give your marital status, use *sein* + the word, with no ending:

- *Ich bin verheiratet.* — I'm married.
- *Mein Bruder ist ledig.* — My brother is single.
- *Meine Eltern sind geschieden.* — My parents are divorced.

## Dialogue: photos on the phone

Sara shows her colleague Mehmet some photos.

> Mehmet: *Wer ist das? Ist das deine Familie?*
>
> Sara: *Ja. Das ist mein Vater und das ist meine Mutter.*
>
> Mehmet: *Und das Baby?*
>
> Sara: *Das ist Lukas, der Sohn von meiner Schwester. Meine Schwester ist verheiratet. Ihr Mann heißt Jan.*
>
> Mehmet: *Hast du auch einen Bruder?*
>
> Sara: *Nein, ich habe nur eine Schwester. Und du? Hast du Geschwister?*
>
> Mehmet: *Ja, zwei Brüder. Wir haben auch einen Hund. Sein Name ist Kebab!*
>
> Sara: *Kebab? Euer Hund heißt Kebab? Toll!*

Translation:

> Mehmet: Who's that? Is that your family?
>
> Sara: Yes. That's my father and that's my mother.
>
> Mehmet: And the baby?
>
> Sara: That's Lukas, my sister's son. My sister is married. Her husband is called Jan.
>
> Mehmet: Do you have a brother too?
>
> Sara: No, I only have one sister. And you? Do you have siblings?
>
> Mehmet: Yes, two brothers. We have a dog too. His name is Kebab!
>
> Sara: Kebab? Your dog is called Kebab? Great!

(*nur* = only; *toll* = great. *der Sohn von meiner Schwester* is a common spoken way to say
"my sister's son"; *meiner* is a form you'll learn with the dative in lesson 18.)

Notice *Sein Name*: the dog is *der Hund*, so "his" is *sein*. German refers to animals and
things by their grammatical gender: a masculine noun is "he" (*er*) and takes *sein*.

## Pronunciation

- *Vater* begins with an *f* sound: FAH-ter. The *v* in German words is usually an *f*.
- *Tochter* and its plural *Töchter*: the *ch* after *o* is the throaty *ach*-sound;
  after *ö* it's the soft *ich*-sound.
- *Eltern*, *Geschwister*, *verheiratet*, *geschieden*: stress *EL-tern*, *ge-SCHWIS-ter*,
  *ver-HEI-ra-tet*, *ge-SCHIE-den*. *ei* sounds like "eye", *ie* like "ee".
- *Familie* has four syllables: fa-MI-li-e.
- *Baby* is said as in English, with a German *a*: BEH-bi.

## Common mistakes

- Using the owner's gender for the ending. ~~Anna und ihre Vater~~ → *Anna und ihr Vater*:
  *Vater* is masculine, so no ending.
- Translating "her" as *sie*. *sie* is "she / they"; the possessive "her" is *ihr*: *ihr Bruder*.
- Saying ~~euere~~. The form is *eure*.
- Writing *Ihr* (formal your) in lower case, or the plural pronoun *ihr* with a capital.
  *Wie ist Ihr Name?* (formal) vs *Wo wohnt ihr?* (you all).
- Saying ~~er habt~~. It's *er hat*; *habt* belongs to *ihr*.
- Counting *Eltern* as one person. *Meine Eltern sind …* — always plural, with *sind*.
- Calling a friend *mein Freund* when you don't mean a partner: *mein Freund* and *meine
  Freundin* usually suggest a boyfriend or girlfriend. For a plain friend Germans often say
  *ein Freund von mir* (a friend of mine).

## Key points

- Possessive articles: *mein, dein, sein, ihr, sein, unser, euer, ihr, Ihr*.
- They take the same endings as *ein*: none for masculine and neuter, *-e* for feminine, and
  *-e* in the plural (*meine Eltern*).
- The owner chooses the word (*sein* or *ihr*); the thing owned chooses the ending.
- *haben*: *habe, hast, hat, haben, habt, haben*. After *haben*, masculine *ein* becomes
  *einen* (explained in lesson 9).
- *Wer ist das? — Das ist meine Mutter. Wer sind die Leute? — Das sind meine Eltern.*
