## What this lesson is for

In lesson 23 you learned the frame of a short message: *Liebe Anna, … Viele Grüße* for a
friend, *Sehr geehrte Frau Weber, … Mit freundlichen Grüßen* for someone you address with
*Sie*. This honors lesson goes further. You learn how a formal German letter is laid out, how
to choose between the many openings and closings Germans really use (there are more than
two!), the spelling rules for *Sie* and *du* in writing, and how to write a message that
covers three points, the kind of task you meet in the writing part of the A1 and A2 exams.
Along the way you meet some customs: who offers *du*, how people greet each other in
different regions, and the holiday postcard.

## The parts of a formal letter

A formal letter to an office, a school or a company has a fixed layout. German offices
follow a standard for business letters (DIN 5008), but you only need the basic order:

![A formal German letter with its parts labeled: the sender's name and address at the top left (Absender), the recipient's address below it (Empfänger), the place and date on the right (Ort und Datum: Bremen, 12. Mai 2026), the subject line in bold (Betreff: Kurs am Dienstag), the salutation (Anrede: Sehr geehrte Frau Berger,), the text, the closing (Grußformel: Mit freundlichen Grüßen) and the handwritten signature (Unterschrift) above the typed name.](images/de1-m6-letter.svg)

| part | German name | example |
|---|---|---|
| your name and address | der Absender (sender) | Paul Novak, Gartenweg 4, 28195 Bremen |
| their name and address | der Empfänger (recipient) | Sprachschule am Markt, Frau Berger, … |
| place and date | Ort und Datum | Bremen, 12. Mai 2026 |
| subject line | der Betreff | Kurs am Dienstag |
| opening | die Anrede (**salutation**) | Sehr geehrte Frau Berger, |
| text | der Text | ich habe am Dienstag … |
| closing | die Grußformel (**sign-off**) | Mit freundlichen Grüßen |
| signature and name | die Unterschrift | Paul Novak |

A few details that make a letter look German:

- The date is written with the day first and a full stop after the day number, because it
  is an ordinal number (lesson 16): *12. Mai 2026* or *12.05.2026*. You read it *zwölfter
  Mai*. An older style adds *den*: *Bremen, den 12. Mai 2026*, read *den zwölften Mai*.
- The subject line is short and printed in bold. In modern letters the word *Betreff* itself
  is usually left out. In an e-mail, the subject goes in the field called *Betreff*.
- The text starts with a small letter after the comma of the salutation (lesson 23).
- There is no comma after the closing, and nothing like "Yours" before your name.

An e-mail keeps only the last five parts: subject, salutation, text, closing, name.

## Register: from Sehr geehrte to Hallo

The **register** of a text is how formal it is. In lesson 23 you saw the two ends of the
scale. Real messages use the steps in between too:

| opening | form of address | when |
|---|---|---|
| *Sehr geehrte Frau Berger,* | Sie | formal letters, offices, first contact |
| *Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren,* | Sie | you don't know the name |
| *Liebe Frau Berger,* | Sie | friendly but still formal: a teacher you know |
| *Hallo Frau Berger,* | Sie | very common in e-mails; neutral |
| *Hallo zusammen,* | ihr or Sie | to a group, such as your course |
| *Liebe Anna,* / *Hallo Anna,* | du | friends, family, classmates |

Look carefully at the third and fourth rows. *Liebe Frau Berger* and *Hallo Frau Berger* are
friendlier than *Sehr geehrte*, but with a surname (*Frau Berger*) you still write *Sie*.
The first name (*Anna*) goes with *du*.

The closings have steps too:

| closing | register |
|---|---|
| *Mit freundlichen Grüßen* | formal, the safe choice for letters |
| *Freundliche Grüße* | formal, a little lighter; common in e-mails |
| *Viele Grüße* | neutral: colleagues, a teacher, friends |
| *Liebe Grüße* | warm: friends and family |
| *LG*, *VG* | only in quick text messages to friends |

Many people find the abbreviation *MfG* (for *Mit freundlichen Grüßen*) rude, because it looks
as if you couldn't be bothered to write three words. Don't use it.

### Saying hello around the German-speaking countries

Spoken greetings change from region to region. You learned *Grüß Gott* in lesson 1, and the
honors lesson on the German-speaking world (module 1) shows more. Three you will hear often:

- *Moin* (or *Moin moin*): northern Germany, at any time of day, not just in the morning.
- *Servus*: Bavaria and Austria, informal, for both hello and goodbye.
- *Grüezi*: Switzerland, a polite hello to people you address with *Sie*.

You don't need to use them yourself, but it's good to recognize them. In writing, stick to
the openings in the table.

## Sie and du in writing

Two spelling rules:

1. The polite *Sie* and all its forms always have a capital letter, even in the middle of a
   sentence: *Sie, Ihnen, Ihr, Ihre*. This is how a reader can tell "you" from "she" or
   "they": *Können Sie mir helfen?* (Can you help me?) but *Können sie mir helfen?* (Can they
   help me?). *Ihr Brief* is "your letter", *ihr Brief* "her letter".
2. *du* and *dein* are normally written with a small letter. In letters and messages you may
   write them with a capital as a sign of warmth (*Wie geht es Dir?*); both are correct
   since the 2006 spelling reform. The same goes for *ihr* and *euer* when you write to
   several friends. Choose one way and keep to it.

### Who says du first?

German has two verbs for this: *duzen* (to say *du* to someone) and *siezen* (to say *Sie*).
Traditionally the older person, or the person in the higher position, offers *du*: *Wollen
wir uns duzen?* A younger person or a new employee waits. This is a custom, not a law, and
it is changing: many young people, sports clubs and some companies use *du* from the start.
If you are not sure, use *Sie*; nobody is offended by it, and the other person will offer
*du* if they want to.

## A message with three points

Exam writing tasks and real life often give you three things to say. Plan before you write:
one or two sentences for each point, joined with *und, aber, denn* or *deshalb*.

### Example 1: an informal e-mail

Task: You are on holiday. Write to your friend Lisa. Say where you are, what you did
yesterday, and when you are coming back.

Plan:

1. Where: *Ich bin auf Rügen.* (Rügen is an island in the Baltic Sea.) Add the weather
   with *war* or *ist*.
2. Yesterday: perfect tense (lesson 21 and 22): *Gestern sind wir … gefahren / haben wir …
   gemacht.*
3. Coming back: present tense with a time: *Am Samstag komme ich zurück.*

Message:

> Liebe Lisa,
>
> viele Grüße von der Insel Rügen! Das Wetter ist super. Gestern sind wir mit dem Fahrrad
> zum Strand gefahren und sind im Meer geschwommen. Das Wasser war kalt, aber schön. Am
> Samstag komme ich zurück. Hast du am Sonntag Zeit?
>
> Liebe Grüße
> Mira

Check: all three points are there, the opening and closing match (*Liebe Lisa* … *Liebe
Grüße*), the *du* form is used throughout, and the verbs are in position 2. About 45 words.

### Example 2: from informal to formal

Paul wrote this to his classmate Tom:

> Lieber Tom,
>
> ich kann am Freitag leider nicht zum Kurs kommen, denn ich bin krank. Kannst du mir bitte
> die Hausaufgabe schicken?
>
> Viele Grüße
> Paul

Now he needs to write the same thing to his teacher, Frau Weber, whom he addresses with
*Sie*. Go through it part by part:

1. Salutation: *Lieber Tom* → *Sehr geehrte Frau Weber* (she is a woman, so *geehrte*). If
   he knows her well, *Liebe Frau Weber* is also fine.
2. The first sentence has no *du* in it, so it stays the same.
3. *Kannst du* → *Können Sie*, with a capital *S*. Remember that the verb form changes too:
   *Sie* takes the *-en* form.
4. Closing: *Viele Grüße* → *Mit freundlichen Grüßen*, or *Viele Grüße* if the salutation
   was *Liebe Frau Weber*.
5. Name: the full name, *Paul Novak*.

> Sehr geehrte Frau Weber,
>
> ich kann am Freitag leider nicht zum Kurs kommen, denn ich bin krank. Können Sie mir bitte
> die Hausaufgabe schicken?
>
> Mit freundlichen Grüßen
> Paul Novak

## The holiday postcard

Even in the age of the smartphone, many Germans still send postcards from their holidays,
especially to grandparents and to colleagues at work. A postcard is short and uses exactly
the grammar of this module: *war* for the weather, the perfect for what you did. The
address goes on the right, the message on the left, and you need a stamp (*die Briefmarke*).

> Liebe Oma,
>
> viele Grüße aus Wien! Wir sind am Montag mit dem Zug angekommen. Das Hotel ist klein, aber
> sehr gemütlich. Gestern waren wir im Museum, und heute haben wir viel Kuchen gegessen!
> Am Sonntag fahren wir zurück.
>
> Dein Max

Translation: Dear Grandma, greetings from Vienna! We arrived on Monday by train. The hotel is
small but very cosy. Yesterday we were at the museum, and today we've eaten lots of cake! On
Sunday we're going back. Love, Max.

Notice the closing: on a postcard to family, *Dein Max* (your Max) or *Deine Mira* is
common. The form matches the writer: *Dein* for a man or boy, *Deine* for a woman or girl.

## Pronunciation

- *Absender* is stressed on the first syllable: AB-sen-der. *Empfänger* is stressed on the
  second, emp-FÄN-ger, because *emp-* is an inseparable prefix and is never stressed.
- *Briefmarke*: BRIEF-mar-ke, with a long "ee".
- *Grüezi* is Swiss German: GRÜ-e-tsi, three syllables.
- *Servus*: SER-vus, with a voiced *s* at the start, like English z.

## Common mistakes

- Writing *sie* for *Sie*. In a message to Frau Berger, *Können sie mir helfen?* asks
  whether *they* can help you.
- Writing *du* after *Liebe Frau Berger*. A surname always goes with *Sie*.
- Mixing levels: *Sehr geehrte Frau Berger … Liebe Grüße*. Match the closing to the opening.
- The American date: *Mai 12, 2026* is wrong. Write *12. Mai 2026*, with the full stop.
- Forgetting a point of the task. In the exam, a missing point costs more than a small
  grammar mistake. Tick each point off your plan.
- Writing *MfG*, or putting a comma after the closing.

## Key points

- A formal letter: Absender, Empfänger, Ort und Datum, Betreff (bold), Anrede, text,
  Grußformel, Unterschrift. Dates: *12. Mai 2026*.
- Openings from formal to informal: *Sehr geehrte/r … → Liebe/r Frau/Herr … → Hallo Frau/Herr
  … → Liebe/r or Hallo + first name*. Surname = *Sie*, first name = *du*.
- Closings: *Mit freundlichen Grüßen → Freundliche Grüße → Viele Grüße → Liebe Grüße*.
- *Sie, Ihnen, Ihr* always have a capital; *du, dein* may have one in letters.
- *duzen* and *siezen*: traditionally the older or more senior person offers *du*.
- For a three-point message, plan one or two sentences per point and link them.
