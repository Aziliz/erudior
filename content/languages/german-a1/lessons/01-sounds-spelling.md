## What this lesson is for

German is spelled far more regularly than English. Once you know how the letters sound, you can
read almost any German word out loud, and you can usually write a word you hear. This lesson
gives you that key: the alphabet and its letter names, the vowels (including the three with
dots), the letter ß, the letter groups that trip up English speakers, and where the stress
falls. Along the way you'll learn the first words everyone needs: *ja*, *nein*, *bitte*,
*danke* and the everyday greetings, and how to spell your name when someone asks.

Read every example aloud: pronunciation is learned with your mouth, not your eyes.

## The alphabet

German uses the same 26 letters as English, plus *ä*, *ö*, *ü* and *ß*. The letter names are
different, and you need them to spell your name on the phone or at a counter. The respellings
below are approximate English sounds.

| Letter | Name | Letter | Name | Letter | Name |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | ah | J | yot | S | ess |
| B | bay | K | kah | T | tay |
| C | tsay | L | ell | U | oo |
| D | day | M | emm | V | fow (rhymes with *cow*) |
| E | ay | N | enn | W | vay |
| F | eff | O | oh | X | iks |
| G | gay (hard g) | P | pay | Y | üpsilon |
| H | hah | Q | koo | Z | tset |
| I | ee | R | air (roughly) | ß | ess-tset |

The letters English speakers mix up most are A (ah), E (ay), I (ee) and J (yot). English
"ay" is the German name of E, and English "ee" is the German name of I, so if you spell *Jane*
the English way ("jay, ay, en, ee"), a German writes down something like "?, E, N, I".
Practise your own name until the German letter names come without thinking.

## Vowels: long and short

Every German **vowel** (a, e, i, o, u) can be long or short, and the difference can change the
meaning of a word. Some rough rules for spotting a long vowel:

- a vowel followed by *h* is long: *Jahr*, *wohnen*, *sehr*;
- a doubled vowel is long: *Tee* (tea), *Boot* (boat);
- *ie* is a long "ee": *sie*, *wie*, *vier*.

A vowel before a double **consonant** (*ll*, *mm*, *ss*, *tt*…) is short: *Hallo*, *kommen*,
*bitte*. Short vowels are clipped, as in English *bit* or *hot*; long vowels are held, with no
glide at the end. Long *e* in *Tee* is a pure, steady "ay" that doesn't slide into "ee" the way English
"ay" does.

| Short | Long |
|---|---|
| *Stadt* (city) | *Tag* (day) |
| *bitte* (please) | *sie* (she) |
| *Wort* (word) | *wohnen* (to live) |

An unstressed *e* at the end of a word is never silent. *Name* has two syllables: "NAH-meh".
*Danke* is "DAHN-keh". 

## The umlauts ä, ö, ü

The two dots are called an **umlaut**. They change the sound of the vowel:

- *ä* sounds like *e* in English *bed* (long *ä* as in *Käse* is a longer version of that);
- *ö*: say "ay", then round your lips as if to whistle, keeping your tongue where it is;
- *ü*: say "ee", then round your lips the same way.

You know *Tschüss* (bye). Try *Österreich* (Austria) and *Grüß Gott* (hello, in the south). The
umlaut is part of the spelling, not decoration: *schon* (already) and *schön* (beautiful) are
different words.

Typing them: in this app, use the buttons under the answer box. On a Windows US keyboard,
hold Alt and type 0228 (ä), 0246 (ö), 0252 (ü), 0223 (ß) on the number pad, or switch to the
US-International layout. On a Mac, press Option+u and then the vowel; Option+s gives ß. On a
phone, press and hold the vowel. Germans write *ae*, *oe*, *ue* (*Oesterreich*) only when
they can't type the real letter.

## ß and ss

The letter *ß* is called *Eszett* (ess-tset) or *scharfes S*. It always sounds like a
sharp *s* as in *kiss*. The rule for when to use it:

- after a long vowel or a **diphthong** (two vowels gliding together, like *ei* or *au*),
  write *ß*: *Straße*, *heißen*, *Grüß Gott*;
- after a short vowel, write *ss*: *Tschüss*, *dass*, *Wasser*.

Switzerland doesn't use *ß* at all and writes *ss* everywhere. No German word begins with
*ß*, so you never need it as a capital at the start of a word; in text written all in capitals,
*ß* becomes *SS* (*STRASSE*).

## Vowel pairs: ei, ie, eu, äu, au

These are the pairs English speakers swap most often.

| Spelling | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *ei* | English *eye* | *nein* (no), *heißen* (to be called) |
| *ie* | English *ee* | *Wie bitte?* (pardon?), *Wiedersehen* |
| *eu*, *äu* | *oy* as in *boy* | *Deutschland*, *Häuser* (houses) |
| *au* | *ow* as in *cow* | *auf*, *Frau* |

Memory hook: in *ei* and *ie*, say the English name of the second letter. *Nein* ends in
*i*, so you say "eye"; *wie* ends in *e*, so you say "ee". *Wein* (wine) and *Wien* (Vienna)
are a good pair to practise.

## Consonants that differ from English

| Spelling | Sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *w* | English *v* | *wie*, *Wort*, *wohnen* |
| *v* | usually English *f* | *Vorname*, *vier* |
| *j* | English *y* | *ja*, *Jahr* |
| *z* | *ts* as in *cats* | *zehn* (ten), *zwei* (two) |
| *s* before a vowel | English *z* | *sie*, *Sonne* (sun), *langsam* |
| *sch* | English *sh* | *schreiben*, *schön* |
| *sp*, *st* at the start of a word or part | *shp*, *sht* | *sprechen*, *Stadt*, *buchstabieren* |
| *ch* after a, o, u, au | rough sound at the back of the throat | *Nacht* (night), *noch* (still), *Buchstabe* (letter) |
| *ch* after other letters | soft hiss, like the *h* in *huge* | *ich*, *nicht* (not) |
| *-ig* at the end | like *ich* | *zwanzig* (twenty) |
| *qu* | *kv* | *Quatsch* (nonsense) |
| *th* | plain *t* | *Theater* |

The two *ch* sounds deserve practice. After *a*, *o*, *u* and *au* (*Nacht*, *noch*, *auch*),
it's the scraping sound in Scottish *loch*. After everything else (*ich*, *nicht*, *Milch*),
it's softer, made at the front of the mouth: say "hue" and stretch the h. (*Milch* means milk.) Never turn either
into an English *k* or *ch* as in *church*.

At the end of a word or syllable, *b*, *d* and *g* lose their voice and
sound like *p*, *t* and *k*. This is called **final devoicing**: *Tag* sounds like "tahk", *und* like "oont", *gelb* (yellow) like
"gelp". The spelling doesn't change.

The German *r*: at the start of a syllable, *r* is made at the back of the throat, close to a
light gargle: *richtig* (right), *Grüß Gott*. After a vowel, it mostly melts into a short
"uh": *der* (the) sounds close to "dare-uh", and the *r* in *Wort* is barely heard. If the throat *r* is hard at first, a
rolled *r* is understood everywhere, and many southern speakers use one.

## Where the stress falls

**Word stress** in German is usually on the first syllable: *DAN-ke*, *BIT-te*, *SCHREI-ben*,
*VOR-na-me*. Important exceptions:

- words beginning with *be-*, *ent-*, *ver-* and a few other short prefixes stress the next
  syllable: *Ent-SCHUL-di-gung*, *ver-STE-hen*;
- verbs ending in *-ieren* stress the *ie*: *buch-sta-BIE-ren*;
- many words borrowed from other languages stress the end: *Al-pha-BET*, *Ad-RES-se*;
- *Auf Wiedersehen* is stressed on *WIE*, and *Tschüss* has one syllable.

## Nouns have capital letters

Every German **noun** starts with a capital letter, wherever it is in the sentence: *der Name*,
*das Wort*, *mein Vorname*. That's a gift for readers: you can spot the nouns at a glance. Other
words are lowercase unless they start a sentence. The pronoun *ich* (I) is always lowercase,
unlike English *I*.

*Mein Name ist Lena.* — My name is Lena.
*Wie ist Ihr Familienname?* — What is your surname?
*Das Wort ist neu.* — The word is new.

## Spelling your name

When you give your name in Germany, people will often ask you to spell it. The question is *Wie
schreibt man das?* (literally "How does one write that?"), and the answer is the letters,
said one by one with the verb *buchstabieren* (to spell):

*Wie schreibt man das?* — How do you spell that?
*Können Sie das bitte buchstabieren?* — Could you spell that, please?
*M – Ü – Double-L – E – R.* In German: *em, ü, zwei el* or *doppel-el, e, er.*

A double letter is often said as *zwei* (two) or *Doppel-* plus the letter.

## Polite words and greetings

Greetings change with the time of day. *Guten Morgen* is used until about 10 or 11 o'clock,
*Guten Tag* through the day, *Guten Abend* from about 6 p.m. *Gute Nacht* is only for going to
bed. *Hallo* is friendly and works almost anywhere; *Grüß Gott* is the standard greeting in
Bavaria and Austria. For goodbye, *Auf Wiedersehen* is polite and *Tschüss* is casual.

*Danke!* — *Bitte!* is the everyday exchange: thanks, you're welcome. *Bitte* also means
"please" and "here you are". *Danke schön* and *Bitte schön* are a little warmer.
*Entschuldigung* means "excuse me" (to get attention) and "sorry". *Wie bitte?* asks someone
to repeat what they said: "pardon?". If you need it again and slower, say *Noch einmal,
bitte. Langsam, bitte.*

## Dialogue: at a reception desk

> Frau Brandt: Guten Morgen!
>
> Tom: Guten Morgen. Mein Name ist Tom Weaver.
>
> Frau Brandt: Wie bitte? Noch einmal, bitte. Langsam, bitte.
>
> Tom: Tom Weaver. Mein Vorname ist Tom, mein Familienname ist Weaver.
>
> Frau Brandt: Danke. Wie schreibt man das?
>
> Tom: W – E – A – V – E – R.
>
> Frau Brandt: W, E, A, V wie Vogel, E, R?
>
> Tom: Ja, richtig.
>
> Frau Brandt: Danke schön, Herr Weaver.
>
> Tom: Bitte schön. Auf Wiedersehen!

Good morning! — Good morning. My name is Tom Weaver. — Pardon? Again, please. Slowly, please.
— Tom Weaver. My first name is Tom, my surname is Weaver. — Thank you. How do you spell that? —
W-E-A-V-E-R. — W, E, A, V as in *Vogel* (bird), E, R? — Yes, right. — Thank you very much, Mr
Weaver. — You're welcome. Goodbye!

Notice that Tom says the letter names the German way: *vay, ay, ah, fow, ay, air*.
Germans clarify a letter with a word that starts with it ("V as in Vogel"), much like "B as
in Bravo".

## Pronunciation

- *Entschuldigung*: stress on *SCHUL*; *-ung* is a short "oo" plus the *ng* of English *sing*,
  with no hard g: "oong".
- *Tschüss*: one syllable; start with "ch" as in *church*, then *ü*.
- *Wiedersehen*: "VEE-der-zay-en", with *w* as v and *s* as z.
- *buchstabieren*: *ch* at the back (after *u*), *st* as "sht", stress on *BIE*.
- *schreiben*: "SHRY-ben", *ei* as in *eye*.
- *Familienname*: stress on *MI*; *-lien* is one syllable, "lyen": "fa-MEE-lyen-nah-meh".

## Common mistakes

- Reading *w* as English w. *Wie* is "vee", *Wort* is "vort".
- Swapping *ei* and *ie*. *nein* is "nine", *sie* is "zee". Use the second-letter hook.
- A silent final *e*. *Danke*, *bitte*, *Name*: always pronounce the last *e*.
- Using *Gute Nacht* as a greeting in the evening. It means goodbye at bedtime; say *Guten
  Abend* when you arrive.
- Leaving off the umlaut. *schon* and *schön* are different words; write the dots.
- Lowercase nouns. *das wort* is wrong; nouns are always capitalized: *das Wort*.
- Spelling with English letter names. Practise *E* = ay, *I* = ee, *J* = yot, *V* = fow,
  *W* = vay.

## Key points

- German spelling is regular: learn the sounds of the letters once and you can read aloud.
- *w* = v, *v* = f, *j* = y, *z* = ts, *s* before a vowel = z, *sp/st* = shp/sht.
- *ei* = "eye", *ie* = "ee", *eu/äu* = "oy"; two *ch* sounds; final *b/d/g* sound like *p/t/k*.
- *ä, ö, ü* are separate sounds; write *ß* after long vowels and diphthongs, *ss* after short ones.
- Stress is usually on the first syllable, except after *be-, ent-, ver-*, in *-ieren* verbs
  and in many borrowed words.
- All nouns start with a capital letter.
- *Wie schreibt man das?* — answer with German letter names.
