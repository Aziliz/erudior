## What this lesson is for

Lesson 3 taught you to say the numbers from 0 to 100 and to give your age, phone number and
address. Numbers do more than that in daily life: you add up a bill out loud, help a child with
homework, hear that someone "got a one" at school, hold up fingers to order two coffees, and
read a handwritten note. Each of these works a little differently in the German-speaking
countries. This honors lesson covers doing sums in German, numbers used as nouns, school grades
in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, counting on your fingers and writing digits by hand. Along
the way you'll meet a new pattern in the present tense, in verbs like *rechnen* (to calculate).

## Doing sums out loud

The four basic operations:

| Sign | German | Example | Said |
|---|---|---|---|
| + | *plus* | 3 + 4 = 7 | *drei plus vier ist sieben* |
| − | *minus* | 9 − 5 = 4 | *neun minus fünf ist vier* |
| · or × | *mal* | 6 · 7 = 42 | *sechs mal sieben ist zweiundvierzig* |
| : or ÷ | *geteilt durch* | 20 : 4 = 5 | *zwanzig geteilt durch vier ist fünf* |

For "equals", say *ist*, or more formally *ist gleich* or *gleich*: *zwei plus zwei ist gleich
vier*. In casual speech, *und* often replaces *plus* (*zwei und zwei ist vier*), and *durch*
on its own replaces *geteilt durch* (*zwanzig durch vier*). The usual verb is singular, *ist*,
because the whole sum is treated as one thing (you'll also hear *sind*).

German schools usually write multiplication with a raised dot (6 · 7) and division with a colon
(20 : 4). The × and ÷ signs that English-speaking schools use are understood, but you'll rarely
see ÷ on a German worksheet.

To ask for a result, use *Wie viel ist …?* (How much is …?), which you'll use for prices in
Lesson 10:

*Wie viel ist acht mal neun? — Acht mal neun ist zweiundsiebzig.*

### Example 1: Reading sums aloud

Read each line aloud, then check the result.

1. 15 + 27 = 42: *fünfzehn plus siebenundzwanzig ist zweiundvierzig*. Remember: units first in
   27 and 42.
2. 81 − 36 = 45: *einundachtzig minus sechsunddreißig ist fünfundvierzig*. *ein* (not *eins*)
   inside *einundachtzig*; *sechs* keeps its *s* in *sechsunddreißig*.
3. 7 · 8 = 56: *sieben mal acht ist sechsundfünfzig*.
4. 96 : 4 = 24: *sechsundneunzig geteilt durch vier ist vierundzwanzig*.

## A new present-tense pattern: rechnen, zeichnen, öffnen

In Lesson 4 you added an *e* before *-st* and *-t* when the stem ends in *-t* or *-d*
(*arbeitest*). The same thing happens when the stem ends in *m* or *n* after another
consonant, because *rechnst* or *rechnt* would be almost impossible to say:

| | rechnen (to calculate) | zeichnen (to draw) | lernen (to learn) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ich | *rechne* | *zeichne* | *lerne* |
| du | *rechnest* | *zeichnest* | *lernst* |
| er / sie / es | *rechnet* | *zeichnet* | *lernt* |
| wir | *rechnen* | *zeichnen* | *lernen* |
| ihr | *rechnet* | *zeichnet* | *lernt* |
| sie / Sie | *rechnen* | *zeichnen* | *lernen* |

Look at *lernen*: its stem *lern-* also ends in consonant + *n*, but no *e* is added. After *l*
or *r*, the *n* or *m* is easy to say, so the regular endings stay. The same is true when the
letter before *n* is a silent *h*: *wohnen*, *du wohnst*. Other verbs with the extra *e* that
you'll meet: *öffnen* (to open: *er öffnet*, Lesson 15) and *regnen* (to rain: *es regnet*,
Lesson 16).

*zählen* (to count) is completely regular: *ich zähle, du zählst, er zählt*.

### Example 2: Which form?

Fill in the present tense: *Du ___ sehr schnell.* (rechnen) — *Mia ___ ein Haus.* (zeichnen) —
*Du ___ Deutsch.* (lernen) — *Ihr ___ bis zehn.* (zählen)

1. *rechnen*: stem *rechn-*. Before the *n* comes *ch*, a real consonant sound (not a silent
   *h* as in *wohnen*), so add the *e*: *Du rechnest sehr schnell.* (You calculate very fast.)
2. *zeichnen*: stem *zeichn-*, again *ch* + *n*: *Mia zeichnet ein Haus.* (Mia is drawing a
   house.)
3. *lernen*: stem *lern-*, *r* + *n*, so no extra *e*: *Du lernst Deutsch.*
4. *zählen*: stem *zähl-*, no *m* or *n* at the end, so regular: *Ihr zählt bis zehn.* (You
   count to ten.)

## Numbers as nouns

A **cardinal number** is a counting number: *eins, zwei, drei*. When you talk about a number as
a thing (a digit, a grade, a bus line), it becomes a **number noun**: it gets a capital letter
and the article *die*, whatever the number is:

*die Eins, die Zwei, die Drei, … die Zehn, die Zwanzig*

*Die Sieben ist meine Zahl.* — Seven is my number.
*Ich nehme die Fünf.* — I'm taking the number 5 (bus or tram).
*Ich habe eine Eins in Mathe.* — I got a one (the top grade) in maths.

That last sentence leads straight to school grades.

## School grades

In Germany, school grades (*die Noten*) run from 1 to 6, and 1 is best. Austria uses 1 to 5,
also with 1 as best. Switzerland turns the scale upside down: 6 is best, 1 is worst, and 4 is
the pass mark. Each German and Austrian grade also has a name:

![Three columns of school grades, best at the top. Germany: 1 sehr gut, 2 gut, 3 befriedigend, 4 ausreichend, 5 mangelhaft, 6 ungenügend; 1 to 4 pass, 5 and 6 fail. Austria: 1 Sehr gut, 2 Gut, 3 Befriedigend, 4 Genügend, 5 Nicht genügend; 1 to 4 pass, 5 fails. Switzerland: 6 best down to 1 worst; 4 is the pass mark, so 4 to 6 pass and 1 to 3 fail. Swiss schools often give half grades such as 5.5.](images/de1-m1-grades.svg)

*sehr gut* means very good, *gut* good, *befriedigend* satisfactory, *ausreichend* sufficient,
*mangelhaft* poor and *ungenügend* insufficient. In everyday talk, people just use the number
noun: *eine Eins*, *eine Zwei*, *eine Fünf*. (In the last two years of a German *Gymnasium*,
grades are given as points from 0 to 15 instead, with 15 the best.)

The scales are a classic misunderstanding between the countries. A Swiss pupil who proudly says
*Ich habe eine Sechs!* has the top grade; a German pupil with *eine Sechs* has the worst.

### Example 3: Good or bad news?

1. Jonas, in Munich, says: *Ich habe eine Zwei in Englisch.* Germany, 2 = *gut*. Good news.
2. Anna, in Zurich, says: *Ich habe eine Fünf in Mathe.* Switzerland, 5 is above the pass mark
   of 4. Good news.
3. Lukas, in Hamburg, says: *Ich habe eine Fünf in Mathe.* Germany, 5 = *mangelhaft*, a fail.
   Bad news.
4. Sara, in Graz, has *eine Vier*. Austria, 4 = *Genügend*, the lowest pass. She passed, just.

Notice that a 4 is a pass in all three countries, while a 5 is a fail in Germany and Austria
but a good grade in Switzerland.

## Counting on your fingers

To show "one" with your hand, most Germans hold up the thumb (*der Daumen*), not the index
finger (*der Zeigefinger*, the "pointing finger"; *der Finger* is finger). Two is thumb and index
finger, three adds the middle finger, and so on. If you order *zwei Kaffee* by
holding up your index and middle finger, you'll almost always get two, but thumb and index
finger is the local way and can't be misread. When Germans count aloud on their fingers, they
also start with the thumb.

## Writing digits by hand

Handwritten digits differ too. In Germany, a 1 is usually written with a long upstroke, which
can look like an American 7. To keep them apart, the 7 gets a short line across the middle.

![Handwritten digits. On the left, as often written in Germany: a 1 with a long sloping upstroke and a 7 with a short bar across the middle. On the right, as often written in the US: a 1 as a single straight line and a 7 without a bar.](images/de1-m1-digits.svg)

If you read German handwriting, a "7" without a bar is probably a 1. If you write numbers for
Germans, cross your sevens.

## Number mix-ups

Because German says 73 as "three and seventy", it's easy to write the digits in the wrong
order, even for native speakers. This mistake has its own name, *der Zahlendreher* (a number
"turner"), and it happens with phone numbers, prices and bank details. A small association,
*Zwanzigeins e.V.*, even campaigns for counting the English way (*zwanzig-eins* for 21). It's
a proposal only; standard German keeps *einundzwanzig*, and that's what you should learn.

When a number matters, do what Germans do: say the digits one by one (*sieben, drei*) or repeat
the number back. For decimals, remember the **decimal comma** from Lesson 10: *2,5* is
*zwei Komma fünf*.

## Reading: homework at the kitchen table

> Es ist sieben Uhr. Ben ist neun Jahre alt, und er macht Hausaufgaben. Er rechnet: "Acht mal
> sieben ist … sechsundfünfzig!" Seine Schwester Emma ist vierzehn. Sie zeichnet, und sie hört
> Musik. "Emma, wie viel ist zweiundsiebzig geteilt durch acht?" Emma zählt nicht, sie
> antwortet sofort: "Neun." Dann sieht sie Bens Heft. "Ben, hier steht 'acht mal eins'! Eine
> Sieben hat einen Strich." Ben lacht. Morgen hat er Mathe, und er möchte eine Eins.

It's seven o'clock. Ben is nine years old, and he's doing homework. He calculates: "Eight times
seven is … fifty-six!" His sister Emma is fourteen. She's drawing and listening to music. "Emma,
how much is seventy-two divided by eight?" Emma doesn't count; she answers at once: "Nine."
Then she sees Ben's exercise book. "Ben, it says 'eight times one' here! A seven has a line
through it." Ben laughs. Tomorrow he has maths, and he wants a one.

(*Hausaufgaben* means homework, *seine Schwester* his sister, *sofort* at once, *sieht* sees,
*Bens Heft* Ben's exercise book, *hier steht* it says here, *der Strich* line or stroke, *lacht*
laughs and *möchte* would like.)

Check the maths: 8 · 7 = 56 and 72 : 8 = 9. And notice the joke: Ben wrote his 7 without a bar,
so to German eyes it's a 1.

## Common mistakes

- *zwanzig geteilt vier*. Division needs *durch*: *zwanzig geteilt durch vier* (or just
  *zwanzig durch vier*).
- *du rechnst*, *er zeichnt*. After a consonant, *m* or *n* needs the extra *e*: *du rechnest*,
  *er zeichnet*.
- *du lernest*. After *r* (or *l*), no extra *e*: *du lernst*.
- *der Eins*, *das Eins*. Number nouns are all feminine: *die Eins*, *eine Eins*.
- Reading a Swiss *Sechs* as a bad grade, or a German *Fünf* as a good one. Check the country.
- Writing a 7 without a bar for German readers. Cross it.
- Showing "one" with your index finger and "two" with index and middle finger may work, but
  the German way starts with the thumb.

## Key points

- *plus, minus, mal, geteilt durch*; "equals" is *ist* or *ist gleich*.
- German schools write 6 · 7 and 20 : 4.
- Stems ending in consonant + *m*/*n* add an *e*: *du rechnest, er zeichnet*; but *du lernst*,
  *du wohnst*.
- Numbers as nouns are feminine: *die Eins, eine Fünf, die Sieben*.
- Grades: Germany 1 (best) to 6; Austria 1 to 5; Switzerland 6 (best) to 1, pass at 4.
- One finger is the thumb; a handwritten 7 has a bar.
- Number mix-ups (*Zahlendreher*) are common: say important numbers digit by digit.
