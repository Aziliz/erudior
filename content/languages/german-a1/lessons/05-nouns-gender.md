## What this lesson is for

In Module 1 you learned to greet people, give your name, count and build simple sentences with
verbs. Now you start talking about *things*: the pen on the desk, the phone in your bag, the
keys you can't find. To do that in German you need to know three facts about every noun:
its gender, its article and its plural.

By the end of this lesson you can point at an object and ask *Was ist das?* (What is that?),
answer *Das ist ein Stift* (That's a pen), and talk about more than one thing: *Das sind
Stifte* (Those are pens).

## Every noun has a gender

English has one word for "the". German has three, because every German noun belongs to one of
three groups called **gender**: **masculine**, **feminine** and **neuter**. The word for "the"
— the **definite article** — changes with the gender:

| Gender | "the" | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | der | *der Tisch* (the table), *der Stuhl* (the chair), *der Schlüssel* (the key) |
| feminine | die | *die Lampe* (the lamp), *die Tür* (the door), *die Tasche* (the bag) |
| neuter | das | *das Buch* (the book), *das Fenster* (the window), *das Handy* (the mobile phone) |

Grammatical gender is a label for the word, not a fact about the object. A table isn't male
and a door isn't female; *der* and *die* simply tell you which group the word is in. Even
words for people can surprise you: *das Kind* (the child) is neuter.

The gender matters because many other words have to agree with it — the article now, and
later possessives (*mein*, *meine*), adjective endings and pronouns. If you know the gender,
all of these fall into place.

The golden rule: never learn a noun without its article. Don't learn *Tisch = table*;
learn *der Tisch = the table*. Say it aloud as one unit — *derTisch, dieLampe, dasBuch* — until
the article feels like part of the word. Your vocabulary cards always show nouns with their
article, and you'll be asked to type it.

A memory hook many learners like: picture every masculine noun in blue, every feminine noun
in red and every neuter noun in green. When you learn *die Uhr* (the clock), imagine a red
clock.

## The indefinite article: ein and eine

The **indefinite article** means "a" or "an". It has two forms in the basic (subject) form:

| Gender | "the" | "a / an" |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | der Stift | ein Stift |
| feminine | die Brille | eine Brille |
| neuter | das Heft | ein Heft |

Masculine and neuter share *ein*; feminine adds *-e*: *eine*. So *ein* alone doesn't tell you
whether a noun is masculine or neuter — another reason to learn nouns with *der/die/das*.

- *Das ist ein Kuli.* — That's a ballpoint pen.
- *Das ist eine Zeitung.* — That's a newspaper.
- *Das ist ein Foto.* — That's a photo.
- *Ist das eine Uhr?* — Is that a clock?

Just like English "a", *ein/eine* has no plural. For more than one thing you simply drop it:
*ein Buch* → *Bücher* (books).

## Was ist das? Das ist … / Das sind …

The question *Was ist das?* means "What is that?" or "What is this?" — German uses the same
word for both. The answer starts with *Das ist …* for one thing and *Das sind …* for several:

- *Was ist das?* — *Das ist ein Schlüssel.* — What's that? — That's a key.
- *Und das?* — *Das sind Schlüssel.* — And that? — Those are keys.
- *Das ist die Tasche von Anna.* — That's Anna's bag. (Literally "the bag of Anna".)

Notice that the *das* in *Das ist* never changes. It means "this" or "that" and points at
something, whatever its gender: *Das ist der Tisch. Das ist die Lampe. Das sind Stühle.* Only
the article in front of the noun shows the gender.

## Gender hints

You can't guess every gender, but some endings are reliable:

| Ending | Gender | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft | feminine (always) | *die Zeitung*, *die Freiheit* (freedom), *die Möglichkeit* (possibility) |
| -ion, -tät | feminine | *die Information*, *die Universität* |
| -chen, -lein (small things) | neuter (always) | *das Mädchen* (girl), *das Brötchen* (bread roll) |
| -e | usually feminine | *die Lampe*, *die Tasche*, *die Brille* — but *der Name* |
| -er (for a person or a machine that does something) | usually masculine | *der Computer*, *der Lehrer* (teacher) — but *das Fenster* |

Two more hints:

- Words borrowed from English that end in *-y* are often neuter (*das Handy*, *das Hobby*,
  *das Baby*), but not always: *die Party*.
- In a **compound noun** — two nouns joined into one word — the *last* part decides the gender.
  *das Haus* (house) + *die Tür* = *die Haustür* (the front door). *das Handy* + *die Nummer* =
  *die Handynummer*. The first part only adds meaning.

Use the hints to check yourself, but the article you memorised always wins.

## Plurals: one article, many endings

Here is the good news: in the plural every noun takes the same article: *die*.

- *der Tisch* → *die Tische*
- *die Lampe* → *die Lampen*
- *das Buch* → *die Bücher*

The bad news: German has several ways to form the **plural**, so you learn each noun's plural
with it, just like the article. The main patterns:

| Pattern | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| -e | der Tisch, das Heft, der Stift | die Tische, die Hefte, die Stifte |
| umlaut + -e | der Stuhl | die Stühle |
| -er | das Kind | die Kinder |
| umlaut + -er | das Buch | die Bücher |
| -n / -en | die Lampe, die Tür, die Zeitung | die Lampen, die Türen, die Zeitungen |
| -s | das Handy, das Foto, der Kuli | die Handys, die Fotos, die Kulis |
| no ending | der Schlüssel, das Fenster, der Computer | die Schlüssel, die Fenster, die Computer |

Patterns that help:

- Most feminine nouns add *-n* or *-en*: *die Tasche → die Taschen*, *die Uhr → die Uhren*.
- Masculine and neuter nouns ending in *-er*, *-el* or *-en* usually add nothing: *der
  Schlüssel → die Schlüssel*. The article tells you it's plural: *der Schlüssel* (one key),
  *die Schlüssel* (keys).
- Short words ending in a vowel other than *-e*, and many English loanwords, add *-s*:
  *das Handy → die Handys*.
- An umlaut can appear in the plural: *der Stuhl → die Stühle*. The vowel sound changes, so
  listen for it.

A useful word for anything: *das Ding*, plural *die Dinge* — "the thing". *Was ist das Ding
hier?* — What's this thing here?

## Dialogue: the first day in the shared office

Lena helps Tom, a new colleague from Canada, find his way around the desk they share.

> Tom: *Was ist das?*
>
> Lena: *Das ist ein Schlüssel. Der Schlüssel ist für die Tür hier.*
>
> Tom: *Danke! Und das? Ist das eine Lampe?*
>
> Lena: *Ja, das ist die Lampe. Und das sind Stifte, Kulis und Papier.*
>
> Tom: *Super. Ist das dein Handy?*
>
> Lena: *Nein, das ist das Handy von Max. Mein Handy ist hier in der Tasche.*
>
> Tom: *Und die Zeitung?*
>
> Lena: *Die Zeitung ist für alle. Das Fenster ist auch für alle!*

Translation:

> Tom: What's that?
>
> Lena: That's a key. The key is for the door here.
>
> Tom: Thanks! And that? Is that a lamp?
>
> Lena: Yes, that's the lamp. And those are pens, ballpoints and paper.
>
> Tom: Great. Is that your phone?
>
> Lena: No, that's Max's phone. My phone is here in the bag.
>
> Tom: And the newspaper?
>
> Lena: The newspaper is for everyone. The window is for everyone too!

(*dein* = your, *mein* = my, *für* = for, *alle* = everyone. You'll learn *mein* and *dein*
properly in the next lesson; *in der Tasche* means "in the bag" and the form *der* there is
explained in lesson 19.)

## Pronunciation

- *Stuhl*: a long *u* — the *h* isn't pronounced, it only shows the vowel is long.
- *Tür*, *Bücher*, *Stühle*, *Schlüssel*: round your lips for *u* and say *ee* — that's *ü*.
  It's long in *Tür* and *Bücher*, short in *Schlüssel*.
- *Zeitung*: *z* sounds like *ts* and *ei* like English "eye": TSAI-tung.
- *Uhr* sounds close to English "poor" without the *p*: a long *u* and a soft *r*.
- *Handy* is said much like English "Henny" with a *d*; *Computer* keeps the English stress
  (kom-PYOO-ter).
- *Brille*: a short *i* and a double *l* — BRIL-le.

## Common mistakes

- Translating "the" as *die* everywhere. *Die* is only feminine and plural. *der Tisch*,
  not ~~die Tisch~~.
- Guessing the plural with -s. English adds *-s*; German usually doesn't. ~~die Tischs~~ →
  *die Tische*; ~~die Buchs~~ → *die Bücher*.
- Lowercase nouns. Every German noun starts with a capital letter, in the middle of a
  sentence too: *Das ist eine Uhr*, not ~~eine uhr~~.
- Thinking *die Brille* is plural. English says "glasses", but *die Brille* is one pair,
  feminine singular: *Das ist meine Brille.* Two pairs are *zwei Brillen*.
- Changing *Das ist* to match the noun. It's always *Das ist die Lampe*, never
  ~~Die ist die Lampe~~ for "That's the lamp".
- The *Handy* trap. In German *das Handy* means a mobile (cell) phone, not something useful.

## Key points

- Every noun is masculine (*der*), feminine (*die*) or neuter (*das*). Learn each one with its
  article and plural.
- "A" is *ein* for masculine and neuter, *eine* for feminine. There's no plural "a".
- All plurals take *die*. Plural endings vary: *-e, ¨e, -er, ¨er, -(e)n, -s* or none.
- Endings like *-ung, -heit, -keit* (feminine) and *-chen* (neuter) give reliable hints; in a
  compound the last part decides.
- *Was ist das?* — *Das ist …* (one thing) / *Das sind …* (several).
