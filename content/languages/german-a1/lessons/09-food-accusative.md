## What this lesson is for

So far your sentences have mostly described people and things: *Das ist ein Tisch.* *Meine Schwester ist Lehrerin.* *Der Computer ist kaputt.* In this lesson people start doing things to things: eating bread, drinking coffee, ordering a cake. To talk like that, German needs a new form of the article, the **accusative**. The good news is that the change is small: only masculine words change.

You will also learn the verbs you need at the table (*essen*, *trinken*, *nehmen*, *möchten*), how *essen* and *nehmen* change their vowel, and how to order politely in a café. By the end you can say what you like to eat and order breakfast in German.

## Food and drink

Learn every food word with its article, as always. Many drinks are masculine, which matters in a moment.

| der (masculine) | die (feminine) | das (neuter) |
|---|---|---|
| der Kaffee — coffee | die Milch — milk | das Wasser — water |
| der Tee — tea | die Butter — butter | das Bier — beer |
| der Saft — juice | die Wurst — sausage | das Brot — bread |
| der Wein — wine | die Kartoffel — potato | das Brötchen — bread roll |
| der Käse — cheese | die Nudeln — pasta (plural) | das Ei — egg |
| der Fisch — fish | | das Fleisch — meat |
| der Apfel — apple | | das Gemüse — vegetables |
| der Reis — rice | | das Obst — fruit |
| der Salat — salad | | das Essen — food, meal |
| der Kuchen — cake | | |

The three meals are all neuter: *das Frühstück* (breakfast), *das Mittagessen* (lunch) and *das Abendessen* (dinner). *Mittag* means midday and *Abend* means evening, so the words explain themselves.

Two words name whole groups, and German treats them as one uncountable thing, like English *fruit*: *das Obst* (fruit) and *das Gemüse* (vegetables). So *Gemüse ist gesund* — "vegetables are healthy" — uses a singular verb. (*gesund* means healthy.)

German also builds long food words by sticking nouns together. The last part decides the gender: *der Apfel + der Saft = der Apfelsaft* (apple juice), *der Käse + das Brot = das Käsebrot* (cheese sandwich), *der Apfel + der Kuchen = der Apfelkuchen* (apple cake).

## Subject and object: what the accusative is

In "The woman drinks the coffee", the woman does the drinking: she is the subject. The coffee is what gets drunk: it is the **direct object**, the thing the action is done to. English shows this by word order alone. German also marks it on the article, and the form it uses for a direct object is the accusative.

In lesson 6 you met the nominative, the case of the subject (and of anything after *sein*). The accusative is the second of German's four cases; from lesson 18 you'll add a third, the dative.

### Only the masculine changes

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nominative (subject) | der / ein / kein | die / eine / keine | das / ein / kein | die / – / keine |
| accusative (object) | den / einen / keinen | die / eine / keine | das / ein / kein | die / – / keine |

Feminine, neuter and plural forms are exactly the same in both cases. Only the masculine gets an -n: *der* becomes *den*, *ein* becomes *einen*, *kein* becomes *keinen*. A memory hook: the masculine is the only one that changes, and it always picks up an *n*: *den, einen, keinen*.

- *Der Kaffee ist heiß.* — The coffee is hot. (subject: nominative)
- *Ich trinke den Kaffee.* — I'm drinking the coffee. (object: accusative)
- *Ein Apfel kostet nicht viel.* — An apple doesn't cost much. (subject)
- *Er isst einen Apfel.* — He's eating an apple. (object)
- *Ich habe keinen Saft.* — I don't have any juice. (object, negative)
- *Sie trinkt eine Cola.* — She's drinking a cola. (feminine: no change)
- *Wir essen das Brot.* — We're eating the bread. (neuter: no change)
- *Ich kaufe die Eier.* — I'm buying the eggs. (plural: no change; *kaufen* = to buy)

Possessives follow *ein*: *mein* becomes *meinen* for a masculine object. *Ich trinke meinen Tee.* — I'm drinking my tea.

### Which verbs take the accusative?

Most verbs that act on something take a direct object in the accusative: *essen*, *trinken*, *nehmen*, *möchten*, *haben*, *machen*, *hören*, *lernen*, *fragen* and many more. One verb never does: *sein*. After *sein* you keep the nominative, because nothing is being done to anything: *Das ist ein Kuchen* but *Ich esse einen Kuchen*.

## The verbs: essen, trinken, nehmen

*trinken* (to drink) is regular. *essen* (to eat) and *nehmen* (to take; when ordering, to have) change their vowel in the *du* and *er/sie/es* forms: e becomes i.

| | trinken | essen | nehmen |
|---|---|---|---|
| ich | trinke | esse | nehme |
| du | trinkst | isst | nimmst |
| er / sie / es | trinkt | isst | nimmt |
| wir | trinken | essen | nehmen |
| ihr | trinkt | esst | nehmt |
| sie / Sie | trinken | essen | nehmen |

Notice three details. *du isst* has no extra *s* ending, because the stem already ends in *ss*. *er isst* sounds exactly like *er ist* (he is); only the spelling is different. *nehmen* loses its *h* and doubles the *m*: *du nimmst, er nimmt*.

- *Was isst du gern?* — What do you like to eat?
- *Paul nimmt einen Tee.* — Paul is having a tea.
- *Ihr esst viel Obst.* — You (all) eat a lot of fruit.

## möchten: the polite "would like"

*möchten* means "would like". It is the most useful word in any café or shop, and much more polite than "I want".

| | möchten |
|---|---|
| ich | möchte |
| du | möchtest |
| er / sie / es | möchte |
| wir | möchten |
| ihr | möchtet |
| sie / Sie | möchten |

Watch out: *ich möchte* and *er möchte* are identical — there is no *-t* on the *er* form. The object after *möchten* is accusative, like after any other verb:

- *Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte.* — I'd like a coffee, please.
- *Möchten Sie ein Wasser?* — Would you like a water?
- *Sie möchte keinen Fisch.* — She doesn't want any fish.

You can also put a verb at the end: *Ich möchte Kuchen essen* (I'd like to eat cake). Lesson 14 explains this pattern; for now, use *möchten* with a noun.

## Saying what you like: gern

To say you like doing something, add *gern* after the verb. *Ich trinke Tee* is "I drink tea"; *Ich trinke gern Tee* is "I like drinking tea". For dislikes, use *nicht gern*.

- *Ich esse gern Nudeln.* — I like eating pasta.
- *Mein Bruder isst nicht gern Gemüse.* — My brother doesn't like eating vegetables.
- *Trinkst du gern Wein?* — Do you like wine?

When you talk about food in general, you usually leave out the article: *Ich esse gern Fisch* (fish in general), but *Ich esse den Fisch* (that particular fish).

## Hunger and thirst

German says "I have hunger": *Ich habe Hunger* (I'm hungry) and *Ich habe Durst* (I'm thirsty). There is no article. Ask *Hast du Hunger?* — Are you hungry? If food tastes good, it is *lecker*: *Der Kuchen ist lecker!*

## Ordering in a café

| Situation | German | English |
|---|---|---|
| The waiter asks | *Was möchten Sie?* / *Was nehmen Sie?* | What would you like? |
| You order | *Ich nehme einen Tee.* / *Ich möchte ein Brötchen, bitte.* | I'll have a tea. / I'd like a roll, please. |
| Asking about something | *Haben Sie Apfelkuchen?* | Do you have apple cake? |
| With / without | *mit Milch* / *ohne Milch* | with milk / without milk |

*mit* (with) and *ohne* (without) are useful here as set phrases; *Kaffee mit Milch*, *Tee ohne Zucker* (*der Zucker* = sugar).

## Dialogue: In the café

*Kellnerin* is a female waiter.

> Kellnerin: Guten Morgen! Was möchten Sie?
>
> Herr Weber: Ich nehme einen Kaffee mit Milch, bitte.
>
> Kellnerin: Und Sie?
>
> Frau Weber: Ich habe Durst. Ich möchte einen Orangensaft und ein Wasser.
>
> Kellnerin: Möchten Sie auch etwas essen? Das Frühstück ist heute sehr gut.
>
> Frau Weber: Ja, gern. Ein Brötchen mit Butter und Käse, bitte. Haben Sie auch Eier?
>
> Kellnerin: Ja, natürlich. Und Sie, Herr Weber? Nehmen Sie auch ein Brötchen?
>
> Herr Weber: Nein, ich habe keinen Hunger. Aber der Apfelkuchen dort sieht toll aus …
>
> Frau Weber: Paul! Du isst jeden Morgen Kuchen!
>
> Herr Weber: Ja, Kuchen ist lecker. Einen Apfelkuchen, bitte.

In English:

> Waitress: Good morning! What would you like?
>
> Mr Weber: I'll have a coffee with milk, please.
>
> Waitress: And you?
>
> Mrs Weber: I'm thirsty. I'd like an orange juice and a water.
>
> Waitress: Would you like something to eat too? The breakfast is very good today.
>
> Mrs Weber: Yes, please. A roll with butter and cheese, please. Do you have eggs too?
>
> Waitress: Yes, of course. And you, Mr Weber? Are you having a roll too?
>
> Mr Weber: No, I'm not hungry. But the apple cake there looks great …
>
> Mrs Weber: Paul! You eat cake every morning!
>
> Mr Weber: Yes, cake is delicious. An apple cake, please.

Look at the objects: *einen Kaffee*, *einen Orangensaft* and *einen Apfelkuchen* are masculine, so they take *einen*; *ein Wasser* and *ein Brötchen* are neuter and stay *ein*. *Ich habe keinen Hunger* uses *keinen* because *Hunger* is masculine. (*etwas* = something, *natürlich* = of course, *jeden Morgen* = every morning, *sieht … aus* = looks.) In a café, *einen Apfelkuchen, bitte* means a slice, not the whole cake; you can also say *ein Stück Apfelkuchen* (lesson 10).

## Pronunciation

- *Brötchen*: the *ö* is made by saying "ay" with rounded lips; *-chen* has the soft *ch* of *ich*.
- *Käse*: long *ä*, like the vowel in English "air" without the r: KÄ-se.
- *Ei* rhymes with English "eye"; *Eier* (eggs) is "EYE-er".
- *Gemüse* is stressed on the middle: ge-MÜ-se. *Kartoffel* too: kar-TOF-fel.
- *Frühstück*: stress on the first part, FRÜH-stück; the *h* is silent and makes the *ü* long.
- *isst* and *ist* sound the same; *nimmt* has a short *i*.

## Common mistakes

- *Ich möchte ein Kaffee.* — Wrong. *Kaffee* is masculine and the object, so: *Ich möchte einen Kaffee.*
- *Ich nehme einen Wasser.* — Wrong in the other direction. Only masculine words get *-en*; *Wasser* is neuter: *ein Wasser*.
- *Er esst Brot.* / *Du nehmst …* — Use the stem change: *er isst*, *du nimmst*.
- *Er möchtet …* — No *-t*: *er möchte*.
- *Ich bin Hunger.* / *Ich habe ein Hunger.* — Say *Ich habe Hunger*, with no article.
- *Ich gern esse Fisch.* — The verb comes second: *Ich esse gern Fisch.*
- *Das ist einen Apfel.* — After *sein*, no accusative: *Das ist ein Apfel.*

## Key points

- The accusative marks the direct object. Only masculine articles change: *den, einen, keinen* (and *meinen*, *deinen* …).
- Feminine, neuter and plural look the same as the nominative.
- *sein* is never followed by the accusative.
- *essen* and *nehmen* change e→i in the *du* and *er/sie/es* forms: *isst, nimmt*.
- *möchte* has no *-t* in the *er/sie/es* form. Use it to order politely.
- *gern* after the verb says you like doing something; *Ich habe Hunger / Durst* has no article.
