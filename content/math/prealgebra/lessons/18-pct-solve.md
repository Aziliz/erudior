## One equation, three questions

Nearly every percent problem is a version of one sentence: *"$A$ is $P$ percent of $B$."* The three numbers are the part, the percent and the whole, and a problem gives you two and asks for the third. This lesson shows how to recognize which is which, solve for the missing one in two ways (an equation or a proportion), and then handle percent increase and decrease, which trip up even careful people.

## The three parts

- The **base** $B$ is the whole, the amount you are taking a percent *of*. It usually follows the word "of."
- The **percent** $P$ is the rate, written with % or the word "percent."
- The **amount** $A$ (the part) is the result: the piece of the base that the percent describes. It usually sits next to "is."

With the percent written as a decimal or fraction, the sentence becomes an equation:

$$A = P \times B$$

"Is" means equals and "of" means multiply, just as you saw with fractions.

The same relationship as a proportion:

$$\frac{A}{B} = \frac{P}{100}$$

(part over whole equals percent over 100). Both setups give the same answers; use whichever you find clearer.

## Type 1: finding the amount

### Example 1

What is 35% of 240?

The base is 240 (after "of") and the percent is 35%. The amount is unknown.

Equation: $A = 0.35 \times 240 = 84$.

Proportion: $\dfrac{A}{240} = \dfrac{35}{100}$, so $100A = 8{,}400$ and $A = 84$.

*Mental math with 10%.* 10% of a number is that number divided by 10 (move the point one place left). Build other percents from it: 10% of 240 is 24, so 30% is 72, 5% is 12, and 35% is $72 + 12 = 84$.

## Type 2: finding the percent

### Example 2

18 is what percent of 72?

The base is 72, the amount is 18, and the percent is unknown.

Equation: $18 = P \times 72$, so $P = 18 \div 72 = 0.25 = 25\%$.

Proportion: $\dfrac{18}{72} = \dfrac{P}{100}$, so $72P = 1{,}800$ and $P = 25$, meaning 25%.

In words: the percent is part ÷ whole, then converted to a percent. That's the same as converting the fraction $\tfrac{18}{72} = \tfrac14$ to 25%.

## Type 3: finding the base

### Example 3

42 is 60% of what number?

The amount is 42 and the percent is 60%; the base ("of what number") is unknown.

Equation: $42 = 0.6 \times B$, so $B = 42 \div 0.6 = 70$.

Proportion: $\dfrac{42}{B} = \dfrac{60}{100}$, so $60B = 4{,}200$ and $B = 70$.

Check: 60% of 70 is $0.6 \times 70 = 42$. ✓ Since 42 is *less than* 100% of the base, the base must be bigger than 42, which it is.

## Recognizing the type in a word problem

### Example 4

A survey found that 312 of the people asked, or 39%, preferred the morning bus. How many people were surveyed?

The total surveyed is the base (39% *of* the people surveyed), and 312 is the amount. So $312 = 0.39B$ and $B = 312 \div 0.39 = 800$ people.

### Example 5

A basketball player made 27 of 45 free throws. What percent did she make?

The whole is 45 attempts; the part is 27 made. $27 \div 45 = 0.6 = 60\%$.

## Percent change

When a quantity changes from an original value to a new value, the **percent change** compares the size of the change to the *original*:

$$\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100\%$$

If the result is positive it's a **percent increase**; if negative, a **percent decrease** (usually stated as a positive number with the word "decrease").

### Example 6

A town's population grew from 12,500 to 14,000. What was the percent increase?

Change: $14{,}000 - 12{,}500 = 1{,}500$. Divide by the *original*: $1{,}500 \div 12{,}500 = 0.12 = 12\%$ increase.

### Example 7

A jacket's price dropped from \$80 to \$62. What was the percent decrease?

Change: $62 - 80 = -18$. Divide by the original: $-18 \div 80 = -0.225$, a 22.5% decrease.

Why the original and not the new value? Because "percent change" answers "by what percent of what we started with did it change?" Dividing by 62 would answer a different question and give about 29%, which is wrong.

### Finding a new value after a percent change

Write the percent of change as a decimal $r$ (15% is $r = 0.15$). To increase a number by that percent, multiply it by $(1 + r)$; to decrease it, multiply by $(1 - r)$. A 15% raise on \$40,000 gives $40{,}000 \times 1.15 = 46{,}000$, which matches $40{,}000 + 0.15 \times 40{,}000$.

### Up and down don't cancel

### Example 8

A stock worth \$100 rises 20%, then falls 20%. What is it worth now?

After the rise: $100 \times 1.2 = 120$. After the fall: $120 \times 0.8 = 96$. It ends at \$96, not \$100, because the 20% decrease is taken of the larger value, 120. Percent changes don't simply add.

## Common mistakes

- *Confusing the base.* The base follows "of." In "what percent of 50 is 20," the base is 50.
- *Forgetting to convert the percent* to a decimal: 35% of 240 is $0.35 \times 240$, not $35 \times 240$.
- *Dividing by the new value* in a percent change. Always divide by the original.
- *Reporting the decimal as the percent:* $18 \div 72 = 0.25$ means 25%, not 0.25%.
- *Adding successive percent changes:* +20% then −20% is a 4% net decrease, not 0%.

## Key points

- Amount = percent × base, or $\tfrac{A}{B} = \tfrac{P}{100}$.
- Find the amount by multiplying, the percent by dividing part by whole, the base by dividing amount by percent.
- Percent change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100%.
- Increase by $r$: multiply by $1 + r$. Decrease by $r$: multiply by $1 - r$.
- Successive percent changes multiply; they don't add.
