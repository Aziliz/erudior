## Is the relationship real?

In Module 3, a least-squares line described a sample. But a different sample would give a
different slope. If we regress rail-wear depth on tonnage for 20 segments and get a slope of
2.35, is there really a relationship in the population of all segments — or could a slope this
large arise by chance when the true slope is 0? Inference for regression answers with a t test
and a t interval for the slope, built exactly like those for a mean.

*AP exam scope:* inference for the regression slope was the last unit of AP Statistics until
the framework revision that took effect in the 2026–27 school year, which removed it. It is a
standard topic in introductory college statistics, and it is the natural next step after the
t procedures for means.

## The regression model

Think of the data as generated by a population line plus random scatter:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon,$$

where $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are the population intercept and slope, and the error
$\varepsilon$ has mean 0 and standard deviation $\sigma$ at every $x$. The **population
regression line** $\mu_y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ gives the mean response at each $x$. The sample
LSRL $\hat{y} = a + bx$ estimates it: $b$ estimates $\beta_1$, $a$ estimates $\beta_0$, and the
residual standard deviation $s = \sqrt{\text{SSE}/(n-2)}$ estimates $\sigma$.

### Conditions (LINER)

- L — *Linear*: the true relationship is linear (scatterplot and residual plot show no curve).
- I — *Independent*: observations are independent (10% condition if sampling without replacement).
- N — *Normal*: for each $x$, responses vary normally about the line (residuals roughly normal,
  no strong skew or outliers — less important for large $n$).
- E — *Equal SD*: the spread about the line is the same for all $x$ (no fan shape in the residual plot).
- R — *Random*: data from a random sample or randomized experiment.

## The sampling distribution of b

When the conditions hold, the slope $b$ is unbiased for $\beta_1$, approximately normal, with
standard deviation $\sigma_b = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sigma_x\sqrt{n}}$. Its standard error is

$$SE_b = \frac{s}{s_x\sqrt{n-1}},$$

where $s$ is the residual standard deviation and $s_x$ the standard deviation of the $x$ values.
This formula shows what makes a slope precise: little scatter about the line (small $s$), widely
spread $x$ values (large $s_x$), and more data (large $n$).

## t test and interval for the slope

To test $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ (no linear relationship):

$$t = \frac{b - 0}{SE_b}, \qquad \text{df} = n - 2.$$

A confidence interval for $\beta_1$:

$$b \pm t^* SE_b, \qquad \text{df} = n - 2.$$

Why $n - 2$? Two parameters (slope and intercept) were estimated before computing residuals.

## Reading computer output

Software prints a table like this (wear depth in mm vs. tonnage in millions of tons, $n = 20$):

| Predictor | Coef | SE Coef | T | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constant | 1.84 | 1.10 | 1.67 | 0.112 |
| Tonnage | 2.35 | 0.82 | 2.87 | 0.010 |

$s = 2.61$, R-sq $= 31.3\%$.

- The slope is $b = 2.35$ (row "Tonnage", column "Coef"); $SE_b = 0.82$.
- T $= 2.35/0.82 \approx 2.87$, and P is the **two-sided** p-value for $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$.
- The "Constant" row tests the intercept, which is rarely of interest.

### Example 1

Using the output: is there convincing evidence at $\alpha = 0.05$ of a positive linear
relationship between tonnage and wear? Give a 95% interval for the slope.

1. *State*: $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$, $H_a: \beta_1 > 0$, where $\beta_1$ is the slope of the population
   regression line of wear on tonnage.
2. *Plan*: t test for slope; assume LINER conditions were checked with the scatterplot and
   residual plot.
3. *Do*: $t = 2.87$, df $= 18$. The output's two-sided p-value 0.010 halves to about 0.005 for
   the one-sided test (the sign of $b$ matches $H_a$).
4. *Conclude*: $0.005 < 0.05$: reject $H_0$. Convincing evidence that wear increases with tonnage.
5. *Interval*: $t^* = 2.101$ (df 18): $2.35 \pm 2.101(0.82) = 2.35 \pm 1.72 = (0.63, 4.07)$ mm per
   million tons. We are 95% confident that each additional million tons is associated with
   between 0.63 and 4.07 mm more average wear.

### Example 2: computing SE from summaries

For the five-point training data in Module 3 ($\hat{y} = 2.6 + 0.8x$): SSE $= 3.6$, so
$s = \sqrt{3.6/3} \approx 1.095$; $s_x = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.581$; $n = 5$.

$SE_b = \dfrac{1.095}{1.581\sqrt{4}} \approx 0.346$; $t = 0.8/0.346 \approx 2.31$, df $= 3$,
two-sided p-value $\approx 0.10$.

Despite $r = 0.8$, five points are not enough for convincing evidence of a nonzero slope at
$\alpha = 0.05$. Small samples can produce impressive-looking correlations by chance.

## What the test does and doesn't show

- A significant slope shows a linear *association* in the population. Causation still needs
  a randomized experiment.
- A non-significant slope doesn't prove there's no relationship — it might be curved (check
  the scatterplot), or the sample might be too small.
- Testing $\beta_1 = 0$ is equivalent to testing that the population correlation is 0.

## Common mistakes

- Using df $= n - 1$ instead of $n - 2$.
- Reading the intercept's row of computer output instead of the slope's.
- Forgetting that software p-values are two-sided.
- Writing hypotheses about $b$ instead of $\beta_1$.
- Skipping the residual plot: a curved pattern or fan shape invalidates the inference.

## Key points

- Model: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma)$; conditions LINER.
- $SE_b = s/(s_x\sqrt{n-1})$; $t = b/SE_b$; interval $b \pm t^* SE_b$; df $= n - 2$.
- Computer output: slope row gives $b$, $SE_b$, $t$ and the two-sided p-value.
- Significant slope = evidence of linear association, not necessarily causation.
