## Building with carbon

The last lesson ended with water, the medium of life. Now we turn to what is dissolved in and built around that water: the large carbon-based molecules that make up most of a cell's dry mass. Carbon is ideal for building, because each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds. Carbon atoms link into chains, branches and rings, with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and other atoms attached, giving an almost unlimited variety of shapes. Molecules built around carbon are called organic molecules.

Biologists sort the large molecules of life into four classes: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Each class has a characteristic building block, a characteristic set of elements and characteristic jobs. Learning these patterns lets you predict what a molecule does from what it is made of.

## Monomers, polymers and the water rule

Three of the four classes are polymers. A **monomer** is a small molecule that can link to others like it; a **polymer** is a long chain of monomers bonded together, like a train made of cars.

Cells build polymers by **dehydration synthesis**: two monomers are joined by a covalent bond, and in the process a molecule of water is removed (one monomer loses an –OH, the other loses an –H). Cells break polymers apart by **hydrolysis** ("splitting with water"): a water molecule is added across the bond, separating the monomers. Digestion is mostly hydrolysis. Specific enzymes speed up both reactions, as the next lesson explains.

The bookkeeping is simple. Joining $n$ monomers in a single chain makes $n - 1$ bonds, so it releases $n - 1$ water molecules. Breaking the chain back into monomers uses $n - 1$ water molecules.

### Example 1: Counting water and mass

Glucose has a molar mass of 180 g/mol and water 18 g/mol. (a) What is the molar mass of a disaccharide made from two glucose molecules? (b) A short chain of 10 glucose units is built by dehydration synthesis. How many water molecules are released, and what is the mass of the chain?

(a) One bond forms and one water leaves: $180 + 180 - 18 = 342$ g/mol. (Maltose, made of two glucoses, and sucrose, made of glucose and fructose, both have formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ and mass 342.)

(b) Ten monomers make $10 - 1 = 9$ bonds, releasing 9 water molecules. Mass $= 10 \times 180 - 9 \times 18 = 1800 - 162 = 1638$ g/mol.

The usual mistake is to subtract 10 waters instead of 9. Picture three cars in a train: two couplings, not three.

## Carbohydrates

A **carbohydrate** is a sugar or a polymer of sugars, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, typically in about a 1:2:1 ratio (glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆). Carbohydrates are the main short-term energy source for cells and also serve as building material.

- *Monosaccharides* are single sugars: glucose (the fuel most cells burn), fructose (fruit sugar) and galactose.
- *Disaccharides* are two sugars joined: sucrose (table sugar), lactose (milk sugar), maltose.
- *Polysaccharides* are long chains, often hundreds or thousands of glucose units. Starch stores energy in plants (potatoes, grains). Glycogen stores energy in animals, mainly in the liver and muscles. Cellulose builds plant cell walls; it is the most abundant organic molecule on Earth. Chitin forms the exoskeletons of insects and the walls of fungi.

Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose, yet you can digest one and not the other. The glucose units in cellulose are linked in a different orientation, and human enzymes cannot hydrolyze that bond. Cows and termites can live on cellulose only because microbes in their guts make the needed enzymes. Same monomer, different linkage, completely different properties: structure determines function.

## Lipids

A **lipid** is a member of a diverse group of molecules that are mostly nonpolar and therefore do not dissolve in water. Lipids are not true polymers, because they are not built from repeating identical monomers. They are made mainly of carbon and hydrogen, with relatively little oxygen.

- *Fats and oils (triglycerides)* consist of one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acid chains. They store energy compactly: about 9 kcal per gram, compared with about 4 kcal per gram for carbohydrates and proteins. They also insulate and cushion organs. In a *saturated* fatty acid every carbon holds as many hydrogens as possible, and the chain is straight; the chains pack tightly, so saturated fats such as butter are solid at room temperature. An *unsaturated* fatty acid has one or more carbon–carbon double bonds that, in the usual natural (cis) form, put kinks in the chain; the chains cannot pack tightly, so unsaturated fats such as olive oil are liquid.
- *Phospholipids* have two fatty acid tails and a phosphate-containing head. The head is polar and the tails are not, so phospholipids form the double layer of every cell membrane (Lesson 9).
- *Steroids* have four fused carbon rings. Cholesterol stiffens animal cell membranes and is the starting material for hormones such as testosterone and estrogen.

## Proteins

A **protein** is a polymer of amino acids folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. An **amino acid** has a central carbon bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen and a side chain called the R group. Twenty different R groups, some polar, some nonpolar, some charged, give the 20 common amino acids their different properties. Amino acids join by dehydration synthesis; the bond between them is a peptide bond, and a chain of amino acids is a polypeptide. Proteins contain C, H, O and N, and most also contain sulfur.

![The general structure of an amino acid: a central carbon atom bonded to a hydrogen above, an H2N amino group on the left, a COOH carboxyl group on the right (a carbon double-bonded to O and bonded to OH), and an R group, the side chain, below. Labels name each part and note that the R group differs among the 20 amino acids.](images/m1-amino-acid.svg)

The picture labels the four groups attached to the central carbon. Only the R group changes from one amino acid to another. When two join, the carboxyl group of one meets the amino group of the next and a water leaves, as in the diagram above.

Proteins do more kinds of jobs than any other class: enzymes speed up reactions, hemoglobin carries oxygen, antibodies fight infection, collagen gives structure, actin and myosin move muscles, and insulin carries signals.

A protein's job depends on its shape, which is described at four levels:

1. *Primary structure*: the sequence of amino acids, set by a gene.
2. *Secondary structure*: local coils (α helices) and folded sheets (β sheets), held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
3. *Tertiary structure*: the overall 3-D fold of one chain, held by interactions between R groups.
4. *Quaternary structure*: two or more chains assembled together. Hemoglobin has four.

![A four-panel diagram. Panel 1, primary: a chain of beads labeled Met, Ala, Lys, Gly, Leu, Ser, Cys, joined by peptide bonds. Panel 2, secondary: a coiled alpha helix and a beta sheet of three side-by-side arrows linked by dashed hydrogen bonds. Panel 3, tertiary: one chain, including a helix and a strand, folded into a compact shape. Panel 4, quaternary: four subunits, two alpha and two beta, packed together as in hemoglobin.](images/m1-protein-levels.svg)

In the picture, each level is built from the one before it.

How do we know the sequence determines the shape? In the late 1950s Christian Anfinsen treated the enzyme ribonuclease with chemicals that broke its internal bonds and unfolded it completely; it lost all activity. When he slowly removed the chemicals, the chains refolded on their own into the correct shape, and nearly all the activity returned. No other information was present, so the amino acid sequence alone must hold the instructions for folding. A single change in sequence can matter enormously: in sickle cell disease, one amino acid substitution in hemoglobin changes how the molecules stack, distorting red blood cells.

## Nucleic acids

A **nucleic acid** is a polymer of nucleotides that stores and transmits genetic information. Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P.

- **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores the hereditary instructions. Its sugar is deoxyribose and its bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). It is usually a double strand.
- **RNA** (ribonucleic acid) carries out the instructions, chiefly by helping build proteins. Its sugar is ribose, it uses uracil (U) in place of thymine, and it is usually single-stranded.

One nucleotide deserves a mention now. ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is built like an RNA nucleotide, with the base adenine and the sugar ribose, but it carries a chain of three phosphate groups instead of one. It is the cell's main short-term energy carrier (Module 3). Module 6 covers DNA and RNA in depth.

## Identifying the class

| Class | Monomer | Elements | Main jobs | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharide | C, H, O (≈1:2:1) | Quick energy, structure | Glucose, starch, cellulose |
| Lipids | None (glycerol + fatty acids for fats) | C, H, little O; P in phospholipids | Long-term energy, membranes, hormones | Oils, phospholipids, cholesterol |
| Proteins | Amino acid | C, H, O, N (often S) | Enzymes, transport, structure, signals | Hemoglobin, collagen, insulin |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotide | C, H, O, N, P | Store and express genetic information | DNA, RNA |

### Example 2: Identify the molecule

An unknown molecule is analyzed. It contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur, but no phosphorus. Hydrolysis breaks it into about 300 small molecules of 20 different kinds. What is it?

1. Nitrogen rules out ordinary carbohydrates and fats.
2. No phosphorus makes a nucleic acid unlikely, since every nucleotide contains phosphate.
3. Sulfur is characteristic of proteins (from the amino acids cysteine and methionine).
4. Twenty kinds of monomer matches the 20 amino acids; four kinds would suggest a nucleic acid; one kind would suggest a polysaccharide such as starch.

It is a protein of about 300 amino acids.

### Example 3: Food test results

Standard chemical indicators detect each class: Benedict's solution turns from blue to green, orange or brick-red when heated with simple sugars such as glucose; iodine solution turns blue-black with starch; biuret reagent turns violet with protein; lipids leave a translucent spot on brown paper (or stain red with Sudan III). A student tests four samples:

| Sample | Benedict's | Iodine | Biuret | Paper spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | brick-red | brown (no change) | blue (no change) | none |
| B | blue (no change) | blue-black | blue (no change) | none |
| C | blue (no change) | brown (no change) | violet | none |
| D | blue (no change) | brown (no change) | blue (no change) | translucent |

Sample A contains a simple sugar (could be glucose solution or fruit juice). B contains starch (a potato extract). C contains protein (egg white). D contains lipid (vegetable oil). A distilled-water sample tested the same way is the negative control: it shows what "no change" looks like for each reagent.

## Common mistakes

- *Subtracting $n$ waters for $n$ monomers.* A chain of $n$ monomers has $n - 1$ bonds.
- *Mixing up the two reactions.* Dehydration synthesis builds and releases water; hydrolysis breaks and uses water.
- *Calling lipids polymers.* They lack a repeating monomer.
- *Thinking starch and cellulose differ in their sugars.* Both are glucose; the linkage differs.
- *Forgetting which classes contain N and P.* Proteins have N (and often S); nucleic acids have N and P; ordinary carbohydrates such as sugars, starch and cellulose have neither (chitin, a sugar polymer with nitrogen added, is the notable exception).

## Key points

- **Monomers** link into **polymers** by **dehydration synthesis** (water released) and are split by **hydrolysis** (water added); $n$ monomers → $n - 1$ water molecules.
- **Carbohydrates**: sugars and polysaccharides, for energy and structure.
- **Lipids**: nonpolar fats, phospholipids and steroids, for long-term energy storage, membranes and hormones.
- **Proteins**: chains of 20 kinds of **amino acid**; shape (from sequence) determines function.
- **Nucleic acids**: **DNA** and **RNA**, polymers of nucleotides that store and use genetic information.
