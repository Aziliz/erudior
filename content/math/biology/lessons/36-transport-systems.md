## Supply and removal

The last lesson described how the body senses and corrects changes. This one covers three systems that do much of the correcting by moving materials. Every cell needs oxygen and glucose delivered and carbon dioxide and nitrogen wastes removed. In a single cell, diffusion handles this, but diffusion is fast only over tiny distances (the surface-area-to-volume problem from the cell module). A large animal solves it with a pump and pipes (the circulatory system), a huge exchange surface for gases (the respiratory system), and a filter that cleans the blood (the excretory system).

## The heart and double circulation

The human heart is a muscular pump with four chambers: two thin-walled atria on top that receive blood, and two thick-walled ventricles below that pump it out. One-way valves between chambers and at the exits keep blood moving forward; their closing makes the "lub-dub" sounds.

The heart is really two pumps side by side, so blood makes two loops:

- Pulmonary circuit: the right ventricle pumps oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen and drops off carbon dioxide, then returns to the left atrium.
- Systemic circuit: the left ventricle pumps oxygen-rich blood to every other tissue, and it returns oxygen-poor to the right atrium.

Tracing one red blood cell: right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body capillaries → veins → back to the right atrium. The left ventricle's wall is the thickest because it pushes blood through the whole body. Keeping the two circuits separate lets the systemic one run at high pressure.

The heartbeat starts in the heart itself: a patch of specialized muscle in the right atrium, the pacemaker (sinoatrial node), fires on its own about 60–100 times a minute at rest. Nerves and hormones such as adrenaline speed it up or slow it down.

## Vessels

| Vessel | Carries blood | Wall | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Artery** | Away from the heart | Thick, muscular, elastic | High pressure; the pulse you feel |
| **Capillary** | Between arteries and veins | One cell thick | Where exchange with tissues happens |
| **Vein** | Toward the heart | Thinner, less muscle | Low pressure; valves stop backflow |

Direction, not oxygen, defines the vessel: the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood (away from the heart, to the lungs), and the pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood. Capillaries are so narrow that red blood cells pass in single file, and so numerous that almost no cell is more than a few cell-widths from one. In the limbs, contracting muscles squeeze the veins, and the valves let blood go only toward the heart.

## Blood

Blood is about 55% plasma (water carrying dissolved nutrients, salts, hormones, proteins and most of the carbon dioxide, as bicarbonate) and 45% cells:

- Red blood cells carry oxygen. Each is packed with roughly 250 million molecules of **hemoglobin**, an iron-containing protein that binds up to four oxygen molecules. Human red blood cells lose their nucleus as they mature, leaving more room for hemoglobin.
- White blood cells defend the body (lesson 37).
- Platelets, cell fragments, start blood clotting.

## Cardiac output

The volume of blood the heart pumps per minute is the **cardiac output**:

$$\text{cardiac output} = \text{heart rate} \times \text{stroke volume},$$

where stroke volume is the volume pumped by one ventricle in one beat. At rest, about 70 beats/min $\times$ 70 mL = 4.9 L/min, roughly the body's entire blood volume every minute. During hard exercise, both heart rate and stroke volume rise, and cardiac output can reach 20 L/min or more in a trained athlete.

### Example 1: Harvey's argument

Before 1628, physicians followed Galen, who taught that the liver constantly made new blood from food and that tissues used it up. William Harvey disproved this with an observation and a calculation. Redo it with modern numbers: a resting heart pumps about 70 mL per beat at 72 beats per minute. How much blood does it pump in a day, and what does that imply?

- Per minute: $70 \times 72 = 5040$ mL $\approx 5.0$ L.
- Per day: $5.04 \text{ L/min} \times 1440 \text{ min} \approx 7260$ L.

No one eats or drinks 7,000 liters a day, and the body holds only about 5 L of blood. The same blood must go round and round. Harvey also showed that valves in veins let blood flow only toward the heart. He predicted that tiny connecting vessels must link arteries to veins; Marcello Malpighi saw these capillaries with a microscope in 1661.

## The respiratory system

Air enters through the nose and mouth, passes the pharynx and larynx, and goes down the trachea, which branches into two bronchi, then into thousands of narrower bronchioles, ending in clusters of tiny air sacs called **alveoli**. The adult lungs hold several hundred million alveoli with a total surface area of very roughly 70 m², a large fraction of a tennis court, folded into the chest.

Gas exchange is pure diffusion. Alveolar walls and capillary walls are each one thin cell, so air and blood are separated by well under a micrometer. Oxygen is more concentrated in alveolar air than in the blood arriving from the body, so it diffuses into the blood and binds hemoglobin; carbon dioxide is more concentrated in that blood, so it diffuses out into the alveoli. In the body's tissues, the gradients run the other way.

Breathing moves air by changing pressure. To inhale, the diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs) contracts and flattens, and rib muscles lift the rib cage; the chest volume increases, pressure in the lungs drops below air pressure, and air flows in. Quiet exhaling is mostly passive: the muscles relax and the elastic lungs recoil.

The drive to breathe comes mainly from carbon dioxide, not from a lack of oxygen. Carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid in the blood; sensors in the brainstem (the medulla) and in major arteries detect the resulting drop in pH and increase the breathing rate and depth. That's negative feedback: more breathing removes more carbon dioxide, and pH returns to normal.

### Example 2: ventilation and dead space

A student at rest breathes 12 times a minute, taking in 500 mL per breath (the tidal volume). About 150 mL of each breath only fills the airways (trachea, bronchi), where no gas exchange happens; this is called dead space. Find the total air moved per minute and the air that actually reaches the alveoli.

- Minute ventilation $= 500 \text{ mL} \times 12 = 6000$ mL/min $= 6.0$ L/min.
- Alveolar ventilation $= (500 - 150) \times 12 = 4200$ mL/min $= 4.2$ L/min.

Now suppose the same student breathes shallow and fast: 250 mL at 24 breaths per minute. Minute ventilation is unchanged at 6.0 L/min, but alveolar ventilation falls to $(250 - 150) \times 24 = 2400$ mL/min. Deep, slow breaths ventilate the alveoli far better than shallow, fast ones.

### Oxygen and metabolic rate

Because cells use oxygen to release energy from food (cellular respiration), oxygen consumption measures metabolic rate. About 20 kJ of energy is released for each liter of oxygen used, whatever mix of food is being burned. The **basal metabolic rate** (BMR) is the energy used at complete rest, awake, after fasting. A typical adult at rest uses about 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram per minute, about 0.25 L/min for a 70 kg person, which gives $0.25 \times 20 = 5$ kJ per minute, or about 7,200 kJ (about 1,700 kcal) per day just to stay alive. Exercise can raise oxygen use more than tenfold.

## The excretory system

Breaking down amino acids releases nitrogen as ammonia, which is toxic. The liver converts it to urea, which is far less toxic, and the kidneys remove it in urine. The kidneys also regulate water, salts and blood pH, so they are homeostatic organs as much as waste removers.

Each kidney contains about a million **nephrons**, its filtering units. A nephron works in three steps:

1. Filtration: at the glomerulus, a ball of capillaries, blood pressure pushes water and small molecules (glucose, salts, urea, amino acids) into the nephron. Blood cells and large proteins stay in the blood.
2. Reabsorption: as this filtrate flows along the tubule, needed substances (nearly all the glucose and amino acids, most water and salts) are moved back into surrounding capillaries.
3. Secretion: some wastes and excess ions (hydrogen, potassium, some drugs) are moved from the blood into the tubule.

What remains drains through collecting ducts to the ureters, bladder and out. The amount of water reabsorbed is set by negative feedback: when the blood becomes too concentrated, the hypothalamus senses it and the pituitary releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water. More water returns to the blood and the urine becomes concentrated and darker. When you drink a lot, ADH falls and you make plenty of dilute urine.

### Example 3: how much is reabsorbed?

The kidneys filter about 180 L of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 L of urine. What percentage of the filtered water is reabsorbed?

- Reabsorbed $= 180 - 1.5 = 178.5$ L.
- Percentage $= 178.5 / 180 \times 100 \approx 99.2\%$.

Filtering everything small and then taking back what's needed seems wasteful, but it means the kidney doesn't need to recognize every possible waste or toxin. Anything not specifically reclaimed is removed. Glucose normally appears in urine only when blood glucose is so high (as in untreated diabetes) that the carriers reabsorbing it are saturated.

## Common mistakes

- Defining arteries as vessels with oxygen-rich blood. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, whatever its oxygen content.
- Thinking the left side of the heart pumps to the lungs. The right side does; the left side pumps to the body.
- Saying low oxygen is the main breathing trigger. Rising carbon dioxide (falling pH) is.
- Thinking the lungs actively pull air in. The diaphragm and rib muscles enlarge the chest, and air pressure pushes air in.
- Believing kidneys filter only wastes. They filter almost everything small and reabsorb what the body needs.

## Key points

- Four-chambered heart, double circulation: right side to the lungs, left side to the body.
- Arteries (away, thick), capillaries (exchange, one cell thick), veins (toward, valves).
- Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, about 5 L/min at rest.
- Alveoli give a huge, thin surface for diffusion; carbon dioxide level drives breathing.
- Oxygen use measures metabolic rate: about 20 kJ per liter of oxygen.
- Nephrons filter, reabsorb and secrete; ADH controls water reabsorption by negative feedback.
