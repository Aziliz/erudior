## Two basic plans

The last lesson showed how microscopes revealed cells and led to cell theory. Once biologists could see cells clearly, especially with the electron microscope, they found that every cell on Earth is built on one of two basic plans. Prokaryotic cells are small and simply organized, with no nucleus. Eukaryotic cells are larger and divided into compartments, with their DNA enclosed in a nucleus. This lesson compares the two, then looks at viruses, which are not cells at all.

## What every cell has

Whatever the plan, every cell has four things:

1. A **plasma membrane** (cell membrane), a thin, flexible boundary of lipids and proteins that separates the inside of the cell from its surroundings and controls what enters and leaves (Lesson 9).
2. Cytoplasm, the jelly-like, water-based interior where much of the cell's chemistry happens.
3. DNA, the genetic instructions.
4. **Ribosomes**, small structures made of RNA and protein that read genetic instructions and build proteins. Ribosomes are not surrounded by a membrane, and every cell needs them, because every cell makes proteins.

These shared features are strong evidence that all cells descend from a common ancestor.

## Prokaryotic cells

A **prokaryote** is a single-celled organism whose cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. The word means "before the nucleus." Its DNA, usually one circular chromosome, sits in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, with no membrane around it. Many prokaryotes also carry plasmids, small extra loops of DNA that often hold useful genes, such as genes for antibiotic resistance, and can be passed between cells.

Typical prokaryotic features:

- *Size:* usually about 1–5 µm long.
- *Cell wall* outside the plasma membrane, which keeps the cell from bursting. In bacteria the wall contains peptidoglycan, a mesh of sugars and amino acids found nowhere else.
- Some have a sticky *capsule* outside the wall, which helps them attach to surfaces and evade immune cells.
- Many have *flagella* (long whip-like tails for swimming) and *pili* (short hair-like projections for attachment and for transferring DNA).
- Ribosomes that are smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes and differ in structure.

Prokaryotes fall into two very different domains. Bacteria include the familiar ones in soil, water and your gut (and a few that cause disease). Archaea look similar under the microscope but differ chemically: they have no peptidoglycan, their membranes are built from unusual lipids, and some of their machinery for reading genes resembles that of eukaryotes. Many archaea live in extreme places like hot springs and salt lakes, but they are also common in oceans, soils and the human gut.

The split was discovered in 1977 when Carl Woese compared the sequences of ribosomal RNA, a molecule every cell has, across many organisms. The "prokaryotes" fell into two groups as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes. Life is now divided into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. (Module 8 returns to classification.)

## Eukaryotic cells

A **eukaryote** is an organism whose cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles; the name means "true nucleus." Animals, plants, fungi and protists are all eukaryotes. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm across, ten or more times the diameter of a typical bacterium.

The key innovation is compartments. Internal membranes divide the cell into organelles, each with its own chemical environment: a nucleus holding the DNA, which is organized into several linear chromosomes; mitochondria that release energy from food; in plants and algae, chloroplasts that capture light; and a system of membranes that makes, packages and ships proteins and lipids. Separate compartments let incompatible reactions run side by side, the way a kitchen and a laundry room each work better behind their own door. Lesson 8 tours the organelles.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | No (DNA in nucleoid) | Yes |
| DNA | Usually one circular chromosome, plus plasmids | Several linear chromosomes in the nucleus |
| Membrane-bound organelles | None | Many (mitochondria, ER, Golgi…) |
| Ribosomes | Yes, smaller | Yes, larger |
| Typical size | 1–5 µm | 10–100 µm |
| Cell wall | Usually (peptidoglycan in bacteria) | Plants (cellulose), fungi (chitin); animals none |
| Organisms | Bacteria, archaea | Animals, plants, fungi, protists |

### Example 1: Classify the cell

A researcher studies three unknown cells with an electron microscope. Which is prokaryotic?

| Cell | Size | Nucleus | Mitochondria | Ribosomes | Cell wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 40 µm | Yes | Yes | Yes | Cellulose |
| Y | 2 µm | No | No | Yes | Peptidoglycan |
| Z | 15 µm | Yes | Yes | Yes | None |

1. A nucleus and mitochondria mean eukaryote, so X and Z are eukaryotic.
2. X has a cellulose wall, so it is probably a plant cell. Z has no wall at all, typical of an animal cell.
3. Y has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles, is small, and has a peptidoglycan wall: it is a bacterium, a prokaryote.

Note that every cell has ribosomes, so ribosomes cannot tell the types apart; a nucleus can.

### Example 2: Comparing sizes

A bacterium is 2 µm across and a human white blood cell is 12 µm across. Treating both as spheres, how many times larger is the white blood cell in diameter, and in volume?

1. Diameter ratio: $12 / 2 = 6$ times.
2. Volume of a sphere is proportional to the cube of its diameter, so the volume ratio is $6^3 = 216$.

The white blood cell has more than 200 times the volume of the bacterium, which is why it can engulf and digest bacteria whole. Size differences that look modest in diameter are huge in volume, an idea Lesson 10 develops.

## Viruses: not cells

A **virus** is a tiny infectious particle made of genetic material (DNA or RNA) inside a protein coat called a capsid. Some viruses, such as influenza and HIV, also have an outer envelope of membrane taken from the host cell they last left. Most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, far smaller than bacteria; only the largest can be seen with a light microscope.

Viruses were discovered by their small size. In the 1890s Dmitri Ivanovsky and then Martinus Beijerinck studied tobacco mosaic disease. They crushed infected leaves and pushed the sap through porcelain filters with pores fine enough to trap every known bacterium. The filtered sap still infected healthy plants. Beijerinck also showed that the agent multiplied only inside living plant tissue, never in nutrient broth by itself. Something smaller than any bacterium, and able to reproduce only inside living cells, was causing the disease. In 1935 Wendell Stanley crystallized the tobacco mosaic virus, something no living cell could survive.

A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no metabolism. It reproduces only by entering a host cell and using the cell's ribosomes, enzymes, energy and raw materials to make copies of itself. That is why most biologists do not consider viruses alive (Lesson 1), although they have genes and evolve, often very fast. Every kind of organism has viruses that infect it; viruses that infect bacteria are called bacteriophages. Module 8 covers how viruses reproduce and cause disease.

| | Bacterial cell | Virus |
|---|---|---|
| Made of a cell? | Yes | No |
| Genetic material | DNA | DNA or RNA |
| Ribosomes, metabolism | Yes | No |
| Reproduces | On its own, by dividing | Only inside a host cell |
| Typical size | 1–5 µm | 20–300 nm |
| Killed by antibiotics? | Many are | No |

### Example 3: Why antibiotics don't cure the flu

Penicillin works by blocking the enzymes that build peptidoglycan cell walls. Predict its effect on (a) a bacterial throat infection, (b) human cells and (c) the influenza virus.

(a) Growing bacteria cannot complete their walls, so they burst; penicillin can cure the infection (unless the strain is resistant).

(b) Human cells have no cell wall and no peptidoglycan, so the drug's target is absent and our cells are unharmed. Antibiotics are useful because they hit structures bacteria have and we lack: peptidoglycan walls, bacterial ribosomes.

(c) Influenza viruses have no cell wall, no ribosomes and no metabolism of their own: there is nothing for penicillin to act on. The virus is copied by the patient's own cells. Taking antibiotics for a viral infection does not help and adds to the spread of antibiotic resistance in bacteria. Viral infections are prevented with vaccines and sometimes treated with antiviral drugs that target viral enzymes.

## Common mistakes

- *Saying prokaryotes have no DNA or no ribosomes.* They have both; they lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
- *Saying only plants have cell walls.* Bacteria, archaea, fungi and plants have walls, made of different materials. Animal cells do not.
- *Treating archaea as a kind of bacteria.* They are prokaryotes but form their own domain.
- *Calling viruses cells or bacteria.* Viruses are much smaller, are not cells and cannot reproduce on their own.
- *Thinking antibiotics kill viruses.* They target bacterial structures that viruses do not have.

## Key points

- Every cell has a **plasma membrane**, cytoplasm, DNA and **ribosomes**.
- A **prokaryote** (bacteria and archaea) has no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; DNA sits in a nucleoid; cells are about 1–5 µm.
- A **eukaryote** (animals, plants, fungi, protists) has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; cells are about 10–100 µm.
- Life is divided into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
- A **virus** is genetic material in a protein capsid, about 20–300 nm; it has no metabolism or ribosomes and reproduces only inside a host cell, so antibiotics do not work on it.
