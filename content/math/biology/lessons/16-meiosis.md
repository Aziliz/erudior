## A division that halves

Mitosis, from lesson 14, copies a cell exactly: same chromosome number, same genes. That is perfect for growth and repair, but it creates a puzzle for sexual reproduction. A human egg and a human sperm combine to make a new person. If each carried 46 chromosomes, the child would have 92, the grandchild 184, and so on. That does not happen, so something must halve the chromosome number before fertilization.

Biologists in the 1880s saw this directly. In 1876 Oscar Hertwig, watching transparent sea urchin eggs, saw a sperm nucleus enter the egg and fuse with the egg's nucleus: fertilization is the joining of two nuclei. In 1883 Edouard van Beneden counted chromosomes in a roundworm (Ascaris) whose cells have very few: the egg nucleus and the sperm nucleus each brought 2 chromosomes, and the fertilized egg had 4. August Weismann then argued that a special "reduction division" must halve the number when sex cells form. That division is **meiosis**. This lesson explains how it works and why it makes every offspring genetically unique.

## Pairs of chromosomes

Look at the 46 chromosomes of a human body cell arranged by size and you will see they come in 23 pairs. One chromosome of each pair came from the mother's egg and one from the father's sperm. The two members of a pair are **homologous chromosomes** (homologs): they are the same size and shape, have their centromeres in the same place, and carry the same genes in the same order. They are not identical, though, because they can carry different versions of those genes; your mother's copy of a gene for a blood protein might differ slightly from your father's.

Of the 23 pairs, 22 pairs are autosomes, matched in everyone. The 23rd pair is the sex chromosomes: two X chromosomes in most females, an X and a Y in most males. The X and Y differ in size but pair up during meiosis through a small shared region. (Module 5 covers how sex chromosomes affect inheritance.)

A cell with two complete sets of chromosomes, one from each parent, is **diploid**, written 2n. A cell with one set is **haploid**, written n. In humans 2n = 46 and n = 23. Body cells are diploid. Sex cells, or **gametes** (eggs and sperm), are haploid. Fertilization joins two haploid gametes into a diploid zygote, the first cell of a new organism: n + n = 2n. Every species has its own number: fruit flies 2n = 8, dogs 2n = 78, garden peas 2n = 14.

## Meiosis step by step

Meiosis starts after a normal interphase, so the cell enters it with every chromosome duplicated into two sister chromatids. Then it divides twice: meiosis I and meiosis II.

*Meiosis I separates the homologous pairs.*

1. Prophase I. Chromosomes condense, and each pairs up with its homolog along their whole length. The paired homologs (four chromatids in all) form a tetrad. While paired, chromatids from the two homologs exchange matching segments, a process called **crossing over**. The X-shaped places where they cross can be seen under a microscope.
2. Metaphase I. The homologous pairs line up at the middle of the cell as pairs, side by side. Which homolog faces which pole is random for each pair.
3. Anaphase I. The homologs of each pair are pulled to opposite poles. Sister chromatids stay joined. This is the key difference from mitosis.
4. Telophase I and cytokinesis. Two cells form, each with one chromosome from every pair: they are haploid, although each chromosome still has two chromatids.

*Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids.* No DNA replication happens between the two divisions. Each haploid cell goes through prophase II, metaphase II (chromosomes line up singly), anaphase II (sister chromatids separate), and telophase II. Meiosis II works just like mitosis, but in a haploid cell.

The result: four haploid cells, each genetically different from the parent cell and from each other.

### Example 1

A fruit fly cell has 2n = 8. Track the number of chromosomes and chromatids from the start of meiosis I to the end of meiosis II.

Step 1: Before meiosis (after S phase): 8 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids, so 16 chromatids.

Step 2: Metaphase I: still 8 chromosomes (4 homologous pairs), 16 chromatids.

Step 3: After meiosis I: each of the 2 cells has 4 chromosomes (one from each pair), each still with 2 chromatids: 8 chromatids per cell. The cells are now haploid (n = 4).

Step 4: Anaphase II: in each cell the sister chromatids separate, so each pole receives 4 chromosomes of one chromatid each.

Step 5: After meiosis II: 4 cells, each with 4 single-chromatid chromosomes. Halved from 8, ready to combine with another gamete (4 + 4 = 8).

## Mitosis and meiosis compared

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Making gametes for sexual reproduction |
| Divisions | One | Two |
| Homologs pair up? | No | Yes, in prophase I |
| Crossing over? | No | Yes |
| Daughter cells | 2, diploid (2n → 2n) | 4, haploid (2n → n) |
| Genetically | Identical to parent | Different from parent and each other |

## Three sources of variation

Siblings with the same parents (other than identical twins) are never genetically identical. Meiosis and fertilization shuffle genes in three ways.

1. **Independent assortment**. At metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the others. For each pair there are 2 ways it can face the poles, so with n pairs the number of different chromosome combinations in a gamete is $2^n$. For humans, $2^{23} = 8{,}388{,}608$.
2. Crossing over. Exchanging segments between homologs makes recombinant chromosomes, mixing the mother's and father's versions of genes on the same chromosome. Several crossovers usually happen on each pair, multiplying the possibilities far beyond $2^{23}$.
3. Random fertilization. Any one of the millions of possible sperm can fertilize any one of the millions of possible eggs. Ignoring crossing over, one couple could produce $2^{23} \times 2^{23} \approx 7 \times 10^{13}$ (about 70 trillion) different chromosome combinations.

Why does variation matter? A population whose members differ is more likely to include some individuals who survive a new disease or a change in climate. This variation is the raw material for natural selection (module 7).

### Example 2

A plant species has 2n = 6. How many genetically different gametes can independent assortment alone produce? How many different chromosome combinations could a zygote have from two parents of this species (ignoring crossing over)?

Step 1: n = 3 pairs, so gamete combinations $= 2^3 = 8$.

Step 2: Each parent can make 8 kinds of gametes, so the zygote combinations number $8 \times 8 = 64$.

Step 3: Check by thinking about one pair: each pair lines up 2 ways, so 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Doubling the number of chromosome pairs to 6 would square the gamete count, giving 64, not double it.

## When meiosis goes wrong: nondisjunction

Sometimes a homologous pair (in meiosis I) or a pair of sister chromatids (in meiosis II) fails to separate. This is **nondisjunction**. The result is gametes with an extra chromosome (n + 1) or a missing one (n − 1). If such a gamete is fertilized, the zygote has 47 or 45 chromosomes instead of 46.

- Nondisjunction in meiosis I: both homologs go to one cell, so all four gametes are abnormal: two n + 1 and two n − 1.
- Nondisjunction in meiosis II: only one of the two cells is affected, so two gametes are normal, one is n + 1, and one is n − 1.

Most embryos with a missing or extra chromosome do not survive. A well-known exception is trisomy 21 (three copies of chromosome 21), which causes Down syndrome; people with Down syndrome share some physical traits and some degree of intellectual disability, and many live full lives into their 60s. The chance of nondisjunction rises with the age of the egg, which is why trisomy 21 is more common in children of older mothers. Module 5 returns to chromosome disorders and how karyotypes reveal them.

## Making eggs and sperm

In males, each cell that completes meiosis produces four sperm, and sperm are made continuously from puberty on. In females, meiosis divides the cytoplasm unequally: one large egg cell keeps nearly all the cytoplasm (stored nutrients for the early embryo), and the other products are tiny polar bodies that break down. So one meiosis yields one egg. Human egg cells begin meiosis before birth and pause in prophase I for years or decades, finishing only around ovulation and fertilization; that long pause is thought to be part of why nondisjunction rises with maternal age.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking meiosis II halves the chromosome number. The halving happens in meiosis I, when homologs separate.
- Calling homologous chromosomes identical. They carry the same genes but can carry different versions. Sister chromatids are the identical ones.
- Saying the DNA is copied again before meiosis II. It is copied only once, before meiosis I.
- Treating independent assortment as $2 \times n$. The count is $2^n$, because each pair's choice multiplies the possibilities.
- Thinking nondisjunction in meiosis I and II give the same results. Meiosis I nondisjunction makes all four gametes abnormal; meiosis II, only two.

## Key points

- Meiosis turns one diploid cell (2n) into four haploid gametes (n); fertilization restores 2n.
- Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, one from each parent; they pair and cross over in prophase I.
- Meiosis I separates homologs (the reduction step); meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
- Variation comes from crossing over, independent assortment ($2^n$ combinations), and random fertilization.
- Nondisjunction gives gametes with n + 1 or n − 1 chromosomes; trisomy 21 is one result.
- Sperm: four per meiosis. Egg: one per meiosis plus polar bodies.
