## Life on land

The last lesson ended with fungi helping the first plants onto land. Plants evolved from green algae, and they share those algae's chloroplasts (with chlorophylls a and b), cellulose cell walls and starch storage. Moving out of water solved some problems (more light, more carbon dioxide) and created others: how to keep from drying out, how to stand up without water's support, how to move water from soil to leaves, and how to reproduce without water for sperm to swim in. The four major plant groups can be read as successive answers to those problems.

## Four groups, four innovations

| Group | Examples | Vascular tissue? | Seeds? | Flowers and fruit? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nonvascular plants (bryophytes) | Mosses, liverworts | No | No | No |
| Seedless vascular plants | Ferns, horsetails | Yes | No | No |
| Gymnosperms | Pines, spruces, ginkgo | Yes | Yes, "naked" on cones | No |
| Angiosperms | Grasses, oaks, roses | Yes | Yes, enclosed in fruit | Yes |

- A waxy cuticle over leaves and stems slows water loss. Almost all land plants have one.
- Mosses lack true vascular tissue, so water moves only by slow diffusion and capillary action. That's why mosses stay a few centimeters tall and live in damp places, and why their sperm need a film of water to swim to the egg.
- Ferns have vascular tissue, tubes that carry water and food, which let them grow tall. They still need water for fertilization.
- **Gymnosperms** produce seeds on the scales of cones. A seed is a plant embryo packed with a food supply inside a protective coat: it can wait out drought or cold and be carried far away. Pollen, carried by wind, delivers sperm without any need for water.
- **Angiosperms**, the flowering plants, enclose their seeds in an ovary that ripens into a fruit. Flowers recruit animals to carry pollen, and fruits recruit animals to carry seeds. Angiosperms are by far the largest group, with roughly 300,000 species.

## Alternation of generations

Plant life cycles have a twist that animal life cycles lack. In **alternation of generations**, a plant species has two multicellular stages:

- the gametophyte, which is haploid and makes gametes (egg and sperm) by mitosis;
- the sporophyte, which is diploid and makes haploid spores by meiosis.

A spore grows into a gametophyte; gametes fuse into a zygote, which grows into a sporophyte, which makes spores, and the cycle repeats. Over plant evolution, the sporophyte became dominant. In a moss, the green carpet is the gametophyte, and the sporophyte is a small stalk growing out of it. In a fern, the leafy plant is the sporophyte. In seed plants, the gametophytes have shrunk to a few cells: a pollen grain is a male gametophyte, and the female gametophyte lives inside the ovule.

## How a plant is built

A flowering plant has three organs. Roots anchor it and absorb water and minerals, mostly through root hairs that multiply the absorbing surface. Stems hold leaves up to the light and connect roots to leaves. Leaves are the main sites of photosynthesis.

Running through all three is vascular tissue of two kinds:

- **Xylem** carries water and dissolved minerals *up* from the roots. Its conducting cells (tracheids and vessel elements) are dead at maturity: hollow tubes with walls stiffened by lignin, which is also what makes wood strong.
- **Phloem** carries sugars and other organic molecules from where they are made or stored (a source, such as a mature leaf) to where they are used or stored (a sink, such as a root, fruit or growing tip). Its conducting cells, sieve-tube elements, are alive. Phloem flow can go up or down.

On a leaf's surface, mostly underneath, are **stomata** (singular stoma): pores, each between two guard cells. When guard cells take up water and swell, they bow apart and the pore opens; when they lose water, it closes. Stomata let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, but every open stoma also lets water vapor out. Plants constantly trade water for carbon.

## How water reaches the top of a tree

A 100-meter redwood has no pump. The explanation, the cohesion-tension mechanism, rests on the properties of water from module 1.

1. **Transpiration**: water evaporates from cells inside the leaf and diffuses out through open stomata.
2. That loss pulls on the water left behind. Water molecules cling to each other by hydrogen bonds (cohesion), so the pull is passed down the unbroken columns of water in the xylem, putting them under tension.
3. Water also sticks to the xylem walls (adhesion), which helps keep the thin columns from breaking.
4. At the roots, water enters by osmosis to replace what was pulled up.

The energy comes from the sun, which drives evaporation; the plant spends no ATP lifting water. The vast majority of the water a plant absorbs, well over 90%, is lost by transpiration, and only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis. Transpiration also cools leaves and carries minerals up.

Anything that speeds evaporation speeds transpiration: higher temperature, wind, low humidity and bright light (which opens stomata). When water runs short, a hormone called abscisic acid makes guard cells close the stomata, saving water at the cost of photosynthesis.

### Example 1: a potometer

A potometer measures how fast a cut shoot takes up water: the shoot sits in a sealed water-filled tube, and an air bubble in a narrow capillary moves as water is drawn in. In one run, the bubble moves 42 mm in 6 minutes along a capillary whose cross-section is 0.75 mm². Find the rate of water uptake.

- Volume taken up $=$ area $\times$ distance $= 0.75 \times 42 = 31.5$ mm³ (31.5 microliters, since 1 mm³ = 1 µL).
- Rate $= 31.5 / 6 = 5.25$ mm³/min.

Water uptake approximately equals transpiration, since only a little water is used or stored. Now read the bar graph: the fan raised uptake from 8 to 21 mm³/min because moving air sweeps humid air away from the leaf. Enclosing the shoot in a plastic bag dropped it to 3, because the air inside became saturated with water vapor and evaporation nearly stopped.

### Example 2: counting stomata

A student paints clear nail polish on the underside of a leaf, peels it off, and views the imprint at high power. The field of view is 0.40 mm in diameter, and she counts 28 stomata in it. What is the stomatal density?

- Field area $= \pi r^2 = \pi (0.20)^2 = 0.126$ mm².
- Density $= 28 / 0.126 \approx 223$ stomata per mm².

Remember to halve the diameter; using 0.40 mm as the radius makes the area four times too big.

## Plant reproduction

In a flower, the male parts are stamens, each an anther (which makes pollen) on a stalk called a filament. The female part is the carpel (or pistil): a sticky stigma that catches pollen, a style, and an ovary containing ovules. Petals often attract animals.

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma, by wind, insects, birds, bats or water. A pollen grain on a compatible stigma grows a tube down through the style to an ovule, and its sperm cells travel down the tube. Angiosperms then undergo double fertilization: one sperm fertilizes the egg to form the diploid zygote, and the other joins two other nuclei to form triploid endosperm, the seed's food store. The ovule becomes the seed and the ovary wall becomes the fruit. That's why a tomato, a cucumber and a bean pod are botanically fruits: each holds seeds and grew from an ovary.

Many plants also reproduce asexually: strawberry runners, potato tubers, grass rhizomes, and cuttings that root. The offspring are genetically identical clones.

## Responding without a nervous system

Plants respond to their environment mostly by changing how they grow. A **tropism** is growth toward or away from a stimulus: phototropism (toward light), gravitropism (roots down, shoots up), thigmotropism (vines coiling on touch).

Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied phototropism in grass seedlings in 1880. A seedling lit from one side bent toward the light, but not if its tip was covered with a cap; it still bent if the cap was clear, or if only the lower part was shaded. They concluded that the tip senses light and sends some "influence" down to the part that bends. In 1926 Frits Went cut off tips, set them on agar blocks, then placed the blocks off-center on decapitated seedlings, which bent away from the block side even in darkness. The influence was a chemical that had diffused into the agar: the hormone now called auxin. Light on one side causes auxin to move to the shaded side, where it makes cells elongate more, so the stem curves toward the light.

Other plant hormones include gibberellins (stem growth, seed germination), ethylene (fruit ripening and leaf drop) and abscisic acid (stomatal closing, seed dormancy).

## Common mistakes

- Thinking plants "suck" water up with energy. Transpiration, powered by solar evaporation, pulls it.
- Mixing up xylem and phloem. Xylem carries water up; phloem carries sugar from source to sink.
- Saying pollination and fertilization are the same. Pollination moves pollen; fertilization is the fusion of sperm and egg.
- Assuming gymnosperms have fruit. Their seeds sit exposed on cone scales.

## Key points

- Plant groups: mosses (nonvascular), ferns (vascular, no seeds), gymnosperms (seeds on cones), angiosperms (flowers and fruit).
- Alternation of generations: haploid gametophyte and diploid sporophyte; the sporophyte dominates in ferns and seed plants.
- Xylem moves water up by transpiration, cohesion and tension; phloem moves sugars from source to sink.
- Stomata trade water loss for carbon dioxide uptake; guard cells open and close them.
- Flowers: pollination, then fertilization; ovules become seeds, ovaries become fruits.
- Tropisms are growth responses; auxin causes phototropism.
