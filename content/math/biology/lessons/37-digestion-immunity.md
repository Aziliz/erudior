## Getting fuel in and keeping invaders out

The last lesson followed oxygen from the air to the cells. The other raw material of respiration, glucose, and the building blocks for growth come from food, and getting them out of food is the job of the digestive system. The same openings that let food and air in are also doors for bacteria and viruses, so the second half of this lesson covers the immune system, which defends the body against them. As before, this is biology, not medical advice.

## Digestion: from food to molecules

Food arrives as macromolecules (module 1) too large to cross the gut lining. Digestion breaks them into monomers by hydrolysis, each step catalyzed by a specific enzyme:

| Macromolecule | Broken down to | Key enzymes and where they act |
|---|---|---|
| Starch and other carbohydrates | Simple sugars (glucose) | Amylase (mouth, small intestine) |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Pepsin (stomach); trypsin and others (small intestine) |
| Fats | Fatty acids and glycerol (monoglycerides) | Lipase (small intestine) |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides | Nucleases (small intestine) |

Digestion is both mechanical (chewing and churning, which make smaller pieces with more surface for enzymes) and chemical (enzymes breaking bonds).

## The path through the gut

1. Mouth: teeth grind food; saliva moistens it and its amylase starts on starch (chew a cracker long enough and it tastes sweet).
2. Esophagus: food is pushed down by **peristalsis**, waves of muscle contraction that squeeze it along.
3. Stomach: muscular churning plus gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid (pH about 1.5–3.5) and pepsin, which begins protein digestion. The acid also kills many swallowed microbes. A mucus layer protects the stomach wall from its own acid and enzymes.
4. Small intestine: several meters long (about 3 m in a living adult; the often-quoted 6 m is its relaxed length after death) and the site of most digestion and nearly all absorption. The pancreas releases enzymes for every class of macromolecule plus bicarbonate to neutralize stomach acid. The liver makes bile, stored in the gallbladder, which emulsifies fats, breaking large droplets into tiny ones so lipase has more surface to work on. Bile contains no enzymes.
5. Large intestine (colon): absorbs water and salts and houses trillions of bacteria that ferment leftover fiber and make some vitamins, such as vitamin K.

The inner wall of the small intestine is folded, and the folds are covered with **villi**, finger-like projections about a millimeter tall, whose cells bear microscopic microvilli. Together these give the small intestine an absorbing surface of very roughly 30 m² (estimates vary with how it's measured). Each villus holds capillaries that absorb sugars and amino acids and a lymph vessel that absorbs fats.

### Example 1: what digestion is chemical?

In the 1770s, Lazzaro Spallanzani tested whether the stomach digests food by grinding or by chemistry. He swallowed small linen bags and perforated wooden tubes holding pieces of meat, recovered them later, and found the meat partly dissolved even though the containers protected it from grinding. He also collected gastric juice and showed that meat placed in it, in a vial kept at body temperature, slowly dissolved, while meat in plain water did not. What does each result show, and what was the control?

- Meat dissolved inside protective containers: grinding isn't required, so something chemical is acting.
- Meat dissolved in gastric juice outside the body: the active agent is in the juice itself, not in the living stomach wall.
- The control: meat in water at the same temperature. Without it, you couldn't rule out that warmth and time alone break meat down.

The active agent was later identified as pepsin, working with stomach acid.

## Nutrition and energy

The body needs carbohydrates, fats and proteins (macronutrients), plus vitamins, minerals, water and fiber. Energy in food is measured in kilocalories (the "Calories" on a food label are kilocalories) or kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ). The standard values are:

| Nutrient | Energy |
|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g) |
| Protein | 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g) |
| Fat | 9 kcal/g (37 kJ/g) |

Fat is the most energy-dense because its molecules are mostly carbon-hydrogen bonds, with little oxygen; they are less oxidized to start with, so more energy is released when they are fully oxidized in respiration.

### Example 2: reading a label

A granola bar contains 25 g carbohydrate, 4 g protein and 7 g fat. Estimate its energy and the percentage of that energy from fat.

- Carbohydrate: $25 \times 4 = 100$ kcal. Protein: $4 \times 4 = 16$ kcal. Fat: $7 \times 9 = 63$ kcal.
- Total: $100 + 16 + 63 = 179$ kcal.
- From fat: $63 / 179 \times 100 \approx 35.2\%$.

The 7 g of fat is only about 19% of the bar's macronutrient mass but over a third of its energy.

## The immune system, layer by layer

Pathogens (lesson 31) include bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists and parasitic worms. The body defends itself in two broad layers.

### Innate immunity

**Innate immunity** is the defense you're born with. It responds within minutes to hours and in the same way to any invader, recognizing general features shared by many microbes rather than one particular germ.

- Barriers: skin, mucus that traps particles (swept up by cilia in the airways), stomach acid, enzymes in tears and saliva.
- Inflammation: damaged tissue releases chemical signals such as histamine; nearby capillaries widen and leak, causing redness, heat, swelling and pain, and bringing in fluid and white blood cells.
- Phagocytes: white blood cells such as neutrophils and macrophages engulf and digest microbes.
- Natural killer cells destroy virus-infected and cancerous cells; fever raises body temperature in a way that slows some pathogens and speeds immune responses.

### Adaptive immunity

**Adaptive immunity** is specific: it recognizes one particular pathogen, takes days to build up the first time, and remembers. It depends on recognizing an **antigen**, any molecule (usually a protein or sugar on a pathogen's surface) that the immune system can identify as foreign. Its cells are lymphocytes, of two kinds:

- **B cells** make **antibodies**, Y-shaped proteins whose two tips each bind one specific antigen. Antibodies don't kill directly: they neutralize viruses and toxins, clump microbes together, and mark them for phagocytes.
- **T cells** come in two main types. Helper T cells coordinate the response, activating B cells and other cells. Cytotoxic (killer) T cells find body cells infected by viruses and destroy them, removing the virus's factories.

Each lymphocyte carries receptors for just one antigen, and the body makes millions of different lymphocytes before ever meeting an infection. When an antigen appears, only the few cells that match it are activated, and they multiply into a large clone of identical cells, a process called clonal selection. That multiplication is why the first response takes about one to two weeks. Afterwards, most of the clone dies, but long-lived **memory cells** remain. On a second exposure they respond within days, faster and much more strongly, often clearing the pathogen before you feel sick. That's immunity.

HIV shows how central helper T cells are: the virus infects and destroys them. Without treatment, their numbers fall over years until the adaptive response fails (AIDS), leaving the person open to infections a healthy immune system would control. Antiretroviral therapy does not cure HIV, but it keeps the virus from destroying helper T cells, and people who take it can live long, healthy lives.

### Example 3: reading the response curves

Using the graph, compare the two responses in timing and size, and explain the difference.

After the first exposure on day 0, antibodies stay low for several days, then peak around day 12 at a level of about 10. After the second exposure on day 30, the level climbs within a few days and peaks around day 35 at about 100. The secondary response is faster (about 5 days to peak instead of 12) and roughly ten times larger, because memory cells made in the first response are already waiting and multiply quickly.

## Vaccines

A **vaccine** exposes the immune system to an antigen from a pathogen without causing the disease, so the body builds memory cells in advance. In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that inoculating a boy with material from cowpox protected him against smallpox. Vaccination later eradicated smallpox worldwide; the World Health Organization declared it gone in 1980.

Vaccines present the antigen in different ways: weakened live pathogens (measles, mumps and rubella), inactivated pathogens (most flu shots), purified proteins or pieces (hepatitis B, HPV), inactivated toxins (tetanus), and mRNA vaccines (several COVID-19 vaccines), which deliver instructions for cells to make one viral protein for a short time. The mRNA never enters the nucleus, can't change a person's DNA, and is broken down within days.

When enough people are immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, which also protects people too young or too ill to be vaccinated. This is **herd immunity**. If each infected person would infect $R_0$ others in a fully susceptible population, spread stops when the immune fraction exceeds

$$1 - \frac{1}{R_0}.$$

### Example 4: herd immunity threshold

Measles is among the most contagious diseases, with $R_0$ around 12–18. Using $R_0 = 15$, what fraction of the population must be immune, and what vaccination coverage does that need if two doses of vaccine protect about 97% of people?

- Threshold: $1 - \tfrac{1}{15} = \tfrac{14}{15} \approx 0.933$, so about 93% must be immune.
- Coverage needed: $0.933 / 0.97 \approx 0.962$, about 96% of the population vaccinated.

For a disease with $R_0 = 2$, the threshold is only 50%. That's why measles outbreaks start when coverage slips just a few percentage points.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking digestion and absorption both happen mainly in the stomach. The small intestine does most of both.
- Saying bile is an enzyme. It emulsifies fats; lipase digests them.
- Mixing up antigens and antibodies. The antigen is the foreign marker; the antibody is the protein made to bind it.
- Believing antibiotics help against viral infections. They act only on bacteria.
- Thinking vaccines work by treating an infection. They build memory before infection.

## Key points

- Digestion hydrolyzes macromolecules: carbohydrates → sugars, proteins → amino acids, fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
- Mouth → esophagus (peristalsis) → stomach (acid, pepsin) → small intestine (pancreatic enzymes, bile, villi for absorption) → large intestine (water, gut bacteria).
- Energy: 4 kcal/g carbohydrate and protein, 9 kcal/g fat.
- Innate immunity is fast and general (barriers, inflammation, phagocytes); adaptive immunity is specific (B cells and antibodies, T cells) and builds memory.
- Vaccines create memory cells without disease; herd immunity threshold $= 1 - 1/R_0$.
