## What this course is about

Biology is the science of living things: how they are built, how they get and use energy, how they grow and reproduce, how they pass traits to their offspring, how they interact with each other and their surroundings, and how they have changed over Earth's long history. This first lesson answers two questions every later lesson depends on. What makes something alive? And how is life organized, from molecules up to the whole planet?

Neither question is as easy as it sounds. A flame grows, "feeds" on fuel, gives off heat and can spread to new places, yet no biologist calls fire alive. A dormant seed can sit in a drawer for decades doing almost nothing, yet it is alive. Working out why gives us a usable definition.

## The characteristics of life

No single property separates living from nonliving things. Instead, biologists use a set of characteristics that living things share. A living thing shows all of them, at least at some stage of its life or across its lineage.

1. *Made of cells.* Every living thing is built from one or more cells, the smallest units that can carry out all the processes of life. A bacterium is one cell; you are tens of trillions of them. (Module 2 is all about cells.)
2. *Uses energy: metabolism.* Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism: breaking down food to release energy, and using that energy to build the molecules it needs. A plant captures light energy; you get energy from food. Either way, the organism is constantly taking in matter and energy and transforming them.
3. *Maintains a stable internal state.* **Homeostasis** is keeping internal conditions (temperature, water content, pH, sugar levels) within the narrow range the cells need, even when the outside changes. You sweat when you are hot and shiver when you are cold; a single-celled organism pumps out excess water that leaks in.
4. *Responds to stimuli.* A stimulus is any change in the environment an organism can detect. Plants bend toward light; bacteria swim toward food; your hand jerks away from a hot stove.
5. *Grows and develops.* Living things increase in size and change form according to instructions they carry, as a fertilized egg becomes a frog.
6. *Reproduces.* Organisms make more organisms, either sexually (two parents) or asexually (one parent).
7. *Has heritable genetic information.* Instructions stored in DNA are passed from parent to offspring and direct how the organism is built and run.
8. *Populations evolve.* Over many generations, the inherited traits of a population change, and populations become adapted to their environments. This happens to groups, not to an individual during its own lifetime.

Some characteristics apply to individuals and some to groups. A mule (the offspring of a horse and a donkey) is almost always sterile, but it is obviously alive: it has cells, a metabolism and homeostasis, and it came from reproduction. Reproduction and evolution are properties of the lineage, not requirements every individual must meet.

### Example 1: Is it alive?

Apply the checklist to a flame, a dormant bean seed and a crystal of salt growing in a saturated solution.

- *Flame.* It "grows" and gives off energy, but it has no cells, no DNA, no homeostasis (it cannot keep its temperature steady against the surroundings) and no inheritance: a new fire is not built from instructions copied from the old one. Not alive.
- *Bean seed.* It looks inert, but inside is an embryo made of cells, with DNA and a very slow metabolism (it still uses a little oxygen). Given water and warmth it grows, develops and eventually reproduces. Alive, though dormant.
- *Salt crystal.* It grows by adding ions to its surface, but growth alone is not enough. It has no cells, no metabolism and no genetic information. Not alive.

One characteristic is never enough; living things show the whole set.

## Detecting life by its chemistry

Because metabolism is so central, it is what scientists look for when they hunt for life. When the two Viking landers reached Mars in 1976, one experiment (the "labeled release" test) dripped nutrient broth containing radioactive carbon onto soil samples. The idea: if microbes were present, they would metabolize the nutrients and release radioactive carbon dioxide gas. Gas did come off. But a control sample, heated first to kill anything living, gave almost none, and other instruments found no organic molecules in the soil. Most scientists concluded that reactive chemicals in the soil, not organisms, released the gas. The episode shows two things you will see again: a single sign of life is not proof, and a well-chosen control is what lets you interpret a result.

## Levels of organization

Life is organized in a hierarchy, each level built from the one below.

| Level | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Atom | Smallest unit of an element | A carbon atom |
| Molecule | Atoms bonded together | A protein, DNA, water |
| Organelle | A structure inside a cell with a job | A mitochondrion |
| Cell | Smallest unit that is alive | A muscle cell |
| Tissue | Similar cells working together | Heart muscle tissue |
| Organ | Different tissues forming one structure with a job | The heart |
| Organ system | Organs working together | The circulatory system |
| Organism | One complete living individual | A person |
| Population | All the members of one species in one area | The deer in a forest |
| Community | All the populations of different species in an area | Deer, oaks, wolves, fungi together |
| Ecosystem | A community plus its nonliving surroundings | The forest with its soil, water and climate |
| Biosphere | All the ecosystems on Earth | The whole living planet |

Unicellular organisms such as bacteria skip the tissue-to-organ-system levels: the cell is the organism. Plants have the same levels as animals, with their own tissues (such as the xylem that carries water) and organs (roots, stems, leaves).

Two of these levels are worth separating carefully. A population is one species; a community is every species. And an ecosystem adds the nonliving (abiotic) parts, such as sunlight, water and minerals, to the living (biotic) community.

### Example 2: Ordering the levels

Put these in order from smallest to largest: a pancreas, an insulin molecule, all the people in a city, a pancreatic cell, the digestive system, one person.

Work up the table, asking "what is this made of?" at each step:

1. Insulin molecule (a protein: a molecule).
2. Pancreatic cell (makes the insulin: a cell).
3. Pancreas (made of several tissues: an organ).
4. Digestive system (pancreas, stomach, intestines and more: an organ system). The pancreas also belongs to the endocrine system; organs can serve more than one system.
5. One person (an organism).
6. All the people in a city (one species in one area: a population).

The tissue level was skipped because none of the items is a tissue; that is fine, the order still holds.

## Emergent properties

At each level, new abilities appear that the parts on their own do not have. A single heart muscle cell can contract, but only a whole heart, with tissues arranged into chambers and valves, can pump blood. A water molecule is not wet; wetness is a property of many water molecules together. These **emergent properties** arise from how the parts are arranged and interact, not from any one part.

That is why biologists study every level: knowing every molecule in a cell does not tell you how the cell behaves.

## Homeostasis in action

Homeostasis usually works by negative feedback: a change triggers a response that pushes the condition back toward its set point. (Module 9 studies feedback in detail; here you only need the idea.)

### Example 3: Reading homeostasis data

A student records her body temperature and her skin's sweat rate during a 40-minute run on a hot day.

| Time (min) | Body temperature (°C) | Sweat rate (relative) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 37.0 | 1 |
| 10 | 37.6 | 4 |
| 20 | 37.9 | 7 |
| 30 | 37.9 | 7 |
| 40 | 37.8 | 7 |

What does the table show?

1. Running releases heat from muscle metabolism, so temperature rises at first (37.0 to 37.6 °C).
2. Sweating increases as the temperature rises. Sweat evaporating from skin carries heat away.
3. After 20 minutes the temperature stops rising and levels off near 37.9 °C even though she keeps running. The rise in heat production is now balanced by the increased heat loss.

The temperature changed by less than 1 °C during heavy exercise. That small change, held in check by a response that grows with the disturbance, is the signature of homeostasis by negative feedback. If sweating failed (for example, from dehydration), the temperature would keep climbing, which is how heat stroke happens.

## Is a virus alive?

Viruses are the classic hard case. They carry genetic information and evolve, but they are not made of cells, have no metabolism of their own and reproduce only by hijacking a living cell. Most biologists therefore do not classify them as living organisms, while recognizing that they sit near the boundary (Lesson 7).

## Common mistakes

- *Using one characteristic as the definition.* "It grows, so it's alive" makes crystals and fires alive. Check the whole set.
- *Thinking every individual must reproduce.* Sterile organisms (mules, worker bees) are alive; reproduction is a property of the species.
- *Saying individuals evolve.* An individual's inherited traits do not change during its life. Populations evolve across generations.
- *Mixing up population and community.* Population: one species. Community: all species in the area.
- *Forgetting the nonliving parts of an ecosystem.* Sunlight, water, soil and temperature are part of an ecosystem, not of a community.

## Key points

- Living things are made of cells, carry out metabolism, maintain **homeostasis**, respond to stimuli, grow and develop, reproduce, carry heritable DNA, and evolve as populations.
- No single characteristic defines life; nonliving things can show one or two.
- Levels of organization: atom → molecule → organelle → cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.
- New **emergent properties** appear at each level, so every level needs its own study.
- Homeostasis usually works by negative feedback, which returns a condition toward its set point.
- Viruses have genes and evolve but have no cells or metabolism of their own, so most biologists do not consider them alive.
