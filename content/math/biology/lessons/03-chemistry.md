## Why a biology course needs chemistry

The first lesson described life as a hierarchy that starts with atoms and molecules, and the second showed how biologists test ideas. Now we start at the bottom of the hierarchy. Every process in this course, from digestion to DNA copying, is chemistry happening inside cells, and most of that chemistry happens in water. This lesson covers the few chemical ideas biology leans on: what atoms and bonds are, why water behaves so unusually, and how acidity is measured with the pH scale.

## Atoms and the elements of life

All matter is made of atoms. An atom has a dense nucleus of protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge), surrounded by electrons (negative charge). An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom, defined by its number of protons: every carbon atom has 6, every oxygen atom 8.

About 25 elements are essential to life, but six make up roughly 98% of the mass of living things. A handy memory aid is **CHNOPS**: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur. Carbon is the backbone of biological molecules, nitrogen appears in proteins and DNA, phosphorus in DNA and in the energy carrier ATP, and sulfur in some amino acids. Smaller amounts of other elements matter too: calcium in bones, iron in the hemoglobin that carries oxygen in your blood, sodium and potassium in nerve signals.

## Chemical bonds

Atoms join into molecules by bonding, which depends on their outer electrons.

- In a covalent bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons. Covalent bonds are strong and hold the atoms of a biological molecule together: the C–H bonds of sugar, the O–H bonds in water.
- In an ionic bond, one atom transfers electrons to another, producing ions (charged atoms) that attract each other. Table salt, NaCl, is Na⁺ and Cl⁻ held by ionic attraction.

Electrons are not always shared equally. Oxygen pulls on shared electrons much harder than hydrogen does. In a water molecule the electrons spend more time near the oxygen, giving it a slight negative charge (written δ−) and leaving each hydrogen slightly positive (δ+). A molecule with an uneven distribution of charge like this is a **polar molecule**. Water is also bent, not straight, so its two positive ends sit on one side and the negative end on the other.

Opposite charges attract, so the slightly positive hydrogen of one water molecule is drawn to the slightly negative oxygen of a neighbor. This attraction between a hydrogen on one polar molecule and an electronegative atom (oxygen or nitrogen) on another is a **hydrogen bond**. A hydrogen bond is much weaker than a covalent bond, roughly a twentieth as strong, and hydrogen bonds constantly break and re-form in liquid water. But there are enormous numbers of them, and together they explain almost everything special about water. They also hold the two strands of DNA together and help proteins keep their shapes, as you will see in the next two lessons.

## The properties of water

*Cohesion and adhesion.* **Cohesion** is the attraction of water molecules to each other, through hydrogen bonds. It gives water high surface tension, which is why a water strider can stand on a pond. Adhesion is the attraction of water to other substances, such as the walls of a glass tube. Together they produce capillary action, water creeping up a narrow tube. In plants, cohesion holds a continuous column of water together all the way up the thin xylem tubes from root to leaf, so that evaporation from the leaves pulls the whole column upward.

*High specific heat.* **Specific heat** is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C. For water it is about 4.18 J/(g·°C), one of the highest of any common substance; dry sand is about 0.8 J/(g·°C). Heating water means first breaking many hydrogen bonds, which soaks up energy. The result: water changes temperature slowly. Oceans and lakes keep coastal climates mild, and the water that makes up most of your body buffers you against sudden temperature swings. Water also absorbs a lot of energy when it evaporates, which is why sweating cools you.

*Ice floats.* Most substances are denser as solids. When water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open lattice that takes up about 9% more space than the same mass of liquid water, so ice is about 8% less dense and floats. A frozen pond freezes from the top down, and the ice layer insulates the liquid water beneath, letting fish survive the winter.

*Water is an excellent solvent.* In a solution, the substance that does the dissolving is the solvent and the dissolved substance is the solute. Because water is polar, it surrounds ions and other polar molecules and pulls them apart: the δ− oxygens face positive ions and the δ+ hydrogens face negative ions. Sugars, salts, amino acids and many other molecules of life dissolve in water, which is why blood, sap and the inside of cells are all water-based solutions. Nonpolar substances, such as oils and fats, do not dissolve in water; they are hydrophobic ("water-fearing"), while polar and charged substances are hydrophilic.

### Example 1: Using specific heat

How much energy does it take to warm 250 g of water from 20 °C to 30 °C? How much for 250 g of dry sand?

Energy = mass × specific heat × temperature change.

- Water: $250 \times 4.18 \times 10 = 10\,450$ J, about 10.5 kJ.
- Sand: $250 \times 0.8 \times 10 = 2000$ J, about 2 kJ.

The same energy that warms sand by 10 °C would warm the same mass of water by only about 2 °C. That is why a beach's sand is scorching by afternoon while the sea stays cool, and why large bodies of water steady the temperature of the land around them.

## Acids, bases and pH

A small fraction of water molecules split into a hydrogen ion (H⁺) and a hydroxide ion (OH⁻). In pure water these are equal. An acid is a substance that releases H⁺ into a solution, raising the H⁺ concentration; a base lowers it (by releasing OH⁻, which combines with H⁺ to make water, or by taking up H⁺ directly).

**pH** is a measure of the hydrogen ion concentration on a scale usually running from 0 to 14:

$$\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+], \qquad [\text{H}^+] = 10^{-\text{pH}} \text{ mol/L}$$

- pH 7 is neutral (pure water at 25 °C).
- Below 7 is acidic; the lower the pH, the more H⁺.
- Above 7 is basic (alkaline).

The scale is logarithmic: each step of 1 pH unit is a *tenfold* change in H⁺ concentration. A solution at pH 4 has 10 times the H⁺ of one at pH 5 and 100 times that at pH 6.

### Example 2: Comparing pH values

Stomach fluid has a pH of about 2; the small intestine, where the stomach's contents go next, is near pH 8. How many times greater is the H⁺ concentration in the stomach?

The pH differs by $8 - 2 = 6$ units. Each unit is a factor of 10, so the stomach has $10^6 = 1\,000\,000$ times the H⁺ concentration. Equivalently, $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-2}$ mol/L in the stomach and $10^{-8}$ mol/L in the intestine, and $10^{-2} / 10^{-8} = 10^{6}$.

A frequent wrong answer is 6 times (treating the scale as linear) or 4 times (dividing 8 by 2). Neither respects the logarithm.

## Buffers keep pH steady

Living cells tolerate only a narrow range of pH, because pH changes the charges on proteins and with them the proteins' shapes (Lesson 5 shows what that does to enzymes). Human blood is held between about 7.35 and 7.45. Body fluids stay so steady because of a **buffer**, a pair of chemicals that takes up excess H⁺ when acid is added and releases H⁺ when base is added. In blood, the main buffer is the carbonic acid / bicarbonate pair: bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) soaks up extra H⁺, and carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) gives H⁺ back when the blood becomes too basic. Buffering is a chemical part of homeostasis.

### Example 3: Reading buffer data

A student adds drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to 50 mL of pure water and to 50 mL of a buffer solution, measuring pH after each 5 drops.

| Drops of acid | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pure water (pH) | 7.0 | 3.2 | 2.9 | 2.7 | 2.6 |
| Buffer (pH) | 7.0 | 6.9 | 6.9 | 6.8 | 6.7 |

What do the data show?

1. In pure water, the first 5 drops dropped the pH by almost 4 units: a rise in H⁺ of several thousandfold ($10^{3.8} \approx 6000$). Water alone has nothing to absorb the added H⁺.
2. In the buffer, 20 drops lowered the pH by only 0.3 units. The buffer took up almost all the added H⁺.
3. Conclusion: the buffer resists change in pH. A buffer has a limited capacity, though; if acid kept being added, the buffer would eventually be used up and its pH would fall quickly too.

This is a controlled comparison: same volume, same acid, same drops. The only difference between the two groups is whether a buffer is present.

## Common mistakes

- *Treating pH as linear.* A change of 3 pH units is a $10^3 = 1000$-fold change in H⁺, not 3-fold.
- *Getting the direction backward.* Lower pH means more H⁺, which is more acidic.
- *Confusing hydrogen bonds with covalent bonds.* Covalent bonds hold the atoms within a water molecule together; hydrogen bonds are weaker attractions between separate molecules.
- *Mixing up cohesion and adhesion.* Cohesion is water to water; adhesion is water to something else.
- *Thinking a buffer keeps pH exactly constant.* It reduces the change, and only until its capacity runs out.

## Key points

- Six elements (CHNOPS) make up about 98% of living matter; carbon is the backbone of biological molecules.
- Water is a **polar molecule**; **hydrogen bonds** between water molecules cause its **cohesion**, high **specific heat**, floating ice and power as a solvent.
- **pH** $= -\log[\text{H}^+]$. Below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic; each unit is a tenfold change.
- A **buffer** resists changes in pH; buffers in blood keep it near 7.4.
