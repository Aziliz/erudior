## A cell as a factory

The last lesson split cells into two plans and showed that eukaryotic cells are divided into membrane-bound compartments. This lesson opens those compartments. A useful picture is a factory with a head office, assembly lines, shipping, power plants and recycling. Each organelle has a job, and the jobs connect. As you read, keep asking two questions: what does this part do, and how does its structure suit that job?

## The nucleus: the instructions

The **nucleus** is the large organelle that holds the cell's DNA and controls its activities by deciding which genes are used. It is surrounded by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane pierced by thousands of nuclear pores. The pores let messenger RNA, copied from DNA, leave for the cytoplasm and let proteins needed inside enter. Most of the DNA is spread out as chromatin (DNA wound around proteins); it condenses into visible chromosomes only when the cell divides. A dense region called the nucleolus assembles the parts of ribosomes.

## Making proteins: ribosomes and the ER

Ribosomes, whose two subunits are assembled in the nucleolus, build proteins. Some float free in the cytoplasm and make proteins that stay there. Others are attached to the endoplasmic reticulum.

The **endoplasmic reticulum** (ER) is a network of folded membranes and tubes continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope.

- *Rough ER* is studded with ribosomes, which give it its bumpy look. Proteins made here are threaded into the ER as they are built; they are destined to be secreted from the cell, placed in the cell's membranes, or sent to lysosomes. Inside the ER they fold and often have sugar chains added.
- *Smooth ER* has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones; in liver cells it detoxifies drugs and alcohol; in muscle cells it stores calcium ions that trigger contraction.

## Packaging and shipping: the Golgi and vesicles

Small membrane sacs called vesicles bud off the ER, carrying new proteins to the **Golgi apparatus**, a stack of flattened membrane sacs. The Golgi modifies proteins further (for example, trimming and adding sugars), sorts them by destination, and packages them into new vesicles. Some vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell (this is how cells secrete hormones, enzymes and mucus). Others deliver membrane proteins to the surface or carry digestive enzymes to lysosomes.

Together, the nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, vesicles, lysosomes and plasma membrane form the endomembrane system: a set of membranes connected by moving vesicles.

### Example 1: Tracing a secreted protein (Palade's pulse–chase)

How do we know proteins travel ER → Golgi → vesicles? In the 1960s George Palade and his colleagues studied pancreas cells, which secrete large amounts of digestive enzymes. They gave the cells a 3-minute "pulse" of a radioactive amino acid, so only proteins made during those 3 minutes were labeled, then a "chase" of ordinary amino acids. At intervals they found where the radioactivity was, using electron micrographs exposed to photographic film. Simplified, representative results:

| Time after pulse | % of label in rough ER | % in Golgi | % in secretory vesicles |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 min | 85 | 10 | 5 |
| 20 min | 40 | 45 | 15 |
| 120 min | 10 | 15 | 75 |

Reading the table step by step:

1. At 3 minutes, most label is in the rough ER: proteins are made by the ribosomes on the rough ER.
2. By 20 minutes, the label has shifted largely to the Golgi, and the rough ER's share has fallen.
3. By 120 minutes, most label is in secretory vesicles near the cell surface, ready to be released.

Because the same labeled batch of protein moves from one compartment to the next over time, the data show the path: rough ER → Golgi → secretory vesicles → outside the cell. Palade shared the 1974 Nobel Prize, with Albert Claude and Christian de Duve, for discoveries about the cell's structure and organization, including this work.

## Recycling and storage: lysosomes and vacuoles

A **lysosome** is a membrane sac filled with digestive enzymes. Its interior is acidic (about pH 5), the optimum for those enzymes (recall Lesson 5). Lysosomes break down food particles brought into the cell, bacteria engulfed by white blood cells, and worn-out organelles, recycling their molecules. The acidity is a safety feature: if a lysosome leaks, its enzymes work poorly at the near-neutral pH of the cytoplasm. Lysosomes are prominent in animal cells.

Vacuoles are membrane sacs for storage. Plant cells usually have one large central vacuole that can take up most of the cell's volume. It stores water, ions and pigments, and also does much of the breaking down that lysosomes do in animal cells. When it is full of water it presses outward against the cell wall, keeping the plant firm; a wilted plant's vacuoles have lost water.

## Power plants: mitochondria and chloroplasts

A **mitochondrion** (plural mitochondria) is the organelle where cellular respiration releases energy from food molecules and stores it in ATP, the cell's energy currency. It has two membranes; the inner one is folded into ridges called cristae, which give it a large surface area for the enzymes of respiration. Cells that use a lot of energy, such as muscle and sperm cells, have many mitochondria. Plant cells have mitochondria too.

A **chloroplast** is the organelle in plants and algae where photosynthesis captures light energy and uses it to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. It is also wrapped in two membranes and, inside, stacks of membrane discs containing the green pigment chlorophyll. Module 3 explains what happens in both organelles.

## Shape and support: cytoskeleton and cell wall

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibers running through the cytoplasm. *Microtubules*, hollow tubes, act as tracks for motor proteins that carry vesicles and organelles, and they pull chromosomes apart during cell division. *Microfilaments*, thinner fibers of the protein actin, let cells change shape and crawl and, with myosin, contract muscle. *Intermediate filaments* give mechanical strength. Cilia and flagella in eukaryotic cells are built from microtubules. Animal cells also have a pair of centrioles, made of microtubules, near the nucleus.

Plants, fungi and many protists have a **cell wall** outside the plasma membrane: a rigid layer that supports the cell and keeps it from bursting when water enters. Plant walls are made mainly of cellulose; fungal walls of chitin.

| Structure | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall (cellulose) | Yes | No |
| Chloroplasts | Yes (in green tissues) | No |
| Large central vacuole | Yes | No (small vacuoles only) |
| Mitochondria | Yes | Yes |
| Centrioles | No (in most plants) | Yes |
| Lysosomes | Rare | Common |

### Example 2: Structure fits function

Predict which organelle is especially abundant in each cell, and why.

1. *A pancreas cell that secretes digestive enzymes.* Enzymes are proteins made for export, so it has extensive rough ER and a large Golgi apparatus.
2. *A heart muscle cell that contracts nonstop.* Constant work needs constant ATP, so it is packed with mitochondria.
3. *A liver cell that breaks down drugs and alcohol.* Detoxification happens in the smooth ER, so it has abundant smooth ER.
4. *A white blood cell that engulfs bacteria.* It must digest what it eats, so it has many lysosomes.
5. *A leaf cell on a sunny upper surface.* It photosynthesizes, so it is full of chloroplasts.

This kind of reasoning, from a cell's job to its parts, is one of the central skills in biology.

## Where did mitochondria and chloroplasts come from?

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are strange organelles. They have their own DNA and their own ribosomes, and they make some of their own proteins. The **endosymbiotic theory** explains why: mitochondria and chloroplasts are descended from free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by an ancestral host cell, more than 1.5 billion years ago, and lived on inside it ("endo-" means within, "symbiosis" means living together). The engulfed bacteria got shelter and nutrients; the host got efficient energy from oxygen-using respiration and, in the lineage that later took in chloroplasts, sugar from photosynthesis. Over time most of their genes moved to the host's nucleus, and they could no longer live on their own. Lynn Margulis championed the idea in 1967 against strong skepticism. The evidence is now overwhelming:

- They have *two membranes*, as expected if a host membrane wrapped around an engulfed cell.
- They have their own *circular DNA*, like bacterial chromosomes.
- Their *ribosomes resemble bacterial ribosomes* in size and structure, and some antibiotics that block bacterial ribosomes also block them.
- They *divide by splitting in two*, like bacteria, and are never made from scratch by the cell.
- Their *DNA sequences* are most similar to particular bacteria: mitochondria to a group of oxygen-using bacteria (alphaproteobacteria), chloroplasts to photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

### Example 3: Weighing the evidence

A student argues: "If mitochondria were once bacteria, they should be able to grow on their own in a dish. They can't, so the theory is wrong." How would a biologist respond?

1. The theory says mitochondria *descended* from bacteria over more than a billion years, not that they still are independent bacteria.
2. During that time, most mitochondrial genes were transferred to the nucleus; a human mitochondrion keeps only 37 genes. It depends on proteins made from nuclear genes and imported, so it cannot survive alone.
3. The theory is supported by several independent lines of evidence (membranes, DNA, ribosomes, division, sequence similarity), which all point the same way.

The observation does not refute the theory; it fits the predicted history of gene loss.

## Common mistakes

- *Saying plant cells have no mitochondria.* Plants have both chloroplasts and mitochondria; chloroplasts make sugar, mitochondria release its energy.
- *Mixing up rough and smooth ER.* Rough ER (with ribosomes) makes proteins for export and membranes; smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies.
- *Calling the Golgi the protein maker.* Ribosomes make proteins; the Golgi modifies, sorts and ships them.
- *Saying the cell wall replaces the membrane.* Plant cells have both; the wall is outside the membrane.
- *Thinking endosymbiosis means organelles are still bacteria.* They descended from bacteria and now depend on the host.

## Key points

- The **nucleus** holds DNA; nuclear pores let mRNA out; the nucleolus makes ribosome parts.
- Rough **endoplasmic reticulum** makes proteins for export and membranes; smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies.
- The **Golgi apparatus** modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles. Path of a secreted protein: rough ER → Golgi → vesicle → outside (Palade's pulse–chase).
- A **lysosome** digests and recycles; plant central vacuoles store water and keep cells firm.
- A **mitochondrion** releases energy as ATP; a **chloroplast** captures light for photosynthesis.
- The cytoskeleton gives shape and moves things; a **cell wall** supports plant and fungal cells.
- The **endosymbiotic theory**: mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from engulfed bacteria (evidence: double membranes, circular DNA, bacteria-like ribosomes, division by splitting, DNA sequences).
