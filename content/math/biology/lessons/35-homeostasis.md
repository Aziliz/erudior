## Keeping the inside steady

The last module ended with the animal kingdom; this one looks closely at one animal, the human, at the level a biology course expects (it is not medical advice). Your body is a community of tens of trillions of cells, and every one of them needs the same narrow conditions: the right temperature, a steady supply of glucose and oxygen, the right water and salt balance, the right pH. Outside, conditions swing wildly. Homeostasis, the maintenance of a stable internal environment, is what keeps those conditions steady, and every organ system in this module contributes to it. This lesson covers the logic of control and the two systems that carry it out: nerves and hormones.

## Feedback loops

Each regulated variable has a **set point**, a target value, with a normal range around it. Core body temperature sits near 37 °C; fasting blood glucose stays roughly between 70 and 100 mg/dL. Every control system that holds a set point has the same three parts:

- a sensor (receptor) that detects the current value;
- a control center, often in the brain, that compares the value to the set point;
- an effector, a muscle or gland, that changes the value.

In **negative feedback**, the response opposes the change: if the value rises, the response pushes it down; if it falls, the response pushes it up. A home thermostat works this way. Almost all homeostasis is negative feedback, because it is self-correcting.

### Example 1: tracing a temperature loop

A runner's core temperature rises to 38.5 °C on a hot day. Trace the negative feedback loop.

1. Stimulus: core temperature rises above the set point.
2. Sensors: temperature-sensitive neurons in the skin and in the hypothalamus, a region at the base of the brain, detect the rise.
3. Control center: the hypothalamus compares the reading to about 37 °C and sends signals through nerves.
4. Effectors: sweat glands release sweat, whose evaporation removes heat; blood vessels in the skin widen (vasodilation), carrying warm blood to the surface to lose heat (that's why the runner's face flushes).
5. Result: temperature falls toward 37 °C, and as it does, the signals weaken. The response shuts itself off.

In the cold, the same center does the opposite: skin vessels narrow (vasoconstriction) to keep heat in the core, and skeletal muscles shiver, releasing heat.

In **positive feedback**, the response amplifies the change, pushing the variable further from where it started until some event ends the loop. During childbirth, the baby's head stretches the cervix; stretch receptors signal the brain to release the hormone oxytocin, which strengthens contractions, which stretch the cervix more, and so on until the birth. Blood clotting is another example: activated platelets release chemicals that activate more platelets. Positive feedback is useful for events that must be completed quickly, never for holding a value steady.

## The nervous system: fast, targeted signals

The nervous system has two parts. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system is the nerves connecting the CNS to the rest of the body. Its working cells are **neurons**, cells specialized to carry electrical signals.

A typical neuron has:

- dendrites, short branches that receive signals from other cells;
- a cell body containing the nucleus;
- an axon, a single long extension that carries the signal away, in some cases more than a meter long;
- axon terminals that pass the signal to the next cell.

Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath made by supporting cells, with small gaps between segments.

### The action potential

A resting neuron keeps its inside about 70 millivolts negative relative to the outside, built up by sodium-potassium pumps that spend ATP moving sodium ions out and potassium ions in. An **action potential** is a brief reversal of that voltage: when the membrane is stimulated past a threshold, sodium channels open and sodium ions rush in, making the inside positive; a millisecond later, potassium channels open and potassium ions flow out, restoring the negative inside. That disturbance triggers the next patch of membrane, so the impulse travels along the axon like a falling row of dominoes.

Action potentials are all-or-none: a stimulus either reaches threshold and fires a full-size impulse or does nothing. A stronger stimulus doesn't make a bigger impulse; it makes impulses fire more often. Myelin speeds conduction dramatically, because the impulse jumps from gap to gap. Speeds range from under 1 m/s in thin unmyelinated fibers to over 100 m/s in thick myelinated ones. Much of what we know came from Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley's 1952 experiments on the squid giant axon, about half a millimeter wide and large enough to insert electrodes.

### Example 2: measuring nerve speed

In 1850 Hermann von Helmholtz measured the speed of a nerve impulse using a frog's leg muscle and its nerve. He stimulated the nerve at two points and timed when the muscle twitched. Suppose stimulating 50 mm from the muscle gives a twitch after 3.9 ms, and stimulating 20 mm from it gives a twitch after 2.7 ms. What is the conduction speed?

- The muscle's own delay is the same in both trials, so subtracting cancels it.
- Extra distance: $50 - 20 = 30$ mm. Extra time: $3.9 - 2.7 = 1.2$ ms.
- Speed $= 30 \text{ mm} / 1.2 \text{ ms} = 25$ mm/ms $= 25$ m/s (since 1 mm/ms = 1 m/s).

Before this, many scientists assumed nerve signals were nearly instantaneous. A speed slower than sound showed they were a physical process that could be studied.

### Synapses

Neurons don't touch the next cell. At a **synapse**, a narrow gap separates the axon terminal from the receiving cell. When an impulse arrives, the terminal releases a **neurotransmitter**, a chemical messenger, which diffuses across the gap in a fraction of a millisecond and binds receptor proteins on the next cell. Depending on the transmitter and receptor, it can make the receiving cell more likely or less likely to fire. Acetylcholine triggers muscle contraction; dopamine and serotonin act in the brain. The transmitter is then broken down or taken back up, so each signal is brief. Many drugs act at synapses.

Some responses don't wait for the brain. In a reflex, such as pulling a hand off a hot stove, a sensory neuron carries the signal to the spinal cord, which connects directly (sometimes through one interneuron) to a motor neuron that makes the muscle contract. The pain registers in the brain a moment after the hand has moved.

## The endocrine system: slower, broadcast signals

A **hormone** is a chemical messenger secreted by a gland into the blood, which carries it throughout the body. Only target cells with matching receptors respond. Endocrine glands include the pituitary (often called the master gland, controlled by the hypothalamus), thyroid (metabolic rate), adrenal glands (adrenaline for the fight-or-flight response; cortisol), pancreas, and the ovaries and testes (sex hormones).

The first hormone was found in 1902, when William Bayliss and Ernest Starling showed that the intestine still triggered the pancreas to release digestive juice after all the nerves between them were cut. Something in the blood had to carry the message. They extracted it, named it secretin, and coined the word "hormone."

| Feature | Nervous system | Endocrine system |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Electrical impulse, then neurotransmitter | Hormone in the blood |
| Speed | Milliseconds | Seconds to hours |
| Duration | Brief | Can last hours or longer |
| Reach | Specific cells the neuron connects to | Any cell with the receptor |

### Blood glucose

Blood glucose control is the textbook example of hormonal negative feedback, with two opposing hormones from clusters of cells in the pancreas called islets:

- After a meal, glucose rises. Beta cells release **insulin**, which makes liver, muscle and fat cells take up glucose and makes the liver store it as glycogen. Glucose falls.
- Between meals, glucose falls. Alpha cells release **glucagon**, which makes the liver break glycogen down and release glucose. Glucose rises.

In type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the beta cells, so the body makes little or no insulin; people with it take insulin. In type 2 diabetes, much more common, cells respond less to insulin (insulin resistance), and the pancreas eventually can't keep up. In both, blood glucose stays high, which over years damages blood vessels and nerves. Insulin was first isolated in 1921 by Frederick Banting and Charles Best in Toronto, and within a year it was saving the lives of children with type 1 diabetes.

Use the graph. For the typical response, find the peak and say what drives the return. Then compare the dashed curve.

The solid curve rises from 90 to a peak of about 140 mg/dL at 30 minutes, as glucose is absorbed. Rising glucose triggers insulin release; cells take up glucose, so the level falls back toward 90 by about two to three hours. The dashed curve starts higher (140, so fasting glucose is already above the normal range), peaks near 300 at about an hour, is still above 200 at three hours, and only gets down to about 170 by four hours. Little effective insulin action means glucose isn't cleared: the negative feedback loop is broken.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any helpful response "positive feedback." Positive feedback amplifies a change; negative feedback reverses it.
- Thinking insulin "is sugar" or raises blood sugar. Insulin lowers it; glucagon raises it.
- Believing a stronger stimulus makes a bigger action potential. It makes more frequent ones.
- Confusing hormones with neurotransmitters: hormones travel in blood; neurotransmitters cross a synapse.

## Key points

- Homeostasis holds variables near a set point using sensors, a control center and effectors.
- Negative feedback reverses changes (temperature, glucose); positive feedback amplifies them to finish an event (childbirth, clotting).
- Neurons carry all-or-none action potentials (sodium in, then potassium out); myelin speeds them; synapses pass signals chemically.
- Hormones travel in the blood to target cells with receptors: slower and longer-lasting than nerve signals.
- Insulin lowers blood glucose, glucagon raises it; diabetes is a failure of this loop.
